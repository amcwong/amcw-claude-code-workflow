Week 3: Initialization, Autodiff, Overfitting & Regularization

Week 3: Initialization, Autodiff, Overfitting & Regularization

Overview. Week 2 gave us the multi-layer perceptron and the backpropagation algorithm. This week asks what can go wrong when we actually train one, and what tools make training practical. We first restate backpropagation in Week 2’s notation and work one example end to end. That example shows why gradients can shrink or blow up as they travel backward, which motivates choosing the initial weights carefully. We then see how a program can apply the chain rule for us (automatic differentiation). Finally we turn from optimization to generalization: a model that fits its training data too well can do worse on new data, and two simple techniques, weight decay and dropout, push against that.

Backprop Revisited

Week 2 already derived backpropagation. Here we restate it and use it in a worked example, keeping Week 2’s letters for the two quantities every layer computes.

Warning

Notation. These notes write the pre-activation of layer m as a^m and the activation as h^m, the same letters as Week 2. The handwritten slides for this lecture use z^m for the pre-activation and a^m for the activation, so a reader comparing the two should translate z \to a and a \to h.

Object These notes (and Week 2) Handwritten slides
Pre-activation, W^m h^{m-1} + b^m a^m z^m
Activation, \sigma(\text{pre-activation}) h^m a^m

With this notation the forward pass is a^m = W^m h^{m-1} + b^m and h^m = \sigma^m(a^m), where h^0 is the input \bm{x} and L is the last layer. Backpropagation (Backpropagation) then has three steps.

\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a^L} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial h^L}\,\frac{\partial h^L}{\partial a^L}

This step starts at the output layer: the loss depends on a^L only through h^L.

\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a^m} &= \left({W^{m+1}}^\top \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a^{m+1}}\right) \odot \frac{\partial h^m}{\partial a^m}, \\ &\qquad m = L-1, \ldots, 1 \end{aligned}

Here \odot is the Hadamard product, entry-by-entry multiplication. The gradient at layer m is built in two moves. First we multiply the gradient at layer m+1 by {W^{m+1}}^\top, the transpose of the weight matrix that carried layer m into layer m+1. Entry k of the result is \sum_\ell W^{m+1}_{\ell k}\,\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial a^{m+1}_\ell, so each unit in layer m collects the gradients of the units it fed, each weighted by the connection weight it used. We say the gradient is sent backward along the same connections that carried the activations forward. Second, we scale each entry by the local slope of the nonlinearity.

\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W^m} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a^m}\,{h^{m-1}}^\top

Once we know \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial a^m, the weight gradient is its outer product with the previous layer’s activations.

The cost of backpropagation

How expensive is one training step? Suppose the model is a stack of matrix multiplies and element-wise nonlinearities, and let |\theta| be the total number of parameters (all weights and biases). A matrix multiply does one multiply-add per entry of the weight matrix, and a weight matrix has far more entries than its layer has units. The matrix multiplies therefore dominate the cost, and the nonlinearities add comparatively little. For a single example, the forward pass costs roughly |\theta| multiply-adds.

The backward pass has two parts, and each costs about as much as the forward pass:

  • The recursion multiplies by {W^{m+1}}^\top at every layer. That is a matrix–vector product with the same number of entries as the forward multiply.
  • The weight gradients are outer products. Each produces an object as large as W^m itself.
Pass What it computes Approximate cost
Forward a^m and h^m for all m |\theta|
Backward: recursion \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial a^m for all m |\theta|
Backward: weight gradients \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial W^m for all m |\theta|
Note

Rule of thumb. One training step (forward plus backward) costs roughly three forward passes, about 3|\theta| multiply-adds per example. This is why training compute is often estimated directly from the parameter count.

Worked example: sigmoid units with cross-entropy loss

Take a network for binary classification where every layer, including the output layer, uses the sigmoid function:

h^m = \operatorname{sigmoid}(a^m) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-a^m)} \qquad \text{for all } m, \text{ including } m = L

The label is y \in \{0, 1\}, and the loss is the cross-entropy from Week 1, written here for a single output:

\mathcal{L}(\bm{x}, h^L) = (y - 1)\log(1 - h^L) - y \log(h^L)

Output layer. The two factors in the first step are the derivative of the cross-entropy and the derivative of the sigmoid. The sigmoid’s derivative can be written using its own output, \partial h^L / \partial a^L = h^L(1 - h^L). Then:

\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a^L} = \frac{h^L - y}{h^L(1 - h^L)} \cdot h^L(1 - h^L) = h^L - y

The denominator of the cross-entropy derivative cancels exactly against the sigmoid derivative, leaving the prediction minus the label. This is the same “\hat{\bm{y}} - \bm{y}” form we saw for softmax and cross-entropy in Week 1.

One layer back. Apply the recursion with m = L-1. The local slope is again the sigmoid derivative, now evaluated at layer L-1:

\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a^{L-1}} = \left({W^{L}}^\top (h^L - y)\right) \odot h^{L-1}\left(1 - h^{L-1}\right)

Weight gradient. The gradient for the weights of layer L-1 multiplies this by the activations of layer L-2:

\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W^{L-1}} = \left({W^{L}}^\top (h^L - y)\right) \odot h^{L-1}\left(1 - h^{L-1}\right)\, {h^{L-2}}^\top

Reading the result from left to right, the error h^L - y is multiplied by {W^L}^\top, which sends it backward along the connections into layer L-1, and then each entry is multiplied by a sigmoid slope factor h^{L-1}(1 - h^{L-1}). Every additional layer we go back adds one more such pair of factors. That repeated pattern is the subject of the next section.

TipQuestion: What does one more layer back look like, with actual numbers?

Apply the recursion once more, with m = L-2. The gradient at layer L-1 is multiplied by {W^{L-1}}^\top, which sends it backward along the connections into layer L-2, and each entry is then scaled by the sigmoid slope of layer L-2:

\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a^{L-2}} = \left({W^{L-1}}^\top \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a^{L-1}}\right) \odot h^{L-2}\left(1 - h^{L-2}\right)

Substituting the previous line shows the pile-up. The result contains two matrix factors and two slope factors, in the order they were met going backward:

\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a^{L-2}} &= \left({W^{L-1}}^\top \left[\left({W^{L}}^\top (h^L - y)\right) \right.\right. \\ &\qquad \left.\left. \odot\, h^{L-1}(1 - h^{L-1})\right]\right) \odot h^{L-2}(1 - h^{L-2}) \end{aligned}

To see how fast this shrinks, use the smallest possible network: one unit per layer, so every quantity is a single number, with every weight equal to 1. Suppose each unit sits at a = 0, so every activation is h = 1/2 and every sigmoid slope is h(1 - h) = 1/4. Take y = 1. Then the output error is h^L - y = 1/2 - 1 = -1/2. Multiplying by a weight of 1 leaves a number unchanged, so each step back only multiplies by the slope 1/4:

Layer \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial a How it was obtained
L -0.5 h^L - y
L-1 -0.125 1 \cdot (-0.5) \cdot 0.25
L-2 -0.03125 1 \cdot (-0.125) \cdot 0.25
L-3 -0.0078125 1 \cdot (-0.03125) \cdot 0.25

Each layer back divides the signal by 4, so after three layers it is 1/64 of its size at the output. This is the best case for the sigmoid, since 1/4 is the largest slope it can have, and it assumed weights of 1 that neither shrink nor grow the signal. Since no larger slope is available, only the weights could offset the shrinkage, which is why the initialization section below focuses on them.

Vanishing and Exploding Gradients

How do the gradients of early layers behave in a deep network? Look again at the recursion:

\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a^m} = \left({W^{m+1}}^\top \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a^{m+1}}\right) \odot \frac{\partial h^m}{\partial a^m}

Going back one layer applies two operations, and each is repeated at every layer:

  • Repeated matrix multiplies. The factor {W^{m+1}}^\top appears once per layer. Multiplying a vector by many matrices can shrink it toward zero or grow it without bound, depending on the size of the weights.
  • Repeated nonlinearity derivatives. The factor \partial h^m / \partial a^m also appears once per layer.
TipQuestion: Why can multiplying by weight matrices again and again make the gradient explode or vanish?

Start with one number. Multiplying by 2 three times turns 1 into 8, and multiplying by 1/2 three times turns 1 into 1/8. A matrix behaves the same way along special directions. An eigenvector of a matrix M is a direction \bm{v} that M only stretches or shrinks, M\bm{v} = \lambda\bm{v}, and the number \lambda is its eigenvalue. Applying M a total of k times to \bm{v} gives \lambda^k \bm{v}, which grows when |\lambda| > 1 and shrinks when |\lambda| < 1.

For example, let M stretch the first coordinate by 2 and shrink the second by 1/2. Applying it three times to (1, 1)^\top gives

\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/2 \end{pmatrix}^{3} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 8 \\ 1/8 \end{pmatrix}

The first entry exploded and the second vanished.

In the backward recursion the matrix is different at every layer, so no single eigenvalue decides the outcome. What matters is how much each matrix tends to stretch or shrink the vectors passing through it. If they tend to stretch, gradients explode. If they tend to shrink, gradients vanish. These matrices are the weights themselves, so their scale is something we control through how we initialize them.

For the sigmoid, this second factor is at most 1/4. The sigmoid equals 1/2 at a = 0 and flattens toward 0 and 1 on either side, so its slope is largest at 0, where it equals 1/4, and falls toward 0 for large |a|.

Definition. Vanishing gradients occur when the gradient shrinks toward 0 as it is propagated backward, so that early layers receive almost no learning signal. Exploding gradients occur when it grows toward \infty instead. See the vanishing gradient problem.

Both come from the same recursion. If the repeated factors are typically smaller than 1 in size, the gradient decays layer by layer; if they are typically larger, it grows. In a sigmoid network the slope factor alone contributes at most 1/4 per layer, which is why Week 2 already flagged vanishing gradients for sigmoid.

Warning

Why sigmoid is rarely used as a hidden activation. The ReLU activation from Week 2, \operatorname{ReLU}(a) = \max(0, a), has a derivative that is exactly 0 or exactly 1. Where it is 1, multiplying by it over and over neither shrinks nor grows the gradient. This is one reason ReLU made much deeper networks practical to train.

The slope factor is fixed once we choose the activation function. The weight factor is not: it is set by how we choose the weight matrices before training even starts. That is the next topic.

Parameter Initialization

Before training starts, every weight needs a value. The slides use the word initialize to mean something specific: choose a probability distribution and sample the initial weights from it. If we want to avoid vanishing and exploding gradients, the choice of that distribution matters, and the variance of the weights is the natural knob.

Definition. Parameter initialization is the choice of the distribution from which the initial values of the model’s weights are sampled before training begins.

Two bad ideas

Why not start with every weight equal to 0? Then W\bm{x} = \bm{0} for every input, so each layer’s output stops depending on the input at all (ignoring the bias). Training cannot easily fix this either. The backward recursion multiplies by {W^{m+1}}^\top = 0, so at initialization every layer below the output receives exactly zero gradient, and even after updates begin, the units within a layer stay identical to each other.

TipQuestion: What does starting from all zeros look like, with actual numbers?

Take the smallest network that shows both effects: input \bm{x} = (1, 2)^\top, a hidden layer of two sigmoid units, and one output unit with no nonlinearity, so h^2 = a^2. The loss is \mathcal{L} = \tfrac{1}{2}(h^2 - y)^2 with target y = 1, and the learning rate is \eta = 0.1. Every weight and bias starts at 0.

Step 1, forward. The hidden pre-activations are a^1 = W^1\bm{x} + b^1 = (0, 0)^\top. The sigmoid of 0 is 1/2, so h^1 = (0.5, 0.5)^\top. The output is a^2 = W^2 h^1 + b^2 = 0, so h^2 = 0.

Step 1, backward. At the output, \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial a^2 = h^2 - y = -1. The output weights get a real gradient, because they multiply the nonzero activations h^1:

\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W^2} = (-1)\,(0.5,\; 0.5) = (-0.5,\; -0.5), \qquad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial b^2} = -1

The hidden layer gets nothing, because the recursion multiplies by {W^2}^\top = (0, 0)^\top. The sigmoid slope at 0 is 1/4, but a slope times zero is still zero:

\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a^1} = \left({W^2}^\top(-1)\right) \odot \tfrac{1}{4} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}

Since the weight gradient is \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial a^1 times \bm{x}^\top, it is zero too:

\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W^1} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}

Step 1, update. Only the output layer moves: W^2 = (0.05,\; 0.05) and b^2 = 0.1. The hidden weights W^1 and biases b^1 are still all 0.

Step 2. The hidden layer is unchanged, so h^1 = (0.5, 0.5)^\top again, and the output is now a^2 = 0.05 \cdot 0.5 + 0.05 \cdot 0.5 + 0.1 = 0.15. Then \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial a^2 = 0.15 - 1 = -0.85, and the hidden layer finally receives a nonzero gradient, because W^2 is no longer zero:

\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a^1} &= \begin{pmatrix} 0.05 \\ 0.05 \end{pmatrix}(-0.85) \odot \tfrac{1}{4} \\ &= \begin{pmatrix} -0.010625 \\ -0.010625 \end{pmatrix} \end{aligned}

Both hidden units receive the same number. The weight gradient multiplies this by \bm{x}^\top = (1, 2), so both rows of \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial W^1 equal (-0.010625,\; -0.02125), and the update gives

W^1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0.0010625 & 0.002125 \\ 0.0010625 & 0.002125 \end{pmatrix}

The two rows, and the two entries of b^1, are identical. Two units with identical weights and biases compute identical outputs, so they receive identical gradients on every later step and stay identical forever. The hidden layer of two units behaves as a hidden layer of one, which is the wasted capacity described above.

The specific numbers here are our own, invented for illustration, not from the lecture or a homework. In this sigmoid network the zero hidden gradient lasts only one step. The identical-units problem does not go away.

Why not give every weight the same nonzero constant, say 1? Then every row of W computes the same dot product with the input, so every hidden unit computes the same function. We waste nearly all of the layer’s capacity on redundant copies. We need initial values that are random but carefully scaled, and the scale is what the next subsections work out.

A variance refresher

The derivation below uses three facts about a random variable X. The variance is \operatorname{Var}[X] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2, which reduces to \mathbb{E}[X^2] when \mathbb{E}[X] = 0. The normal distribution \mathcal{N}(0, s^2) has mean 0 and variance s^2. For independent variables, the expectation of a product is the product of expectations, \mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[Y].

TipQuestion: Can these three facts be checked on actual numbers before we use them?

Take a random variable X that equals -1 or 1, each with probability 1/2. Its mean is \mathbb{E}[X] = \tfrac{1}{2}(-1) + \tfrac{1}{2}(1) = 0, and \mathbb{E}[X^2] = \tfrac{1}{2}(-1)^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}(1)^2 = 1. Since the mean is 0, the variance is just \mathbb{E}[X^2]:

\operatorname{Var}[X] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2 = 1 - 0^2 = 1

This matches a \mathcal{N}(0, 1) variable, which by definition has variance 1.

Now take a second variable W, independent of X, equal to -2 or 2, each with probability 1/2. By the same computation, \mathbb{E}[W] = 0 and \operatorname{Var}[W] = \mathbb{E}[W^2] = 4, matching a \mathcal{N}(0, 4) variable, so its \sigma^2 = 4.

To check \mathbb{E}[WX] = \mathbb{E}[W]\,\mathbb{E}[X], list all four equally likely combinations of (W, X) and their products:

W X WX
-2 -1 2
-2 1 -2
2 -1 -2
2 1 2

Averaging the last column, \mathbb{E}[WX] = \tfrac{1}{4}(2 - 2 - 2 + 2) = 0, and indeed \mathbb{E}[W]\,\mathbb{E}[X] = 0 \cdot 0 = 0. The two sides agree because every value of W pairs with both values of X equally often, so the positive and negative products cancel. This is the same cancellation used below to drop the cross terms W_{ij}x_jW_{ik}x_k with j \neq k: the sum has both signs of each factor appearing equally often, so its average is 0.

Variance of one linear layer

Consider a single linear layer with no nonlinearity, mapping an input \bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_\text{in}} to an output \bm{h} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_\text{out}}:

\bm{h} = W\bm{x}, \qquad h_i = \sum_{j=1}^{n_\text{in}} W_{ij}\, x_j

The slides make three assumptions: there is no nonlinearity, each input entry satisfies x_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1), and each weight satisfies W_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2), all independent. The question is what \sigma to use.

Because every W_{ij} and x_j has mean 0 and they are independent, \mathbb{E}[h_i] = 0. So the variance of h_i is just \mathbb{E}[h_i^2]. Expanding the square of the sum produces cross terms of the form W_{ij}x_j W_{ik}x_k with j \neq k; each has expectation 0 by independence and zero mean, so only the squared terms remain:

\operatorname{Var}[h_i] = \mathbb{E}[h_i^2] = \sum_{j=1}^{n_\text{in}} \mathbb{E}[W_{ij}^2\, x_j^2]

Independence splits each expectation, and \mathbb{E}[W_{ij}^2] = \sigma^2 and \mathbb{E}[x_j^2] = 1:

TipQuestion: Where do the values E[W_ij^2] = sigma^2 and E[x_j^2] = 1 come from?

Both follow from the identity in the variance refresher above, \mathbb{E}[X^2] = (\mathbb{E}[X])^2 + \operatorname{Var}[X], applied to a mean-0 variable, where the first term vanishes and \mathbb{E}[X^2] reduces to \operatorname{Var}[X].

W_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) by assumption, so \mathbb{E}[W_{ij}] = 0 and \operatorname{Var}[W_{ij}] = \sigma^2. Then \mathbb{E}[W_{ij}^2] = 0^2 + \sigma^2 = \sigma^2.

Likewise x_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1), so \mathbb{E}[x_j] = 0 and \operatorname{Var}[x_j] = 1, giving \mathbb{E}[x_j^2] = 0^2 + 1 = 1.

Neither value takes any work beyond reading off the variance of the assumed distribution, because the assumed mean is 0 in both cases.

\operatorname{Var}[h_i] = \sum_{j=1}^{n_\text{in}} \mathbb{E}[W_{ij}^2]\,\mathbb{E}[x_j^2] = n_\text{in}\,\sigma^2

The output has variance n_\text{in}\sigma^2 while the input has variance 1, so the layer multiplies the variance by n_\text{in}\sigma^2. Stack many layers and this factor is applied repeatedly: if it is above 1 activations grow with depth, if below 1 they shrink.

Choosing sigma

Forward pass. To keep the variance of the activations near 1, we want n_\text{in}\,\sigma^2 = 1.

Backward pass. In the backward pass the roles of input and output swap, because the gradient is multiplied by W^\top and each entry of the result is a sum over n_\text{out} terms. The slides therefore state the analogous requirement n_\text{out}\,\sigma^2 = 1 (the lecture gives this by analogy and does not repeat the derivation).

Unless n_\text{in} = n_\text{out} the two requirements disagree. The slides settle on a compromise, averaging them:

\frac{n_\text{in}\,\sigma^2 + n_\text{out}\,\sigma^2}{2} = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sigma = \sqrt{\frac{2}{n_\text{in} + n_\text{out}}}

W_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\; \frac{2}{n_\text{in} + n_\text{out}}\right)

This is called Xavier or Glorot initialization (see weight initialization). It keeps the layer’s variance factor near 1 in both directions when the layer widths differ, without being exact for either. The derivation also assumed no nonlinearity, so for a network with activation functions it is a starting heuristic and not a guarantee.

Note

He initialization. If we instead assume a ReLU activation, the same style of derivation gives a slightly different scale, \sigma = \sqrt{2/n_\text{in}}, called He initialization. The lecture states this result without the derivation. One intuition, which is ours and not from the slides: for zero-mean, symmetric pre-activations, ReLU sets roughly half of them to 0, which halves the average squared activation, and the extra factor of 2 compensates. Keras and TensorFlow default to Xavier (Glorot) uniform initialization. PyTorch’s linear layers default to a smaller fan-in scale, U(-1/\sqrt{n_\text{in}}, 1/\sqrt{n_\text{in}}), so it is common to request Xavier or He explicitly. Homework 3 assumes ReLU, so the He scale is the relevant one there; the specific calculation is left to the assignment.

Automatic Differentiation

Backpropagation for an MLP took a page of matrix calculus. Can a program produce gradients for any model we write, without us deriving them by hand? The answer is yes, and the tool is called automatic differentiation, or autodiff (in PyTorch, autograd). See automatic differentiation.

A small running example

The slides use a tiny network with one hidden unit. We treat every quantity as a single number, so that the chain of operations is easy to see:

h = \operatorname{ReLU}(w_h x + b_h), \qquad o = w_o h + b_o, \qquad \mathcal{L} = (y - o)^2

The subscripts h and o name which layer a quantity belongs to, hidden or output, not an index running over several units: w_h and b_h are the hidden layer’s weight and bias, and w_o and b_o are the output layer’s. The same convention carries into the computational-graph diagram below, where the intermediate results m_h, a_h, and m_o take the subscript of whichever layer’s computation produced them.

Here \operatorname{ReLU}(a) = \max(0, a) as in Week 2. The parameters are w_h, b_h, w_o, b_o, and we want the four gradients \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial w_o, \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial b_o, \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial w_h, and \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial b_h. Here h is the single hidden unit’s output, the one-number version of the activation h^m above, and o is the network output.

Break the computation into single operations

We can always use the chain rule. To make that mechanical, first rewrite the forward computation as a sequence of single simple operations, giving each intermediate result a name:

\begin{aligned} m_h &= w_h x, & a_h &= m_h + b_h, & h &= \operatorname{ReLU}(a_h), \\ m_o &= w_o h, & o &= m_o + b_o, & e &= y - o, \\ \mathcal{L} &= e^2 \end{aligned}

Then the gradient with respect to w_h is a product of one derivative per operation on the path from \mathcal{L} back to w_h:

\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_h} &= \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial e}\,\frac{\partial e}{\partial o}\,\frac{\partial o}{\partial m_o} \\ &\quad \cdot \frac{\partial m_o}{\partial h}\,\frac{\partial h}{\partial a_h} \\ &\quad \cdot \frac{\partial a_h}{\partial m_h}\,\frac{\partial m_h}{\partial w_h} \end{aligned}

Each factor is the derivative of one elementary operation, and each is simple:

Operation Derivative used
\mathcal{L} = e^2 \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial e = 2e
e = y - o \partial e/\partial o = -1
o = m_o + b_o \partial o/\partial m_o = 1
m_o = w_o h \partial m_o/\partial h = w_o
h = \operatorname{ReLU}(a_h) \partial h/\partial a_h = 1 if a_h > 0, else 0
a_h = m_h + b_h \partial a_h/\partial m_h = 1
m_h = w_h x \partial m_h/\partial w_h = x

Multiplying them gives \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial w_h = 2e \cdot (-1) \cdot w_o \cdot \mathbb{1}[a_h > 0] \cdot x, where \mathbb{1}[a_h > 0] is 1 when a_h > 0 and 0 otherwise.

What autodiff needs to do

To compute any one gradient, such as \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial w_h, we need to know two things:

  1. Which operations were applied, and in what order.
  2. The derivative of each operation.

So an autodiff system does three jobs:

  1. It defines a set of operations.
  2. It defines the derivative of each of those operations.
  3. It keeps track of which operations were applied, in a computational graph.

Definition. A computational graph records a computation as a graph. Each node is either an input (a leaf node, such as x, y, or a parameter) or the result of one operation applied to earlier nodes. The graph is built as the forward computation runs.

To compute \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial w_h, find the path in the graph between \mathcal{L} and w_h and multiply the derivatives of the operations along it. That product is exactly the chain-rule product above, so autodiff is backpropagation applied to an arbitrary graph of operations instead of only to a stack of layers. The forward values that some of the derivatives need, such as x or a_h, are the intermediate results the graph has already recorded.

TipQuestion: Why does the exponent 2 get its own node in the graph?

The slides draw the loss \mathcal{L} = e^2 as a “pow” operation with two inputs: e, and a separate leaf holding the number 2. That follows from how an autodiff library defines its operations.

One power operation, not one per exponent. Rather than having separate “square”, “cube”, and so on, the library defines a single power operation, \operatorname{pow}(u, k) = u^k, that works for any exponent. The exponent is then an input to the operation, the same way w_o and h are the two inputs to the multiply that forms m_o. Every input to an operation is a node in the graph, so the exponent appears as a node too.

It is a leaf because nothing computes it. Leaf nodes are the values the computation starts from. The constant 2 is not produced by any earlier operation, so it sits at the edge of the graph alongside x, y, and the parameters.

The backward pass needs its value. The backward rule for power is \partial u^k/\partial u = k\,u^{k-1}. To apply it at the loss node, the library must know k, and it reads the inputs of an operation from the nodes the graph recorded, just as the multiply rule reads h to compute \partial m_o/\partial w_o = h. With k = 2 the rule gives 2e, which is the \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial e in the table above.

No gradient is needed for it. The 2 is not a parameter we train, so, like x and y, we never use a gradient with respect to it.

This is a design choice, not a law. A library that had a dedicated “square” operation with a single input would have no node for the 2. That is why a question that asks you to count nodes states the convention up front, for example “raise to a constant power (the exponent is a separate leaf node)”.

Putting the pieces together gives the general procedure:

Automatic Differentiation

Define, once, for a small library of elementary operations (add, multiply, subtract, power, ReLU, and so on):

  • forward: how to compute the operation’s output from its inputs.
  • backward: the derivative of the output with respect to each input.

Given a model (a composition of these operations):

  1. Forward pass. Evaluate every operation in order, and record which operations were applied to which inputs. This record is the computational graph.
  2. Backward pass. Start from \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathcal{L} = 1. Visit the nodes in reverse topological order, meaning each node only after every node that uses it. At each operation node, multiply the node’s accumulated gradient by the operation’s derivative with respect to each input, and add the result to that input’s running gradient.

When the sweep finishes, every leaf node holds its gradient. One sweep gives the gradients for all leaves at once.

The word add matters. If a node feeds into more than one later operation, there is more than one path from the loss to it, and the chain rule says to sum the contributions of the paths (the “multiple intermediate variables” case in Week 2). Adding each contribution into a running total performs that sum without listing the paths. For example, in m = w \cdot w the leaf w is both inputs of one multiplication. If the gradient arriving at m is g, the backward rule sends g\,w to each of the two inputs, and adding them gives 2gw, which matches the derivative of w^2.

Overfitting

Suppose training works: the loss on the training set keeps falling. Does that mean the model is getting better? Not necessarily, because the goal is to do well on data the model has not seen.

Recall the Universal Approximation Theorem from Week 2: a sufficiently wide network can fit essentially any function, including simply memorizing a finite training set. So overfitting is a real risk. It is also not specific to neural networks; the same ideas apply to any sufficiently flexible model.

Definition. A validation set is a collection of labeled examples that is held out and never used to update the parameters. The model is overfit when its loss on the training set keeps decreasing while its loss on the validation set stops decreasing and starts to increase. See overfitting and training, validation, and test data sets.

The slides show the same effect in a different picture. A true function generates noisy training points. A flexible enough model can pass through every training point, including the noise, and so its curve between and beyond those points can be far from the true function.

Can a linear model overfit?

The slides ask how we can overfit with a linear model, whose shape is so constrained. The setup is a binary classification problem. Each example has an input vector \bm{x} and a label y \in \{-1, 1\}, and every entry of the input is a noisy measurement of the label:

x_j \sim \mathcal{N}(y, \sigma^2)

That is, an entry of \bm{x} is centered on -1 when y = -1 and on 1 when y = 1, with spread \sigma. (This \sigma is the noise level of the data, not the weight standard deviation from the initialization section or the activation function \sigma from Week 2.) We fit a linear model \hat{y} = \bm{w}^\top\bm{x}.

Every entry of \bm{x} is an equally noisy signal about y, but with a small training set the noise will not look equal across entries. In the first two rows of Figure 5 the classes overlap. In the last row the six labels happen to separate with no mistakes, by chance. A training procedure that only sees this data can give that entry a large weight, because it fits the training labels well. On new data that entry is no more informative than the others, so a model that leans heavily on it does worse than one that spreads its weight across all entries. This is overfitting in a linear model: over-reliance on a feature that happened to look good on the training set.

The same failure mode shows up in deep networks. With many learned features and a limited training set, some learned feature can become accidentally separable, and the model over-invests in it. Both regularizers below act on this over-reliance.

Regularization

How can we avoid overreliance on a feature? The slides give two answers. This kind of technique is called regularization: anything we do to a training procedure to reduce overfitting.

Weight decay

If large weights are the problem, we can penalize them. Add a term to the loss that grows with the squared length \lVert \bm{w} \rVert_2^2 of the weight vector, scaled by a number \lambda > 0:

\mathcal{L}_\text{new} = \mathcal{L} + \lambda \lVert \bm{w} \rVert_2^2

The new loss is the objective we minimize. The value \lambda is a hyperparameter, meaning we choose it and do not learn it (hyperparameter). The gradient of the penalty is 2\lambda\bm{w}, so one step of gradient descent with learning rate \eta becomes:

\bm{w} - \eta\,\nabla_{\bm{w}}\!\left(\mathcal{L} + \lambda \lVert \bm{w} \rVert_2^2\right) = (1 - 2\eta\lambda)\,\bm{w} - \eta\,\nabla_{\bm{w}}\mathcal{L}

Definition. Weight decay is the update above. Before applying the usual gradient step, each weight is multiplied by the factor (1 - 2\eta\lambda), which is slightly less than 1, so the weights shrink toward 0 a little at every step. It is the gradient descent form of adding \lambda\lVert\bm{w}\rVert_2^2 to the loss, a penalty also known as Tikhonov regularization or an \ell_2 penalty (weight decay).

A weight only stays large if the loss gradient keeps pushing it there, so a feature that helped on the training set by chance is held back.

For a weight matrix W, the same penalty uses the squared Frobenius norm, \lVert W \rVert_F^2 = \sum_{i,j} W_{ij}^2, the sum of the squares of all its entries, and the update has the same form with W in place of \bm{w}.

Dropout

A different approach is to make sure no single feature can be relied on, by sometimes removing it. During training, each feature is independently dropped out at random (dropout). With a probability p (a hyperparameter) it is set to 0, and otherwise it is scaled up:

x_i' = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{with probability } p \\[2pt] \dfrac{x_i}{1 - p} & \text{with probability } 1 - p \end{cases}

The scaling by 1/(1-p) keeps the average value of the feature unchanged, because \mathbb{E}[x_i'] = p \cdot 0 + (1-p)\cdot\dfrac{x_i}{1-p} = x_i. Since the training-time input has the same expectation as the original, we do nothing special at test time: during testing, we just use x_i with no dropping and no rescaling.

Because a feature is missing on a random fraction of steps, the model cannot put all its weight on one of them. In practice dropout is applied not only to input features but also to the activations inside a network. Weight decay and dropout are the two regularizers you will reach for most often in deep learning.

Summary

Note

Key takeaways from Week 3:

  • Notation. Pre-activation is a^m and activation is h^m, as in Week 2. The handwritten slides call them z^m and a^m. The backprop recursion is unchanged.
  • Sigmoid plus cross-entropy. The output-layer gradient simplifies to h^L - y, because the sigmoid slope cancels the denominator of the cross-entropy derivative.
  • Cost. A training step (forward plus backward) costs roughly three forward passes, about 3|\theta| per example.
  • Vanishing and exploding gradients. The backward recursion repeats a matrix multiply and a nonlinearity derivative at every layer. The sigmoid derivative is at most 1/4, so gradients tend to shrink. ReLU’s derivative is exactly 0 or 1, and whether the matrix term explodes or vanishes depends on the weights.
  • Initialization. All-zero or all-equal weights fail, so we sample randomly with a careful scale. For a linear layer with unit-variance inputs and \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2) weights, the output variance is n_\text{in}\sigma^2. Averaging the forward and backward requirements gives Xavier/Glorot initialization, \sigma = \sqrt{2/(n_\text{in} + n_\text{out})}. He initialization, \sqrt{2/n_\text{in}}, is the ReLU variant.
  • Autodiff. A system defines operations and their derivatives and records the operations in a computational graph. A gradient is the product of local derivatives along a path from the loss to a parameter. The backward pass visits nodes in reverse topological order and adds each contribution into a running gradient, which sums over paths.
  • Overfitting. Training loss keeps falling while validation loss rises. Overfitting is not specific to neural networks, and even a linear model can overfit by over-relying on a feature that looked good by chance.
  • Regularization. Weight decay multiplies the weights by (1 - 2\eta\lambda) each step. Dropout zeroes each feature with probability p and rescales the rest by 1/(1-p).
