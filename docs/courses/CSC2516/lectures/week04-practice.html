Part 1: Gradient descent and the learning rate

Problem 1: How big is the leftover term?

Take the loss \mathcal{L}(\theta) = 2\theta^2, start at \theta = 1, and take one gradient descent step with \eta = 0.2. The first two terms of the Taylor series predict a new loss. By how much is the exact new loss above that prediction?

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 4, Why Gradient Descent Works and its worked example.

The step. The derivative is \mathcal{L}'(\theta) = 4\theta = 4 at \theta = 1, so the offset is \epsilon = -\eta\,\mathcal{L}'(\theta) = -0.8 and the new point is \theta = 0.2.

The two-term prediction.

\mathcal{L}(\theta) + \epsilon\,\mathcal{L}'(\theta) = 2 + (-0.8)(4) = -1.2

A negative loss is impossible for 2\theta^2, which already says the step is too long for the tangent line to be trusted.

The exact new loss. \mathcal{L}(0.2) = 2 \cdot 0.04 = 0.08.

The gap. For a quadratic the series stops after the third term, so the gap is exactly the term the big-O was hiding, \tfrac{\epsilon^2}{2}\mathcal{L}''(\theta) with \mathcal{L}'' = 4:

\frac{(-0.8)^2}{2}\cdot 4 = 0.32 \cdot 4 = 1.28

and indeed 0.08 - (-1.2) = 1.28. The loss still dropped (from 2 to 0.08), but by less than the tangent line promised.

Answer: 1.28.

Problem 2: A learning rate that lands on the minimum

For the loss \mathcal{L}(\theta) = 4\theta^2, there is one learning rate for which a single gradient descent step from any starting \theta lands exactly on the minimum. What is it?

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 4, Choosing the Learning Rate and the divergence limit in Descending in a valley.

The derivative is 8\theta, so one step gives

\theta - \eta \cdot 8\theta = (1 - 8\eta)\,\theta

Every step multiplies \theta by the same factor 1 - 8\eta. The minimum is at \theta = 0, and we land on it from any start exactly when the factor is 0, that is, \eta = 1/8.

The same factor sorts every other learning rate into the lecture’s regimes. With a second derivative of h = 8:

\eta Factor 1 - 8\eta Behaviour
0.05 0.6 shrinks steadily toward 0 (small \eta)
0.125 0 lands on the minimum in one step
0.2 -0.6 jumps across the minimum but still shrinks
0.3 -1.4 jumps across and grows: divergence, since \eta > 2/h = 0.25

For a quadratic the one-step learning rate is 1/h, half of the divergence limit 2/h.

Answer: 0.125.

Problem 3: The predicted decrease with two parameters

Take \mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}) = \theta_1^2 + 4\theta_2^2, start at \bm{\theta} = (1,\ 1), and take one gradient descent step with \eta = 0.05. According to the first-order Taylor approximation, by how much does the loss decrease?

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 4, The Multivariate Case.

The gradient. \nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L} = (2\theta_1,\ 8\theta_2) = (2,\ 8).

The predicted decrease. Substituting \bm{\epsilon} = -\eta\nabla\mathcal{L} into the first-order approximation gives a decrease of \eta times the dot product of the gradient with itself:

\eta\,\lVert\nabla\mathcal{L}\rVert^2 = 0.05\,(2^2 + 8^2) = 0.05 \cdot 68

Compare with the exact step. The new point is (1 - 0.1,\ 1 - 0.4) = (0.9,\ 0.6), with loss 0.81 + 4 \cdot 0.36 = 2.25. The loss started at 5, so it actually fell by 2.75. The prediction is optimistic by 0.65, the leftover term. Most of that leftover comes from \theta_2, the steeper direction, whose large gradient makes the step there long.

Answer: 3.4.

Part 2: SGD, mini-batches, and schedules

Problem 4: A single example can point the wrong way

A training set has three examples with per-example losses \mathcal{L}_i(\theta) = \tfrac{1}{2}(\theta - a_i)^2, where (a_1, a_2, a_3) = (2,\ 5,\ 11), and the training loss is their average. At \theta = 4, one step of stochastic gradient descent picks one example uniformly at random. What is the probability that this step moves \theta in the opposite direction from a full-batch gradient descent step?

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 4, Stochastic Gradient Descent.

Per-example gradients. The derivative of \tfrac{1}{2}(\theta - a_i)^2 is \theta - a_i, so at \theta = 4 the three gradients are

(4 - 2,\ 4 - 5,\ 4 - 11) = (2,\ -1,\ -7)

Full-batch gradient. Their average is (2 - 1 - 7)/3 = -2. A full-batch step \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta(-2) increases \theta, toward the minimum of the training loss at the mean of the a_i, which is 6.

SGD. Each example is picked with probability 1/3, and its step is -\eta(\theta - a_i):

Example Gradient Step direction
1 +2 decreases \theta (opposite)
2 -1 increases \theta (same)
3 -7 increases \theta (same)

The expected SGD gradient is still \tfrac{1}{3}(2 - 1 - 7) = -2, exactly the full-batch gradient. “Right on average” does not mean every step is right: example 1 has its own minimum at \theta = 2, and stepping toward it moves away from 6.

Answer: 1/3.

Problem 5: How large a mini-batch?

Suppose the gradient of a single randomly chosen example has standard deviation 0.8 around the full-batch gradient. A mini-batch averages B examples, each drawn independently and uniformly at random. What is the smallest B for which the mini-batch gradient’s standard deviation is at most 0.1?

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 4, Mini-batches and the variance rules from the Week 3 practice chapter.

The mini-batch gradient is \tfrac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B} g_i with independent draws. By rule 3 the sum has variance B \cdot 0.8^2, and by rule 2 the factor 1/B comes out squared:

\operatorname{Var}\left[\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B} g_i\right] = \frac{B \cdot 0.8^2}{B^2} = \frac{0.8^2}{B}

So the standard deviation is 0.8/\sqrt{B}. Requiring 0.8/\sqrt{B} \le 0.1 gives \sqrt{B} \ge 8, so B \ge 64.

The square root is the lesson: cutting the noise by a factor of 8 costs 64 times as many gradients per step. That is why mini-batches reduce SGD’s noise but are not a cheap way to remove it.

Answer: 64.

Problem 6: Reading an exponential decay schedule

A run uses the exponential decay schedule \eta(t) = \eta_0\,e^{-\lambda t} with \eta_0 = 0.4, and its decay speed \lambda is chosen so that the learning rate halves every 100 iterations. What is \eta(300)?

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 4, Learning rate schedules.

“Halves every 100 iterations” means e^{-100\lambda} = 1/2, so \lambda = \ln 2 / 100 \approx 0.0069. We do not need \lambda itself: 300 iterations is three halving periods, so

\eta(300) = 0.4\,\left(e^{-100\lambda}\right)^3 = 0.4 \cdot \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^3

Exponential decay shrinks the learning rate by the same factor over every stretch of the same length, which is what lets the parameters settle when SGD starts to jitter near the minimum.

Answer: 0.05.

Part 3: Momentum and Adam

Problem 7: Distance covered with momentum

A single parameter is trained with momentum, v_t \leftarrow \beta v_{t-1} + g_t and \theta_t \leftarrow \theta_{t-1} - \eta v_t, using \beta = 0.5, \eta = 0.1, and v_0 = 0. The gradient is g_t = 2 at every step. How far does \theta move in total over the first three steps?

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 4, Momentum.

The accumulator.

Step v_t = 0.5\,v_{t-1} + 2 Move \eta v_t
1 2 0.2
2 1 + 2 = 3 0.3
3 1.5 + 2 = 3.5 0.35

All three moves are in the same direction, so they add: 0.2 + 0.3 + 0.35 = 0.85.

Compare. Plain gradient descent would move 0.2 per step, 0.6 in total. The gradient is consistent, so momentum’s steps grow, toward the limit v = 2/(1 - 0.5) = 4, twice the plain step. With \beta = 0.9 the limit would be ten times the plain step, as in the week chapter.

Answer: 0.85.

Problem 8: A bias-corrected average is not a plain average

Adam’s first-moment accumulator uses \beta_1 = 0.5 and starts at v_0 = 0. The first two gradients are g_1 = 4 and g_2 = 10. What is the bias-corrected value \hat{v}_2?

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 4, The bias toward zero.

The moving average.

v_1 = 0.5 \cdot 0 + 0.5 \cdot 4 = 2, \qquad v_2 = 0.5 \cdot 2 + 0.5 \cdot 10 = 6

The correction. The total scaling of the gradients in v_2 is 1 - \beta_1^2 = 0.75, so

\hat{v}_2 = \frac{6}{0.75} = 8

Why not 7? The plain average of 4 and 10 is 7. Written out, v_2 = 0.25\,g_1 + 0.5\,g_2, and dividing by 0.75 gives weights 1/3 on g_1 and 2/3 on g_2. Bias correction makes the weights add to 1, so \hat{v}_2 is a true average, but it keeps the exponential moving average’s preference for recent gradients: \tfrac{1}{3}\cdot 4 + \tfrac{2}{3}\cdot 10 = 8.

Answer: 8.

Problem 9: When epsilon is not negligible

A parameter is trained with Adam using the default \beta_1 = 0.9, \beta_2 = 0.999, and \epsilon = 10^{-8}. Its gradient on the first step is tiny: g_1 = 10^{-8}. The size of the first step is what fraction of \eta?

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 4, The full algorithm and its first-step worked example in The bias toward zero.

On the first step bias correction is exact: \hat{v}_1 = g_1 and \hat{s}_1 = g_1^2, so \sqrt{\hat{s}_1} = |g_1|. The step is

\frac{\eta\,\hat{v}_1}{\sqrt{\hat{s}_1} + \epsilon} = \frac{\eta \cdot 10^{-8}}{10^{-8} + 10^{-8}} = \frac{\eta}{2}

In the week chapter’s example the gradient was much larger than \epsilon, so \epsilon could be dropped and the first step was about \eta for any gradient. Here the gradient is the same size as \epsilon, which is no longer negligible, and the step is halved. For a gradient much smaller than \epsilon the step shrinks toward 0. That is the job \epsilon was introduced for: it stops a near-zero gradient from being blown up into a full-size step.

Answer: 0.5.

Part 4: Testing Homework 4

Context for these questions

Homework 4 implements the week’s optimizers as code and adds three pieces that the lecture does not cover in detail. The problems below test each idea on invented setups. None of them reproduce the homework’s function, networks, or hyperparameter searches.

An optimizer with state. An optimizer is an object with a step method that takes the current parameters and their gradients and returns updated parameters. Momentum and Adam must remember their accumulators between calls, so they keep a dictionary self.state. When a network’s parameters arrive as a list of arrays, such as [W0, W1, b0, b1], the optimizer keeps one state entry per array, matched by position in the list, and each entry has the same shape as its array. Plain SGD needs no state.

Notation. The homework writes Adam’s two moments as m (first) and v (second). This chapter keeps the week chapter’s names: v for the first moment and s for the second. Only the letters differ.

AdamW. Weight decay from Week 3 shrinks the weights a little at every step. AdamW applies it after Adam’s normalization, as a separate term in the update. Written in this chapter’s notation:

\bm{\theta}_t \leftarrow \bm{\theta}_{t-1} - \eta\left(\frac{\hat{\bm{v}}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{\bm{s}}_t} + \epsilon} + \lambda\,\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\right)

where \lambda is the weight decay coefficient. The alternative is to add the decay term \lambda\bm{\theta} to the gradient before it enters Adam’s moving averages. Problem 11 shows why the two are different.

Glorot uniform initialization. Week 3 drew weights from a normal distribution with variance 2/(n_\text{in} + n_\text{out}). The homework instead draws them from a continuous uniform distribution on [-a,\ a], with

a = \sqrt{\frac{6}{n_\text{in} + n_\text{out}}}

A uniform variable on [-a,\ a] has mean 0 and variance a^2/3. Problem 12 checks that this choice of a gives the same variance as Week 3’s Glorot rule.

Problem 10: Where plain gradient descent starts to diverge

Plain gradient descent minimizes f(x, y) = 3x^2 + 15y^2 from a start where both x and y are nonzero. It converges to (0, 0) for every learning rate below some threshold and fails to converge at or above it. What is the threshold?

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 4, Descending in a valley and Problem 2 above.

The coordinates update separately. The gradient is (6x,\ 30y), so one step is

x \leftarrow (1 - 6\eta)\,x, \qquad y \leftarrow (1 - 30\eta)\,y

Each coordinate is multiplied by its own factor on every step. It shrinks toward 0 when the factor’s size is below 1.

Each direction’s limit. For x we need |1 - 6\eta| < 1, so \eta < 2/6 = 1/3. For y we need |1 - 30\eta| < 1, so \eta < 2/30 = 1/15. Both must hold, and the steep direction sets the stricter limit. At exactly \eta = 1/15 the factor for y is -1, so y flips sign forever without shrinking.

The cost. Just below the threshold the factor for x is about 1 - 6/15 = 0.6, so x converges, but no larger \eta is available to speed it up. The gentle direction is held back by the steep one: the valley problem from the lecture, and the reason the homework moves on to momentum and Adam.

Answer: 1/15 \approx 0.067.

Problem 11: Weight decay before or after Adam’s normalization

A parameter starts at \theta_0 = 3 with fresh Adam state (t = 1). Its loss gradient on this step is g_1 = 0.4. Use \eta = 0.1, a decay coefficient \lambda = 0.5, and \epsilon = 0. Compare two versions:

  • (A) Add \lambda\theta_0 to the gradient, then take an ordinary Adam step on the result.
  • (B) AdamW: take the Adam step on g_1 alone and add \lambda\theta_0 after the normalization, as in the context section.

How many times larger is the move in version (B) than in version (A)?

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 4, The full algorithm and the AdamW update in the context section.

A fact about Adam’s first step. With \epsilon = 0, bias correction gives \hat{v}_1 = g and \sqrt{\hat{s}_1} = |g| for whatever gradient g goes in, so the normalized term is g/|g| = \pm 1. The first step has size \eta whatever the size of g.

(A) Decay inside the gradient. The gradient fed to Adam is 0.4 + 0.5 \cdot 3 = 1.9. Adam normalizes it to +1, so the move is \eta \cdot 1 = 0.1 and \theta_1 = 2.9. The decay term made the gradient almost five times larger, and the normalization erased that.

(B) AdamW. The normalized term is 0.4/0.4 = 1, and the decay term is added afterwards:

\theta_1 = 3 - 0.1\,(1 + 0.5 \cdot 3) = 3 - 0.25 = 2.75

The move is 0.25.

The ratio is 0.25/0.1 = 2.5. In version (A), Adam’s division by the typical gradient size also divides away the weight decay, so large weights are not shrunk any harder than small ones. AdamW keeps the decay outside the normalization, so a weight is pulled toward 0 in proportion to its size, as in Week 3’s weight decay.

Answer: 2.5.

Problem 12: The Glorot uniform limit

A dense layer maps n_\text{in} = 100 inputs to n_\text{out} = 50 outputs and uses Glorot uniform initialization, W_{ij} \sim \text{Uniform}[-a,\ a] with a = \sqrt{6/(n_\text{in} + n_\text{out})}. What is a?

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 3, Parameter Initialization and the context section above.

The limit.

a = \sqrt{\frac{6}{100 + 50}} = \sqrt{0.04} = 0.2

Why a 6 and not a 2. The rule’s goal is a weight variance of 2/(n_\text{in} + n_\text{out}), the same as Week 3’s normal version. A uniform variable on [-a,\ a] has variance a^2/3, so here

\frac{a^2}{3} = \frac{0.04}{3} = \frac{2}{150} = \frac{2}{n_\text{in} + n_\text{out}}

Matching a^2/3 to 2/(n_\text{in} + n_\text{out}) forces a^2 = 6/(n_\text{in} + n_\text{out}). The 6 is the 2 of Glorot’s variance times the 3 from the uniform distribution. The two versions differ in the shape of the distribution, not in its spread.

Answer: 0.2.

Problem 13: How much state does Adam keep?

An MLP has layer widths [3, 8, 4]: two dense layers, each with a weight matrix and a bias vector. Its parameters are passed to the optimizer as a list of arrays, [W0, W1, b0, b1]. After the first call to step, how many numbers in total are stored in an Adam optimizer’s self.state?

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 4, The memory cost and the optimizer-state paragraph in the context section.

Count the parameters. A dense layer from n_\text{in} to n_\text{out} has n_\text{in} n_\text{out} weights and n_\text{out} biases.

Array Shape Numbers
W0 8 \times 3 24
W1 4 \times 8 32
b0 8 8
b1 4 4
Total 68

Count the state. self.state has one entry per array in the list, four in all, keyed by position. Each Adam entry holds two arrays of the same shape as its parameter, the first moment and the second moment. So Adam stores 2 \cdot 68 = 136 numbers.

For comparison, momentum keeps one accumulator per parameter (68 numbers) and plain SGD keeps none (0). This is the week chapter’s memory point on a small network: momentum doubles the stored state and Adam triples it.

Answer: 136.

Problem 14: Why identical weights stay identical

A classmate initializes every weight of a two-class MLP to the same value 1 and every bias to 0. They hope a strong optimizer like Adam will sort things out. Their loss curve is smooth and flat at about 0.693. Explain what went wrong, and what the value 0.693 tells us.

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 3, Parameter Initialization and Week 4, The full algorithm.

Identical units. In a hidden layer, every unit has the same incoming weights and the same bias, so every unit computes the same pre-activation and the same activation on every example. The units differ only in their names.

Identical gradients. Each hidden unit also has the same outgoing weights, so backpropagation sends it the same error signal. Its incoming weights therefore get the same gradients as every other unit’s. After the update they are still equal to each other, and the argument repeats on the next step.

The optimizer cannot help. Momentum and Adam work entry by entry. Two weights with identical gradient histories have identical accumulators and take identical steps. A per-parameter learning rate gives the same value to parameters that look the same. The hidden layer behaves like a single unit for the whole run, however wide it is. Random initialization exists to break this symmetry before training starts.

What 0.693 means. With all weights equal, both output logits are equal too, so the softmax gives probability 1/2 to each class on every example. The cross-entropy of predicting 1/2 for the correct class is -\ln(1/2) = \ln 2 \approx 0.693. More generally, guessing uniformly among K classes gives \ln K. A loss curve sitting at that value is smooth because nothing is being learned. A low-variance loss curve is not evidence of training: compare it with the chance-level value first.

Part 5: Official Week 5 quiz practice

The four questions below were provided by the course as Week 5 quiz practice, and they test Week 4 material. They are reproduced with the course’s own numbers and answers, in increasing order of difficulty.

Quiz question 1: Updates scale with the feature (easy)

A linear model \hat{y} = w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 is trained with the squared error loss \mathcal{L} = \tfrac{1}{2}(y - \hat{y})^2 using vanilla gradient descent with a single learning rate. For the example \bm{x} = (12,\ 3), y = 2, and initial weights w_1 = w_2 = 0, how many times larger is the magnitude of the update to w_1 than the magnitude of the update to w_2 after one step?

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 4, A linear model with features on different scales.

  1. With w_1 = w_2 = 0, the prediction is \hat{y} = 0.
  2. The gradient for each weight is \dfrac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial w_i} = (\hat{y} - y)\,x_i = -2x_i.
  3. So \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial w_1 = -24 and \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial w_2 = -6.
  4. The update is \Delta w_i = -\eta\,\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial w_i, so |\Delta w_1| = 24\eta and |\Delta w_2| = 6\eta.
  5. The ratio is 24\eta / 6\eta = 12/3.

Both weights share the error factor, so the ratio of the updates is just the ratio of the features.

Answer: 4.

Quiz question 2: Momentum when the gradient reverses (medium)

A parameter is trained with the momentum optimizer (v_t \leftarrow \beta v_{t-1} + g_t, x_t \leftarrow x_{t-1} - \eta v_t) with \beta = 0.9 and \eta = \tfrac{1}{2}. The gradient has been equal to +1 for so long that the accumulator has stopped changing. On the next step the gradient suddenly becomes -1. By what magnitude does x move on that step?

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 4, Momentum, in particular the consistent-gradient worked example there.

The equations from class. Momentum’s two update rules, with the accumulator starting at v_0 = 0:

v_t \leftarrow \beta\,v_{t-1} + g_t, \qquad x_t \leftarrow x_{t-1} - \eta\,v_t

Step 1: what the accumulator is while the gradient is +1. The week chapter’s worked example runs exactly this case with \beta = 0.9 and g_t = 1 at every step:

Step t v_t = 0.9\,v_{t-1} + 1
1 1
2 0.9 \cdot 1 + 1 = 1.9
3 0.9 \cdot 1.9 + 1 = 2.71
4 0.9 \cdot 2.71 + 1 = 3.439

Unrolling the rule turns this into a geometric series:

v_t = 1 + \beta + \beta^2 + \cdots + \beta^{t-1} = \frac{1 - \beta^t}{1 - \beta}

Step 2: what “stopped changing” means. As t grows, \beta^t = 0.9^t shrinks toward 0, so v_t climbs toward

\frac{1 - 0}{1 - \beta} = \frac{1}{0.1} = 10

and each new step changes it by less. “The gradient has been +1 for so long that the accumulator has stopped changing” says we are at this limit: v_{t-1} = 10. (The quiz’s own solution calls this value v_\text{ss}, for steady state. The lecture does not use that name; it is just the value the geometric series approaches.)

A second way to find the same number: if the accumulator has stopped changing, then one more update with g = 1 leaves it where it was, so v_{t-1} must satisfy

v_{t-1} = \beta\,v_{t-1} + 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (1 - \beta)\,v_{t-1} = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_{t-1} = \frac{1}{0.1} = 10

Step 3: the accumulator on the step where the gradient flips. Now g_t = -1:

v_t = \beta\,v_{t-1} + g_t = 0.9 \cdot 10 + (-1) = 9 - 1 = 8

The old value is shrunk by \beta and the new gradient is added, but one gradient of size 1 cannot cancel an accumulated 9.

Step 4: the move. The change in x on this step is

x_t - x_{t-1} = -\eta\,v_t = -\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 8 = -4

What “magnitude” asks for. The question asks for the size of the move, |x_t - x_{t-1}| = |-\eta\,v_t|, not the signed change -\eta\,v_t. Since \eta > 0, this is \eta\,|v_t| = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 8 = 4. The sign is still worth reading: x decreases by 4, the same direction it was moving while the gradient was +1. The new gradient of -1 on its own would say to increase x, but the accumulated momentum outweighs that one reversed gradient, so x keeps going the old way, only a little more slowly than the \eta \cdot 10 = 5 per step it was moving before.

Answer: 4.

Quiz question 3: Adam without bias correction (medium-hard)

A single parameter is trained with Adam using \beta_1 = 0.8, \beta_2 = 0.99, \epsilon = 0, and v_0 = s_0 = 0. On the first step the gradient is g_1 = 5. A student implements Adam but forgets the bias-correction step (that is, uses v_t and s_t directly instead of \hat{v}_t and \hat{s}_t). How many times larger is the first update than the first update of standard Adam?

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 4, The bias toward zero.

  1. The accumulators after one step:
    • v_1 = (1 - \beta_1)\,g_1 = 0.2 \cdot 5 = 1
    • s_1 = (1 - \beta_2)\,g_1^2 = 0.01 \cdot 25 = 0.25
  2. Standard Adam: \hat{v}_1 = 1/0.2 = 5 and \hat{s}_1 = 0.25/0.01 = 25, so the update is \eta \cdot 5/5 = \eta.
  3. No bias correction: the update is \eta \cdot 1/\sqrt{0.25} = 2\eta.
  4. The ratio is 2\eta/\eta = 2.

In general the ratio is (1 - \beta_1)/\sqrt{1 - \beta_2} = 0.2/0.1 = 2, whatever the gradient. The second moment is biased more strongly than the first, so the uncorrected first step comes out too large, as in the week chapter’s worked example.

Answer: 2.

Quiz question 4: SGD noise at the minimum (hard)

A training set has four examples with per-example losses \mathcal{L}_i(\theta) = \tfrac{1}{2}(\theta - a_i)^2, where (a_1, a_2, a_3, a_4) = (1,\ 3,\ 5,\ 7), and the full training loss is their average. Mini-batch SGD with learning rate \eta = 2 and mini-batch size B = 5 (examples sampled independently and uniformly with replacement) is started exactly at the minimizer \theta^* of the full training loss. After one step, what is the expected value of (\theta_1 - \theta^*)^2?

TipWorked solution

This combines three Week 4 equations with the expectation and variance rules from the Week 3 practice chapter. The plan: find \theta^*, write the one step as \theta^* minus \eta times a random mini-batch gradient, then find the average of that gradient’s square.

The equations used.

# Equation Source
E1 \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}_i(\theta) (training loss is the average) Week 4, SGD
E2 \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\dfrac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_{i_b}(\theta) (mini-batch update) Week 4, Mini-batches
E3 \mathbb{E}\big[\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_i(\theta)\big] = \nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}(\theta) for i uniform (unbiased gradient) Week 4, SGD
R1 \operatorname{Var}[X] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2, so \mathbb{E}[X^2] = \operatorname{Var}[X] + (\mathbb{E}[X])^2 Week 3 practice, rule 1
R2 \operatorname{Var}[cX] = c^2\operatorname{Var}[X] and \mathbb{E}[cX] = c\,\mathbb{E}[X] Week 3 practice, rule 2 and expectation rules
R3 \operatorname{Var}[X + Y] = \operatorname{Var}[X] + \operatorname{Var}[Y] for independent X, Y Week 3 practice, rule 3

Here i_b is the index of the b-th example drawn into the mini-batch. The loss is a function of one number \theta, so every gradient is an ordinary derivative.

E1 is not specific to SGD. It defines the training loss, the function every optimizer this week is trying to minimize: full-batch gradient descent, SGD, mini-batch SGD, momentum, and Adam all share it. They differ only in how they estimate its gradient (all n examples, one, or B) and in what they do with that estimate (step directly, accumulate it, or normalize it). Some texts use the sum instead of the average; that multiplies every gradient by n, which a learning rate n times smaller would cancel.

Step 1: the per-example gradient. By the chain rule (the same calculation as the squared error in Week 1), the derivative of \tfrac{1}{2}(\theta - a_i)^2 is

\frac{d\mathcal{L}_i}{d\theta} = \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 2(\theta - a_i)\cdot 1 = \theta - a_i

Step 2: the minimizer \theta^*. By E1 the training loss is the average of the four per-example losses, so its derivative is the average of the four derivatives:

\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{d\theta} = \frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{4}(\theta - a_i) = \theta - \frac{a_1 + a_2 + a_3 + a_4}{4}

The minimum is where this is 0, so \theta^* is the mean of the a_i:

\theta^* = \frac{1 + 3 + 5 + 7}{4} = 4

(The loss is a bowl with second derivative 1 > 0, so this point is a minimum.)

Step 3: the per-example gradients at \theta^*. Using Step 1 with \theta = 4:

Example i a_i Gradient \theta^* - a_i
1 1 3
2 3 1
3 5 -1
4 7 -3

They add to 0, as they must: their average is the full gradient, and the full gradient is 0 at the minimizer. But no single one is 0. That is the whole source of the answer.

Step 4: write the step as a random quantity. One draw picks an example uniformly at random, so its gradient g is a random variable taking the values 3, 1, -1, -3, each with probability 1/4. The mini-batch draws B = 5 of them, g_1, \ldots, g_5. “With replacement” means each draw is made from all four examples regardless of earlier draws, so the five draws are independent and each has the same distribution as g. Their average is the mini-batch gradient

\bar{g} = \frac{1}{5}\sum_{b=1}^{5} g_b

By E2, starting at \theta_0 = \theta^*, one step gives \theta_1 = \theta^* - \eta\,\bar{g}. So the quantity the question asks about is

(\theta_1 - \theta^*)^2 = (-\eta\,\bar{g})^2 = \eta^2\,\bar{g}^2

The randomness is only in which examples were drawn, and the question asks for the average of this over all possible mini-batches.

Step 5: mean and variance of one draw. From Step 3, one draw gives g one of the four values 3,\ 1,\ -1,\ -3, and each example is picked with the same probability 1/4. The expected value of a quantity picked uniformly from a list is just the average of the list:

\mathbb{E}[g] = \frac{3 + 1 + (-1) + (-3)}{4} = 0

\mathbb{E}[g^2] works the same way. Squaring g squares whichever value was picked, so g^2 takes the values 9,\ 1,\ 1,\ 9, again each with probability 1/4, and its expected value is their average:

\mathbb{E}[g^2] = \frac{9 + 1 + 1 + 9}{4} = 5

By R1, \operatorname{Var}[g] = \mathbb{E}[g^2] - (\mathbb{E}[g])^2 = 5 - 0 = 5.

Step 6: mean and variance of the mini-batch average. Write the mini-batch gradient as the sum of the five draws divided by 5. Call the sum S:

S = g_1 + g_2 + g_3 + g_4 + g_5, \qquad \bar{g} = \tfrac{1}{5}\,S

Each draw g_b is a copy of the single draw from Step 5, so every one has \mathbb{E}[g_b] = 0 and \operatorname{Var}[g_b] = 5.

6a. The mean. The expectation rules from the Week 3 practice chapter say that constants come out and sums split, always (no independence needed):

\mathbb{E}[aX + bY] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X] + b\,\mathbb{E}[Y]

Applied to \bar{g}, with a = 1/5:

\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\bar{g}] &= \tfrac{1}{5}\big(\mathbb{E}[g_1] + \mathbb{E}[g_2] + \mathbb{E}[g_3] + \mathbb{E}[g_4] + \mathbb{E}[g_5]\big) \\ &= \tfrac{1}{5}(0 + 0 + 0 + 0 + 0) = 0 \end{aligned}

6b. The variance of the sum. R3 says that for independent X and Y,

\operatorname{Var}[X + Y] = \operatorname{Var}[X] + \operatorname{Var}[Y]

Applying it one term at a time extends it to five independent terms. The draws are independent because they are made with replacement (Step 4), so

\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}[S] &= \operatorname{Var}[g_1] + \operatorname{Var}[g_2] + \operatorname{Var}[g_3] + \operatorname{Var}[g_4] + \operatorname{Var}[g_5] \\ &= 5 + 5 + 5 + 5 + 5 = 25 \end{aligned}

Adding draws makes the sum more spread out, not less: the noise of five draws piles up.

6c. Dividing by 5. R2 says a constant factor comes out of a variance squared:

\operatorname{Var}[cX] = c^2\operatorname{Var}[X]

Here c = 1/5 and X = S:

\operatorname{Var}[\bar{g}] = \operatorname{Var}\big[\tfrac{1}{5}S\big] = \left(\tfrac{1}{5}\right)^2 \operatorname{Var}[S] = \frac{1}{25}\cdot 25 = 1

The sum’s variance grew by a factor of 5, but dividing by 5 shrinks the variance by a factor of 5^2 = 25, so the average ends up 5 times less noisy than a single draw.

6d. The general rule. The same three lines with B draws, each of variance \operatorname{Var}[g], give

\operatorname{Var}[\bar{g}] = \left(\tfrac{1}{B}\right)^2 \cdot B\operatorname{Var}[g] = \frac{\operatorname{Var}[g]}{B}

which is the rule on the equation sheet: here 5/5 = 1.

Step 7: put it together. \eta^2 = 4 is a constant, so it comes outside the expectation (R2). Then R1, applied to \bar{g}, turns \mathbb{E}[\bar{g}^2] into the variance plus the squared mean:

\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\big[(\theta_1 - \theta^*)^2\big] &= \eta^2\,\mathbb{E}[\bar{g}^2] \\ &= \eta^2\left(\operatorname{Var}[\bar{g}] + (\mathbb{E}[\bar{g}])^2\right) \\ &= 4\,(1 + 0^2) \end{aligned}

Answer: 4.

Key takeaways: what this question tests.

  • SGD’s gradient is unbiased but noisy. On average a mini-batch gradient equals the full gradient (Steps 5 and 6a). That does not make any single mini-batch gradient correct: at the minimum the full gradient is 0, but each per-example gradient is not (Step 3).
  • So SGD cannot sit still at the minimum. Full-batch gradient descent started at \theta^* would not move at all. Mini-batch SGD moves anyway, here landing a squared distance of 4 from the minimum on average, a typical distance of about 2. This is the stalling and jittering from The Noise in SGD, measured as a number.
  • The two knobs, and how strongly each one acts. The expected squared distance is \eta^2\operatorname{Var}[g]/B. Doubling B halves it; halving \eta quarters it. This is why mini-batches reduce the noise but do not remove it, and why learning rate schedules lower \eta late in training.
  • The probability skills behind it. The expected value of a uniformly picked value is the average of the list; \mathbb{E}[X^2] = \operatorname{Var}[X] + (\mathbb{E}[X])^2; and the variance of an average of B independent draws is the single-draw variance divided by B. “With replacement” is the detail in the question that makes the draws independent, so that last rule applies.

Part 6: Conceptual questions

These have no numeric answer. Try answering in two or three sentences before opening the solution.

Conceptual 1: Why SGD stalls, and what a schedule fixes

A training curve under SGD with a constant learning rate falls quickly, then flattens out and jitters well above the lowest loss the model could reach. Explain why, and explain what lowering the learning rate late in training does and does not fix.

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 4, The Noise in SGD and Learning rate schedules.

Each SGD step follows one example’s (or one mini-batch’s) gradient, and each example’s loss has its own minimum. Far from the minimum of the average, the per-example gradients mostly agree on which way is downhill, so progress is fast. Near it they disagree: at the minimum of the average their mean is 0, but each one is not (Quiz question 4). So every step still moves the parameters by about \eta times a nonzero gradient, and they bounce around the minimum instead of settling. The jitter is proportional to \eta.

Lowering \eta late in training shrinks those bounces, so the parameters settle closer to the minimum. It does not remove the noise itself: the per-example gradients still disagree. And lowering \eta too early slows the fast first phase of training. A larger mini-batch is the other remedy. It cuts the noise’s standard deviation by \sqrt{B} (Problem 5), and only removes it entirely when the mini-batch is the whole training set.

Conceptual 2: Adam with no memory

Suppose we run Adam with \beta_1 = \beta_2 = 0, so it keeps no memory of past gradients. What does each update reduce to, and what does that reveal about the role of the denominator \sqrt{\hat{\bm{s}}_t}?

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 4, The full algorithm.

With \beta_1 = \beta_2 = 0, the accumulators are just the current gradient and its square: \bm{v}_t = \bm{g}_t and \bm{s}_t = \bm{g}_t^2. The bias corrections divide by 1 - 0^t = 1, so they do nothing. Ignoring the small \epsilon, each entry’s update is

\frac{\eta\,g_t}{\sqrt{g_t^2}} = \frac{\eta\,g_t}{|g_t|} = \pm\eta

Every parameter moves by exactly \eta per step, in the downhill direction, whatever the size of its gradient. Only the sign of the gradient is used.

This isolates the denominator’s job. Dividing by the typical gradient size throws away the gradient’s scale, which is how one global \eta can suit parameters whose gradients differ by orders of magnitude. It also shows why the moving averages matter. With no memory, the step stays \eta even as the gradient shrinks toward 0 near a minimum, so the parameters keep bouncing by \pm\eta. With \beta_1 and \beta_2 close to 1, \hat{\bm{v}}_t averages out gradients that keep flipping sign, while \hat{\bm{s}}_t still remembers their size, so the step shrinks when the gradient keeps reversing.
