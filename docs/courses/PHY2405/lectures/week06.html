Week 6: Tracking Detectors

Week 6: Tracking Detectors

Overview. A tracker reconstructs the path of a charged particle from a series of position measurements, and from the curvature of that path in a magnetic field it obtains the particle’s momentum. Week 5 set up the tension: a tracker wants the minimal interaction with the particle, enough ionization to see where it went but as little material as possible, because material deflects the track through multiple scattering.

This week follows the handwritten notes Tracking Detectors in order. Most of the deck is about gas detectors. We follow the charges a track leaves in a gas: how the freed electrons and ions drift and diffuse, how an avalanche near a thin wire multiplies them, and how the moving charges make an electrical signal. We then build detectors from those pieces (multiwire proportional chambers, drift chambers, time projection chambers) and finish with what a complete tracker delivers: the momentum resolution, the resolution on the impact parameter, and the measured performance of the DELPHI and ATLAS trackers.

Three Week 5 ideas return throughout. Primary ionization (a few tens of ionizing encounters per cm of argon) sets how many charges a track leaves. Landau fluctuations make the amount of charge vary from track to track. Multiple scattering, with width \theta_0 \approx (13.6\ \text{MeV}/\beta cp)\sqrt{x/X_0}, limits how well a track can be followed through material.

The Third Problem Set leans on this week: item 1 on the time development of the signal and timing, item 2 on drift and diffusion in a drift cell, and item 3 on projecting a track back to the beam line. These notes explain the concepts those items depend on. The specific calculations are left to the assignment.

Several pages of the notes are marked Skip in 2024 by the instructor. They are kept here as two shorter sections, labelled as background, because later pages (stereo layers, the time projection chamber, the DELPHI tracker) refer to them.

What a tracker needs

What should a detector that measures trajectories look like? The notes state the objective directly: observe the trajectory with as little interference as possible. That means a thin detector, measured in radiation lengths, with enough points along the path to fit a curve.

Typical position resolutions for the three main technologies:

Technology Typical resolution \sigma
Scintillator strips or fibres about 1 cm
Gas detectors 1 mm or better
Silicon sensors about 10 μm

In this chapter resolution without a qualifier means the point resolution: the standard deviation of a single measured coordinate around the true crossing point. Later sections introduce the momentum resolution and the impact-parameter resolution, and say so explicitly.

Gas detectors are cheap per unit volume and contain very little material, which is why the outer, large-volume parts of trackers have long been gaseous. The deck covers three kinds: the multiwire proportional chamber, the drift chamber, and the time projection chamber. Real experiments often combine several, as the DELPHI tracker does.

The wire chamber and its three stages

The basic gas detector is a wire chamber: a gas volume, usually argon-based, enclosed by an envelope, with cathode surfaces held at one potential and thin anode wires at another. The cathodes shape the electric field so that it points everywhere towards an anode.

Note

Figure 1. The three stages in a wire chamber (schematic, not to scale). The track frees electron-ion pairs along its path (blue dots); the electrons drift along the field towards the anode wire; within a few wire radii of the wire they multiply in an avalanche.

The notes divide what happens into three stages:

  1. Ionization. The track loses energy by dE/dx (Week 5), freeing a few tens of primary electrons per cm of argon, each with some secondaries (the W value). A MIP crossing 1 cm of argon leaves roughly 100 electrons in total.
  2. Drift. The electrons move towards the anode and the positive ions towards the cathode. Each charge performs a random walk that the field gives a direction.
  3. Amplification. About 100 electrons is far too small a charge to read out directly. Close to a thin wire the field is strong enough that each electron gains enough energy between collisions to ionize again, starting an avalanche. The lecture quotes gains up to about 10^7.

A drift chamber adds one more measurement: the time between the particle’s passage (a “start” from an external trigger) and the arrival of the electrons at the wire (the “stop”). That time gives the distance from the wire. Diffusion during the drift spreads the electron cloud and limits how precise that distance can be. The next sections take the three stages in turn.

Drift and diffusion

Diffusion without a field

How far does a cloud of charges spread while it travels? Start with no field at all. Each electron or ion collides constantly with gas molecules and changes direction at random. After many collisions, the charges deposited at one point spread out as a Gaussian.

Definition. Diffusion is the spreading of a group of particles by random thermal collisions. In one dimension, the fraction of charges found between x and x+dx after time t is \frac{dN}{N} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{x^2}{4Dt}\right)dx, where D is the diffusion coefficient of that species in that gas (units of area per time).

The handwritten slide writes the prefactor as 1/(4\pi Dt); the square root is needed for the distribution to integrate to one. Comparing with a Gaussian of standard deviation \sigma, \exp(-x^2/2\sigma^2), we read off

\sigma_x^2 = 2Dt .

The width grows as the square root of time, the signature of a random walk: the spread after many independent steps grows with the square root of their number. The same holds along each axis, so the cloud stays round in the absence of a field; the slide’s plot of ions diffusing in air at 0.1 s, 1 s and 10 s shows this growth.

Drift in an electric field

Collisions with the gas are frequent. For thermal motion the notes quote speeds of 5\times10^4–2\times10^5 cm/s for ions and about 10^7 cm/s for electrons. Apply an electric field and, between collisions, each charge accelerates along the field. Averaged over many collisions this adds a small net velocity to the random motion.

Definition. The drift velocity w = dx/dt is the average velocity of a charge along the field, much smaller than its random thermal speed. For ions it is proportional to the field divided by the gas pressure, w^+ = \mu^+\,\frac{E}{P}, where the mobility \mu^+ is a constant specific to the ion and the gas.

Here \mu is a mobility, not a muon, and P is the gas pressure, not a momentum. The lowercase w for drift velocity is unrelated to Week 5’s W value. The factor 1/P appears because doubling the pressure halves the distance between collisions, so the charge gains half as much energy from the field before each one. That is why drift properties are usually plotted against the reduced field E/P. The deck shows the random walk of a charge at E/P = 10, 100, 500 and 1500 (in V/(cm·atm)): the higher the reduced field, the more the walk is pulled into a straight line.

A typical ion mobility (argon ions in argon) is \mu^+ \approx 1.7 cm²/(V·s·atm). In a field of 1 kV/cm at 1 atm that gives w^+ \approx 1.7\times10^3 cm/s, or 17 μm per μs. Ions are slow, and this will dominate the shape of the signal.

Electrons are different. Their mobility is not constant, because the probability that an electron scatters off a gas atom depends strongly on its energy. In argon the electron-atom cross-section has a deep minimum near 0.3 eV, the Ramsauer-Townsend effect, varying between about 10^{-17} and 10^{-15} cm² over the relevant energies. (This \sigma is a collision cross-section, not a resolution.) An electron with energy near the minimum travels unusually far between collisions, so it diffuses more. Because the energy of the drifting electrons depends on the field and on what molecules they can lose energy to, the drift velocity is very sensitive to the gas: the deck shows that adding 0.1% or 1% of nitrogen to argon changes the electron drift velocity substantially.

Saturation. In some gases, raising the field raises the electrons’ energy into a region where the cross-section increases, which slows them back down. The drift velocity then rises, peaks, and levels off. The deck’s plot of drift velocity against field (in cm/μs against kV/cm) shows methane peaking near 10 cm/μs and ethylene levelling off near 5.5 cm/μs, with CO₂, N₂ and pure argon lower.

Definition. A gas has a saturated drift velocity when w is nearly independent of E over a range of fields. Small field variations across the cell (or small changes in voltage) then barely change w.

Saturation is what a drift chamber wants: if w is constant, the distance is simply x = w\,t, a linear distance-time relation that does not drift with small changes in conditions.

The diffusion limit

How precisely can the drift distance be known? A cloud that drifts a distance x spends time t = x/w in the gas, so by \sigma_x^2 = 2Dt

\sigma_x = \sqrt{\frac{2Dx}{w}} = \sigma(1\,\text{cm})\,\sqrt{x/1\,\text{cm}}, \qquad \sigma(1\,\text{cm}) = \sqrt{\frac{2D\cdot 1\,\text{cm}}{w}} .

The width after 1 cm of drift, \sigma(1\,\text{cm}), is the figure of merit for a drift gas: it combines how fast the electrons diffuse with how quickly they cross the cell. The spread for any other drift length then follows from the square root.

There is a floor. Write D and w in terms of the mobility, w = \mu E, and define the characteristic energy \epsilon_k = eD/\mu. Then

\sigma(1\,\text{cm}) = \sqrt{\frac{2\epsilon_k \cdot 1\,\text{cm}}{eE}} .

For electrons in thermal equilibrium with the gas, the Einstein relation gives \epsilon_k = kT \approx 0.025 eV at room temperature, the smallest diffusion possible at a given field (the thermal limit). Electrons in argon are far from thermal (their energy sits near the Ramsauer minimum), and the deck’s plot shows \sigma(1\,\text{cm}) \approx 200 μm over a wide range of fields. A “cool” gas such as CO₂, whose molecules absorb electron energy efficiently, stays close to the thermal limit and gives the best accuracy; the price is an unsaturated drift velocity, so the distance-time relation must be calibrated carefully.

Note

Worked example. Compare the thermal limit with argon at E = 1 kV/cm.

  1. Thermal limit: \sigma(1\,\text{cm})^2 = 2 \times 0.025\ \text{V} \times 1\ \text{cm} / (1000\ \text{V/cm}) = 5\times10^{-5} cm², so \sigma(1\,\text{cm}) = 7.1\times10^{-3} cm \approx 70 μm.
  2. Argon, from the deck’s plot: \sigma(1\,\text{cm}) \approx 200 μm, about three times the thermal limit.
  3. After a 4 cm drift in argon: \sigma_x = 200\ \text{μm} \times \sqrt{4} = 400 μm.

A drift of a few centimetres in a warm gas already costs several hundred micrometres, which is why long drifts need a cool gas or a strong field.

Third Problem Set item 2 asks for the spread as a function of drift distance in a specific cell, with its own diffusion coefficient and a field that changes with position. The method is the one above, applied piece by piece where w changes; the specific numbers are left to the assignment.

Avalanche multiplication

How does about 100 electrons become a signal we can read? Near the anode the field is strong enough that an electron gains more than the ionization energy between collisions and frees another electron; both then continue, and the number grows exponentially.

The notes start from the simplest model: a uniform field, and a constant probability of ionization per unit length.

Definition. The first Townsend coefficient \alpha is the number of ionizing collisions an electron makes per unit length of drift. Over a step dx, n electrons create dn = n\,\alpha\,dx new ones.

The slide calls \alpha a “mean free path” and a “doubling length”; strictly, \alpha is a number per unit length, so the mean distance between ionizing collisions is 1/\alpha (a mean free path in the Week 5 sense, applied to ionizing collisions of the drifting electron), and the doubling length is \ln 2/\alpha. Integrating dn = n\alpha\,dx:

n(x) = n_0\,e^{\alpha x}, \qquad M = \frac{n}{n_0} = e^{\alpha x} .

M is the gain (multiplication factor). Near a wire the field is not uniform, so \alpha depends on position, and the gain becomes

M = \exp\left(\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\alpha(x)\,dx\right).

The exponential is why gas gain is so sensitive to voltage: a small change in field changes \alpha everywhere along the path, and that change is exponentiated. This process is also called a Townsend avalanche.

Two features of the avalanche matter later:

  • Shape. Electrons are fast and ions slow, so the electrons race ahead to the wire while the ions stay almost where they were made. The avalanche has a teardrop shape, with the electrons at the front.
  • Location. Because the number doubles at each step, half of all the charges are created in the last step before the wire. The avalanche happens within a few micrometres of the wire surface.

The proportional tube

Where does a strong enough field come from? From a thin wire. The simplest example in the notes is a thin anode wire of radius a along the axis of a grounded conducting cylinder of radius b, with the wire at voltage V_0. Gauss’s law for a line charge gives, for a < r < b,

E(r) = \frac{CV_0}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\,\frac{1}{r}, \qquad V(r) = \frac{CV_0}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\,\ln\frac{r}{a}, \qquad C = \frac{2\pi\epsilon_0}{\ln(b/a)} .

Here C is the capacitance per unit length of the tube and V(r) is the potential difference from the wire surface. The field rises as 1/r, so with a wire radius of 10–50 μm it becomes very large in a thin layer around the wire, while staying modest across most of the tube where the electrons drift.

Note

Worked example. The deck’s tube: a = 10 μm, b = 8 mm, V_0 = 3 kV.

  1. \ln(b/a) = \ln 800 = 6.68, so C = 2\pi \times 8.85\times10^{-12} / 6.68 = 8.3 pF/m (the deck rounds to 8 pF/m).
  2. Since CV_0/2\pi\epsilon_0 = V_0/\ln(b/a) = 449 V, the field is E(r) = 449\ \text{V}/r.
  3. At the wire surface: 449/(10^{-3}\ \text{cm}) = 4.5\times10^5 V/cm.
  4. At the outer wall: 449/(0.8\ \text{cm}) = 560 V/cm.

The field varies by a factor of 800 across the tube: gentle drift over most of the volume and intense multiplication in the last few micrometres.

Definition. A proportional counter (proportional tube or chamber) is operated at a gain where every avalanche is independent, so the output pulse is proportional to the number of primary electrons, and hence to the energy deposited.

How the signal forms

When does the wire see a signal: when the electrons arrive, or before? The notes show the answer is: from the moment charges start to move, and mostly from the ions.

At low voltage (no gain) the picture is simple: n primary electrons collected on the wire give V = q/C = ne/C. At high voltage the avalanche wraps around the wire by diffusion; its electrons are collected within about 1 ns, while its ions drift outwards slowly, with w^+ = \mu E/P. Since E \propto 1/r, the ions slow down as they move away from the wire.

The notes use an energy argument. The tube is a capacitor of length l and total capacitance lC, charged to V_0, storing energy \tfrac12 lCV_0^2. A charge Q moving through a potential difference dV takes energy Q\,dV from the field, and the voltage on the wire changes by

dv = \frac{Q}{lCV_0}\,\frac{dV}{dr}\,dr .

The signal is induced by the motion of charge, not by its arrival. (The general statement of this idea is the Shockley-Ramo theorem.)

Suppose the avalanche charge is created within a distance \lambda of the wire surface (here \lambda is a distance, not a mean free path). The electrons move from a+\lambda to a; the ions move from a+\lambda out to b. Integrating dv with dV/dr = (CV_0/2\pi\epsilon_0)/r:

\begin{aligned} V^- &= -\frac{Q}{2\pi\epsilon_0 l}\,\ln\frac{a+\lambda}{a}, \\ V^+ &= -\frac{Q}{2\pi\epsilon_0 l}\,\ln\frac{b}{a+\lambda}, \\ V^- + V^+ &= -\frac{Q}{2\pi\epsilon_0 l}\,\ln\frac{b}{a} = -\frac{Q}{lC} . \end{aligned}

The total is Q/(lC), as it must be for a charge Q on a capacitance lC. What matters is how it is shared.

Note

Worked example. With the deck’s values a = 10 μm, \lambda = 1 μm, b = 10 mm:

  1. Electrons: \ln(11/10) = 0.095.
  2. Ions: \ln(10\,000/11) = 6.81.
  3. Ratio: V^-/V^+ = 0.095/6.81 = 1.4\%.

The electrons, created right next to the wire, cross almost no potential difference. About 99% of the signal comes from the ions moving away.

The time development of the pulse

If the signal comes from the slow ions, how long does it take, and do we have to wait for all of it? Consider the ions alone, starting at the wire surface at t = 0. The signal when they have reached radius r(t) is

V(t) = -\frac{Q}{2\pi\epsilon_0 l}\,\ln\frac{r(t)}{a} .

The ions move with dr/dt = w^+ = (\mu/P)\,E(r) = (\mu/P)(CV_0/2\pi\epsilon_0)(1/r). Separating variables, r\,dr = (\mu CV_0/2\pi\epsilon_0 P)\,dt, and integrating from a:

\begin{aligned} \frac{r^2}{2} - \frac{a^2}{2} &= \frac{\mu CV_0}{2\pi\epsilon_0 P}\,t \\ \Longrightarrow\quad r(t) &= \left(a^2 + \frac{\mu CV_0}{\pi\epsilon_0 P}\,t\right)^{1/2} . \end{aligned}

Substituting into V(t):

V(t) = -\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 l}\,\ln\!\left(1 + \frac{t}{t_0}\right), \qquad t_0 = \frac{\pi\epsilon_0 P a^2}{\mu CV_0} .

The signal grows logarithmically: very fast while the ions are in the strong field near the wire, then ever more slowly as they crawl outwards. The time constant t_0 is tiny (about 0.7 ns for the tube above).

The ions reach the cathode, r = b, after the total drift time

T = \frac{\pi\epsilon_0 P\,(b^2 - a^2)}{\mu CV_0} .

For the deck’s tube (a = 10 μm, b = 8 mm, C = 8 pF/m, \mu = 1.7 cm²/(V·s·atm) for argon, V_0 = 3 kV, P = 1 atm), inserting these values gives about 440 μs; the slide quotes 550 μs, and the difference does not affect the argument. Either way it is hundreds of microseconds, very long compared with the 22 μs between bunch crossings at LEP, and far longer than the 25 ns at the LHC.

Half the signal arrives very early. As a check, at t = T we have 1 + T/t_0 = b^2/a^2, so V(T) = -(Q/2\pi\epsilon_0 l)\ln(b/a), the full ion signal found above. The ratio of the signal at time t to its final value is

\frac{V(t)}{V(T)} = \frac{\ln(1 + t/t_0)}{\ln(b^2/a^2)} .

Setting this ratio to \tfrac12 gives 1 + t_{1/2}/t_0 = b/a. Using t_0 = T a^2/(b^2 - a^2):

t_{1/2} = t_0\left(\frac{b}{a} - 1\right) = T\,\frac{a}{a+b} .

For a = 10 μm and b = 10 mm, t_{1/2} \approx T/1000: half of the signal arrives in a thousandth of the total drift time, while the ions are still within the first factor of \sqrt{b/a} in radius. For the 8 mm tube, t_{1/2} = T/801, about half a microsecond.

Note

Figure 2. The ion-induced signal V(t)/V(T) = \ln(1+t/t_0)/\ln(b^2/a^2) for the 8 mm tube, plotted against time on a logarithmic axis. Each decade of time adds the same amount of signal; half of it is in by t_{1/2} \approx T/800.

Shaping the pulse

Waiting hundreds of microseconds is impossible at a collider, and unnecessary, since much of the signal arrives early. The readout instead keeps only the fast part, by pulse shaping: the wire is terminated so that the pulse decays with a short time constant.

The notes’ example is the wire terminated by a resistor, an RC circuit. A 1 m wire has C = 8 pF/m \times 1 m = 8 pF; with a 1 MΩ terminator, RC = 8 μs. Charge arriving more slowly than RC drains through the resistor before it can build up, so the measured pulse is roughly the signal collected within the first RC after the avalanche, and it returns to the baseline afterwards. A shorter RC gives a faster pulse, ready sooner for the next hit, at the cost of a smaller fraction of the full signal. The fraction kept follows from V(t)/V(T) above.

Third Problem Set item 1 builds on these two results (the long total drift time and the early half-signal). It asks how the fraction of the full amplitude depends on time and how well a pulse can be timed. Two ideas from outside the slides are needed:

  • Timing and position. In a drift chamber an uncertainty \sigma_t on the arrival time becomes a position uncertainty \sigma_x = w\,\sigma_t, so a required position resolution sets a required timing resolution.
  • Time slewing. A simple discriminator marks the time when the pulse crosses a fixed threshold. Because pulses from identical tracks differ in size (Landau fluctuations), a large pulse crosses the threshold earlier than a small one with the same start time. This amplitude-dependent shift is called time slewing. A constant-fraction discriminator removes most of it by triggering when the pulse reaches a fixed fraction of its own peak, which for pulses of the same shape happens at the same time whatever their size.

The specific fractions, timing resolutions and discriminator settings are left to the assignment.

Resolution and gas pressure

The deck closes the physics of drift with a measurement: the resolution of a drift chamber (σ in μm) against the distance x of the track from the wire, for a propane-ethylene mixture at 1, 2 and 4 bar. Two effects shape every curve.

  • Near the wire: primary-ionization statistics. A track leaves its ionization in a few tens of separate clusters per cm, at random positions (Week 5). The measured time comes from the cluster that arrives first, and for a track passing close to the wire the position of that cluster along the track fluctuates by an amount comparable to the distance itself. The resolution is poor close to the wire.
  • Far from the wire: diffusion. The longer the drift, the wider the cloud, growing as \sqrt{x} (the diffusion limit). The resolution worsens again at large x.

Raising the pressure helps both: more primary clusters per cm (the number scales with the gas density) and smaller diffusion. The best resolution lies at intermediate distances.

Background, skipped in 2024: operating a gas detector

The instructor marked the pages of this section Skip in 2024. They are summarized here because later sections mention quenchers and gating.

Operating regions. Plotting the logarithm of the gain against the high voltage shows five regions:

  1. Recombination: too little field, so ion pairs recombine before collection.
  2. Ion chamber: all primary charges collected, no gain.
  3. Proportional: gain up to about 10^4, pulse proportional to the primary charge.
  4. Limited streamer: large avalanches, pulse size no longer proportional.
  5. Geiger-Müller: photons emitted in the avalanche start new avalanches along the whole wire, giving a large, uniform pulse and a long dead time.

Choice of gas. Noble gases are used as the main component because they are not electronegative (they do not capture drifting electrons), have a large Townsend coefficient, and ionize well. Argon is much cheaper than krypton or xenon. Pure argon reaches gains of only 10^3–10^4: excited argon atoms emit 11 eV photons, which knock electrons out of the cathode (the work function of copper is about 8 eV) and set off a runaway discharge.

Quenchers. Adding a polyatomic gas fixes this. Molecules such as methane absorb photons in the 8–14 eV range and dissipate the energy in vibrations and dissociation. A standard mixture is argon-methane 80:20; argon-ethane is another.

Ageing. The dissociated quencher fragments can polymerize and deposit on the wires, reducing the gain. The notes quote failure once the total collected charge reaches about 1 C per cm of wire; additives such as methylal slow this down.

Space charge. At high rates the slow ions accumulate around the wire and screen its field, lowering the gain. The field is restored only once the ions have drifted away, hundreds of microseconds later, which is a real concern in high-rate environments such as CDF Run 2 and ATLAS. The remedies are lower gain, smaller gaps (shorter ion drift), and gating: a grid of wires whose voltage (±150 V in the ALEPH TPC) is switched to block ions from flowing back into the drift volume except when an event is wanted.

Multiwire proportional chambers

How do we cover a large area with position measurements? Put many anode wires side by side in one gas volume. The multiwire proportional chamber (MWPC) was introduced by Georges Charpak in the late 1960s and is used in fixed-target experiments, beam chambers, muon detectors and calorimeters. A typical configuration has anode wires of 20 μm diameter spaced 2 mm apart between two cathode planes; the field near each wire looks like that of a single tube, and, as the notes put it, they actually work.

Why use one shared volume instead of many separate tubes? Separate tubes have dead regions between them (inter-tube inefficiencies). A shared volume raises a different worry, cross-talk: the avalanche on one wire induces a negative signal on its neighbours through their mutual capacitance. In practice that is cancelled, because the positive ions moving away from the struck wire induce positive signals on the neighbouring wires.

An MWPC has no timing information in the sense of a drift chamber. The position is simply the struck wire, or the midpoint if two neighbouring wires fire.

Definition. The pitch s of a chamber is the spacing between neighbouring readout wires (or strips). For a detector that records only which wire was hit, the resolution is the rms of a uniform distribution of width s: \sigma = \frac{s}{\sqrt{12}} .

The \sqrt{12} comes from the variance of a uniform distribution: a track crossing anywhere in the cell of the struck wire, -s/2 < x < s/2, gives

\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{s}\int_{-s/2}^{s/2} x^2\,dx = \frac{s^2}{12} .

The error has a top-hat distribution, not a Gaussian, but its rms is still a useful number to combine with other errors. Pitch/\sqrt{12} is the standard benchmark for any binary-readout detector, wire or silicon.

Note

Figure 3. If only the struck wire (filled circle) is recorded, the true crossing point is anywhere within \pm s/2 with equal probability (blue). A Gaussian with the same rms, s/\sqrt{12} (pink), is narrower at the top and has tails the top-hat lacks.

Note

Worked example. For the typical 2 mm pitch: \sigma = 2\ \text{mm}/\sqrt{12} = 0.58 mm. A silicon strip sensor with 50 μm pitch and binary readout gives 50/\sqrt{12} = 14 μm, matching the “about 10 μm” quoted for silicon in the opening table.

Chamber systems and cathode readout

One plane of wires measures one coordinate. Two planes with crossed wires (an x plane and a y plane) give a point. With several tracks at once this becomes ambiguous: two tracks fire two x wires and two y wires, and the four possible pairings include two ghost points that no particle crossed. A third plane with wires at a different angle (a u or v plane) resolves this, because only real points line up in all three.

Segmented cathodes. The avalanche on an anode wire also induces positive pulses on the cathode. If the cathode is divided into strips, those strips measure the position along the wire. The induced charge spreads over several strips because the cathode sits farther from the wire than the wire spacing (cell thickness about four times the pitch, or the cathode 3–4 pitches away). Taking the centre of gravity of the charges on neighbouring strips locates the avalanche to a small fraction of the strip width, much better than pitch/\sqrt{12}.

Multiple tracks with cathode readout. The ghost problem returns when anode wires and cathode strips both record several hits. The notes give a neat fix: the anode signal and the cathode signal of the same avalanche share the same charge, so their pulse heights are correlated. Since Landau fluctuations make different tracks deposit quite different charges, matching pulse heights pairs each anode hit with its cathode hit. The method is limited by the signal-to-noise ratio of the readout.

Drift chambers

The MWPC uses only the identity of the hit wire. A drift chamber also measures when the electrons arrive. With the time of the particle’s passage known from an external trigger, the arrival time gives the drift distance, x = w\,t for a constant drift velocity. That either improves the resolution far beyond pitch/\sqrt{12} at the same wire spacing, or allows a much larger area per wire, and therefore fewer readout channels, at the same resolution.

A reliable distance-time relation needs a uniform drift field. Field-shaping wires (or electrodes) at graded potentials make the field in the cell nearly uniform, so that w is the same along the drift path. The uniformity breaks down near the anode, where the field turns into the 1/r amplification region, and over long drifts, where diffusion takes over.

The drift-time spectrum. If tracks cross the cell uniformly, the number of hits per unit drift distance, dN/dx, is constant. The number per unit drift time is then

\frac{dN}{dt} = \frac{dN}{dx}\,\frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{dN}{dx}\,w(x),

so the measured time spectrum is flat where the drift velocity is constant and changes shape wherever w does. Its endpoint is the maximum drift time. Third Problem Set item 2 asks for this spectrum in a cell where the field, and hence w, varies with position; that sketch is left to the assignment.

The left-right ambiguity

A single wire measures t = |x|/w: it tells us how far from the wire the track passed, but not on which side. The notes list three solutions.

  1. Staggered layers. Two layers of cells, offset by half a cell. For a track crossing at normal incidence, the two drift times sum to a constant, t_1 + t_2 = (\text{cell size})/w, and only the correct pair of sides is consistent with a straight line. The sum doubles as a calibration of w.
  2. Two anodes per cell separated by an isolation wire, so each side has its own anode. This doubles the electronics.
  3. Reading out the last field-shaping wires as well, which tells which side the charge came from at three times the electronics.

Jet chambers. JADE and OPAL used cells containing many anode wires in a plane, each wire slightly staggered alternately left and right of the plane. A real track gives hits that line up; its mirror image is broken up by the stagger, provided the left-right offset is larger than the position resolution.

Background, skipped in 2024: the third coordinate and the TPC

The instructor marked these pages Skip in 2024, with two exceptions noted below. The ideas are summarized because the DELPHI and ATLAS trackers use them.

Field-shaping problems. Practical drift chambers suffer from shorts or open connections in the resistor chain that sets the field-shaping voltages, from ion currents, and from the charging up of insulating surfaces, all of which distort the field and the distance-time relation.

Charge division. A drift cell measures the coordinate across the wires. To find the position z along a wire, make the wire resistive and read out both ends. The charge splits in inverse proportion to the resistance on each side, so the ratio Q_1/(Q_1+Q_2) gives z. The resolution is about 1% of the wire length or better: OPAL achieved 0.5%, about 1 cm on 2 m wires.

Stereo wires. The page on stereo layers is annotated with ATLAS-SCT, suggesting it is still referred to. Instead of measuring along a wire, tilt some layers by a small angle: layers at 0, +\theta, 0, -\theta. Comparing the coordinate measured by a tilted layer with that of an untilted one gives the position along the wire, with

\sigma_z \approx \frac{\sigma_{r\phi}}{\theta_{\text{stereo}}} .

With a few-degree stereo angle (6° is about 100 mrad), \sigma_z \approx 10\,\sigma_{r\phi}. CDF, ZEUS and the OPAL vertex chamber used this. The advantage is \sigma_z of 1 mm or better without charge measurement or fast timing; the disadvantage is harder pattern recognition, not available in the online trigger.

The time projection chamber. The time projection chamber (TPC), used by ALEPH and DELPHI and planned for the ILD detector, is a large gas volume with a central high-voltage membrane and readout planes on both endcaps. Electrons drift along the axis to the endcaps. The drift time gives z (and with it the polar angle \theta); wires and pads on the endplates give r and \phi. Each measured point is effectively a three-dimensional pixel, which makes pattern recognition straightforward at moderate track multiplicity.

The endplate still needs wires, for the avalanche, and extra wires give many independent samples of the track’s charge, a set of Landau-distributed measurements later used for particle identification. Pads are expensive: an N \times N grid of pads needs N^2 channels, against 2N for crossed wires, but only pads give true three-dimensional points. The deck notes that the data were buffered in CCDs.

Laser calibration. The drift-time relation of a TPC is calibrated with ultraviolet laser beams (a nitrogen laser, 330 nm, about 3 eV per photon). A laser track is straight, insensitive to the magnetic field, free of multiple scattering and Landau fluctuations, of adjustable intensity, and can test two-track resolution. One problem: 3 eV is below the gas ionization energy, so ionization comes from two-photon absorption or from trace impurities in the gas.

Measuring momentum

This page returns to the main thread (the instructor marked it Include in 2024). How do we turn a measured track into a momentum? Bend it in a magnetic field.

In a uniform field along the axis of a solenoid, the Lorentz force bends a charged particle into a helix. Its projection onto the plane transverse to the field is a circle of radius R_T, set by the transverse momentum p_T, the momentum component perpendicular to the field. If \lambda is the dip angle between the track and that transverse plane (here \lambda is an angle, not a distance), then p_T = p\cos\lambda, and for a particle of unit charge

p_T\,[\text{GeV}/c] = 0.3\,B\,[\text{T}]\;R_T\,[\text{m}] .

The 0.3 is the value of e c in these units (more precisely 0.2998). A 1 GeV/c track in a 1 T field curves with a radius of 3.3 m.

Over a measured track length L_T the circle turns through an angle \Delta\phi \approx L_T/R_T (the slide calls this \alpha; it is unrelated to the Townsend coefficient). The quantity actually measured is the sagitta.

Definition. The sagitta s of an arc is the distance between the midpoint of the arc and the midpoint of its chord. For a chord of length L_T on a circle of radius R_T \gg L_T, s \approx \frac{L_T^2}{8R_T} = \frac{0.3\,B\,L_T^2}{8\,p_T} .

The sagitta shrinks as 1/p_T, while the point resolution stays fixed. A stiff track is barely bent, so its momentum is harder to measure.

Note

Figure 4. In the plane transverse to a solenoid field, a track is an arc of radius R_T. Measuring points along a length L_T (blue) determines the sagitta s, and hence p_T. The curvature is exaggerated.

Note

Worked example. A tracker of length L_T = 1 m in B = 1 T.

  1. At p_T = 1 GeV/c: R_T = 1/0.3 = 3.33 m and s = 1^2/(8\times3.33) = 3.75 cm.
  2. At 10 GeV/c: s = 3.75 mm.
  3. At 100 GeV/c: s = 0.375 mm.

With a point resolution of 300 μm, the 1 GeV/c sagitta is measured to under 1%, but at 100 GeV/c the sagitta is barely larger than the measurement error.

Momentum resolution

The notes quote the standard result (R. L. Gluckstern, NIM 24 (1963) 381) for the momentum resolution \delta p/p, the fractional uncertainty on the measured momentum. It has three terms, added in quadrature:

\begin{aligned} \left(\frac{\delta p}{p}\right)^2 &= \left(\frac{\sigma_{r\phi}\,a_n\,p_T}{0.3\,B\,L_T^2}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\sigma_z\,f_n\,\sin\lambda\cos\lambda}{L_T}\right)^2 \\ &\quad + \left(\frac{0.053}{B\,\beta\cos\lambda\,\sqrt{L_T X_0}}\right)^2 . \end{aligned}

Lengths are in metres, B in tesla and p_T in GeV/c. The three terms are:

  1. Curvature measurement. The point resolution \sigma_{r\phi} in the bending plane, over the sagitta. It grows linearly with p_T, falls with the square of the length, and falls with B. Doubling the tracker length is worth four times as much as doubling the point resolution.
  2. Dip-angle measurement. The resolution \sigma_z along the field determines \lambda, which converts p_T to p. Since \sin\lambda\cos\lambda \le \tfrac12 and \sigma_z \lesssim 1 mm, this term is at most about 10^{-3} for a metre-long tracker and does not depend on momentum.
  3. Multiple scattering. Scattering in the tracker’s own gas and material bends the track at random, mimicking curvature. It is the Highland formula applied to the sagitta: the scattering angle and the curvature both scale as 1/p, so their ratio does not depend on momentum (except through \beta). X_0 here is the radiation length in metres of the tracker volume.

The coefficient a_n depends on the number n of equally spaced measurements:

a_n = \sqrt{\frac{720\,(n-1)^3}{(n-2)\,n\,(n+1)\,(n+2)}} \;\approx\; \sqrt{\frac{720}{n+4}} \quad (n > 10),

giving a_3 = 9.8, a_{12} = 6.6 and a_{100} = 2.6. More points help, but slowly, as 1/\sqrt{n}. If instead the points are clustered, n/2 at the middle and n/4 at each end, a'_n \approx \sqrt{256/n}, about 40% better, as long as multiple scattering is not a problem (the points at the ends are then far apart in material). For the dip term, f_n = \sqrt{12(n-1)/[n(n+1)]}, about 1 for n = 10 and 0.3 for n = 100.

Note

Worked example. The deck’s three-point tracker: L_T = 1 m, B = 1 T, n = 3, \sigma_{r\phi} = 300 μm.

\frac{\delta p_T}{p_T} = \frac{p_T}{0.3\times1}\times\frac{3\times10^{-4}\times9.8}{1^2} = 0.0098\,p_T \approx 1\%\ \text{per GeV}/c .

So 1% at 1 GeV/c, 10% at 10 GeV/c. The same result follows from the sagitta directly: the middle point’s error relative to the average of the two ends is \sqrt{3/2}\,\sigma, and dividing by s = 0.3BL_T^2/(8p_T) gives 8\sqrt{3/2} = 9.8, which is a_3.

The deck applies the first term to real detectors:

Tracker L_T (m) B (T) n \sigma_{r\phi} \delta p_T/p_T
Example above 1 1 3 300 μm 1\%\,p_T
CDF 1.35 1.4 100 300 μm 0.1\%\,p_T
DELPHI 1.5 1.2 20 150 μm 0.1\%\,p_T
ATLAS (projected) 1.1 2 10 (clustered) 15 μm 0.01\%\,p_T

(p_T in GeV/c.) The notes call the ATLAS figure optimistic. The handwritten ATLAS \sigma_{r\phi} could read 15 or 19 μm; 15 μm with a'_{10} = 5.0 reproduces the quoted 0.01%.

Multiple scattering decides the gas. Using standard radiation lengths at 20 °C and 1 atm, X_0 \approx 118 m for argon and about 5.7 km for helium (the handwritten values for some gases on the slide do not match the standard tables). In a 1 m, 1 T tracker for a relativistic track at \lambda = 0:

  • Argon: 0.053/\sqrt{1 \times 118} = 0.5\%.
  • Helium: 0.053/\sqrt{1 \times 5700} = 0.07\%; the lecture quotes about 0.1% for practical helium-based mixtures, which need a quencher.

Compare with the curvature term of a CDF-like tracker, 0.1\%\,p_T: for tracks of 0.1–1 GeV/c the scattering term in argon is larger. Experiments dominated by low-momentum tracks therefore chose helium-based gases; CLEO III and BaBar did.

Electron drift in electric and magnetic fields

A tracker sits inside a magnet, so its drifting electrons feel the magnetic field as well. Which way do they go? The notes write the average motion of an electron with a friction term for collisions:

\frac{\mathbf v_D}{\tau} + \frac{e}{m_e}\,\mathbf B\times\mathbf v_D = \frac{e}{m_e}\,\mathbf E,

where \tau is the mean time between collisions (the sign of the electron charge is absorbed into the directions). The solution is

\begin{aligned} \mathbf v_D = \frac{\mu E}{1+\omega^2\tau^2}\Big[\,&\hat{\mathbf E} + \omega\tau\,(\hat{\mathbf E}\times\hat{\mathbf B}) \\ &+ \omega^2\tau^2\,(\hat{\mathbf E}\cdot\hat{\mathbf B})\,\hat{\mathbf B}\Big], \end{aligned}

with \mu = e\tau/m_e the electron mobility and \omega = eB/m_e the cyclotron frequency. The product \omega\tau compares the time between collisions with the time to turn in the field:

  • \omega\tau \ll 1: the electron collides before it turns, and drifts along \mathbf E. Cool gases such as CO₂ behave this way.
  • \omega\tau \gg 1: the electron turns many times between collisions, and drifts along \mathbf B. Argon-methane is closer to this case.

Typical gases are in between. When \mathbf E \parallel \mathbf B, as in a TPC, all three terms point the same way and the drift is undisturbed.

When \mathbf E \perp \mathbf B, as in a drift chamber inside a solenoid, take \mathbf E along x:

v_x = \frac{\mu E}{1+\omega^2\tau^2}, \qquad v_y = -\frac{\mu E\,\omega\tau}{1+\omega^2\tau^2}, \qquad |\mathbf v_D| = \frac{\mu E}{\sqrt{1+\omega^2\tau^2}} .

Definition. The Lorentz angle \alpha_L is the angle between the electron drift direction and the electric field when \mathbf E \perp \mathbf B: \tan\alpha_L = \omega\tau, \qquad |\mathbf v_D| = \mu E\cos\alpha_L .

The electrons drift at an angle to the field lines and more slowly. Designers compensate by tilting the drift field in the cell, as the deck’s jet-cell figure shows, so that the electrons arrive along the intended path.

Impact-parameter resolution

The deck’s last physics page is about projecting a track back towards the collision point. Here the impact parameter has its tracking meaning: the distance of closest approach of the reconstructed track to the beam line (or to the primary vertex). This is not the Week 5 impact parameter, the closest distance between a target electron and a passing particle in a single collision. A large impact parameter is a sign that the particle came from the decay of a longer-lived particle, such as a b hadron, that flew away from the collision point before decaying.

How well can it be measured? Take the simplest case: a straight track measured at two radii, R_1 and R_2, with errors \sigma_1 and \sigma_2, extrapolated to r = 0. The extrapolated position is y_0 = (R_2 y_1 - R_1 y_2)/(R_2 - R_1), and propagating the errors:

\begin{aligned} \epsilon_v &= \sqrt{\left(\frac{R_1\,\sigma_2}{R_2-R_1}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{R_2\,\sigma_1}{R_2-R_1}\right)^2} \\ &= \frac{\sqrt{R_1^2+R_2^2}}{\Delta R}\,\sigma \qquad (\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = \sigma) . \end{aligned}

The error at the beam line is the point error magnified by the lever arm of the extrapolation. The lessons are direct: put the first layer as close to the beam as possible (small R_1), make \sigma small, and make R_2 large.

Note

Figure 5. A straight track measured at radii R_1 and R_2 (blue points with error bars) is extrapolated back to the beam line. Tilting the line within the point errors (dashed) moves its intercept by more than either point error; the shaded bar is the resulting impact-parameter error.

Note

Worked example. DELPHI’s silicon layers (below) sit at about R_1 = 6 cm and R_2 = 11 cm with \sigma \approx 8 μm.

  1. \sqrt{R_1^2 + R_2^2} = \sqrt{36 + 121} = 12.5 cm.
  2. \Delta R = 5 cm.
  3. \epsilon_v = (12.5/5) \times 8\ \text{μm} = 20 μm.

This matches DELPHI’s measured high-momentum impact-parameter resolution of 20 μm.

Multiple scattering adds a second term. The track scatters in the beam pipe and the first layer before the first measurement. That changes its direction by \theta_0, and the extrapolation over R_1 turns the angle into a displacement \theta_0 R_1. With the leading Highland term, \theta \approx (14/p\,[\text{GeV}])\sqrt{T/X_0} mrad for a thickness T; for a beam pipe plus first layer of 1% of a radiation length that is about 1.4 mrad/p, which the deck rounds to

\epsilon_v^{\text{scatter}} \approx \frac{1\ \text{mrad}}{p\,[\text{GeV}/c]}\times R_1 .

At 1 GeV/c with R_1 = 6 cm this is about 60 μm, three times the measurement term. Small R_1 helps twice: it shortens the extrapolation and reduces the lever arm for scattering. The two terms add in quadrature, which is why impact-parameter resolutions are quoted in the form a \oplus b/p_T.

Third Problem Set item 3 projects tracks back to r = 0 through a chamber with several layers and specified material. The method combines this section with the Week 5 rule for summing material: add up x/X_0 in front of the measurements, find the scattering angle, and combine it in quadrature with the extrapolated measurement errors. The specific numbers are left to the assignment.

Two real trackers: DELPHI and ATLAS

The deck ends with the measured performance of the DELPHI tracker at LEP and the projected performance of the ATLAS tracker at the LHC. Both combine the technologies of this chapter.

DELPHI, from the inside out:

  1. Three layers of silicon microstrips at r = 6–11 cm: \sigma_{r\phi} \approx 8 μm, \sigma_{rz} \approx 13–25 μm.
  2. A jet chamber with 24 wires per sector at r = 12–23 cm: \sigma_{r\phi} \approx 40 μm.
  3. Five layers of straw tubes at r = 24–28 cm, used in the trigger.
  4. A TPC with 16 pad rows at r = 40–110 cm: \sigma_{r\phi} = 250 μm and \sigma_{rz} = 880 μm, over drift lengths up to 1.34 m.
  5. Five layers of proportional tubes outside the RICH particle-identification detector.

Its measured results: an impact-parameter resolution of 20 μm in r\phi and 60 μm in z (at 90°) for p_T \gtrsim 5 GeV/c, and a momentum resolution \sigma_p/p = 0.6\times10^{-3}\,p. The TPC alone gives about 7\times10^{-3}\,p. Adding the silicon on the inside and the tubes on the outside stretches the lever arm from 0.7 m to 1.9 m, and with the 1/L_T^2 scaling of the curvature term, (0.7/1.9)^2 \times 7\times10^{-3} \approx 1\times10^{-3}. The deck’s plots show the resolution against polar angle and momentum, including the 1/p peak from 45.6 GeV muons in Z \to \mu\mu decays.

ATLAS (as projected in the lecture):

  1. Two pixel layers at r = 11.5–17.5 cm: \sigma_{r\phi} \approx 15 μm, pitch 50 μm.
  2. Four layers of silicon strips at r = 30–60 cm: \sigma_{r\phi} \approx 25 μm and \sigma_z \approx 800 μm, pitch 85 μm, with a stereo angle of about 2°.
  3. About 35 layers of 7 mm straw tubes at r = 63–107 cm, with \sigma = 150 μm per straw: nearly continuous tracking, with transition radiators between straws to identify electrons.

The simulated momentum resolution is \sigma/p_T \approx 0.04\%\,p_T, several times better than CDF, and the impact-parameter resolution takes the form 27 \oplus 220/(p_T\sqrt{\sin\theta}) μm: a constant measurement term and a scattering term falling as 1/p_T, the structure derived in the previous section.

Summary

Note

Key takeaways from Week 6:

  • A tracker must be thin. Resolutions run from about 1 cm (scintillator) through 1 mm (gas) to 10 μm (silicon); material costs precision through multiple scattering.
  • Gas detectors work in three stages. Ionization leaves about 100 electrons per cm of argon; they drift in the field; an avalanche near a thin wire multiplies them by M = \exp\int\alpha\,dx.
  • Drift spreads the charge. Diffusion gives \sigma_x^2 = 2Dt, so \sigma_x = \sigma(1\,\text{cm})\sqrt{x}. Ions drift with w = \mu E/P; electrons are much faster, depend strongly on the gas, and saturate in some mixtures. Cool gases approach the thermal limit.
  • The signal comes from the ions. A wire in a tube has E \propto 1/r; about 99% of the induced signal comes from ions moving out, giving V(t) \propto \ln(1+t/t_0). The full drift takes hundreds of μs, but half the signal arrives in t_{1/2} = T\,a/(a+b), so the pulse is shaped short.
  • Binary readout gives pitch over root 12. An MWPC locates a track to s/\sqrt{12}; crossed and stereo planes resolve ambiguities, and cathode strips with centre-of-gravity readout do much better.
  • Drift time gives distance. A drift chamber measures x = wt, needs a uniform field, and must break the left-right ambiguity. A TPC drifts charge over a large volume to give three-dimensional points.
  • Momentum from the sagitta. p_T = 0.3BR and s \approx L^2/8R; the curvature term in \delta p/p grows as p_T and falls as 1/BL^2, while multiple scattering adds a momentum-independent floor that favours light gases such as helium.
  • Magnetic fields deflect the drift. With \mathbf E \perp \mathbf B, electrons drift at the Lorentz angle, \tan\alpha_L = \omega\tau.
  • The impact parameter needs a short first lever arm. \epsilon_v = \sqrt{R_1^2+R_2^2}\,\sigma/\Delta R, plus a scattering term about (1\ \text{mrad}/p)R_1; small R_1 helps both.

If you can explain why a wire chamber’s signal is mostly induced by the ions, why its useful pulse is much shorter than the ion drift time, and why a tracker’s momentum resolution worsens at high momentum while its impact-parameter resolution worsens at low momentum, using only “Townsend avalanche,” “ion drift,” “pulse shaping,” “sagitta,” and “multiple scattering,” you have understood the core of Week 6.
