Week 4: Rare Event Searches and Cryogenics

Week 4: Rare Event Searches and Cryogenics

Overview. Weeks 2 and 3 built colliders, where the problem is making enough collisions at high enough energy. This week turns to the other half of the course, the experiments of the Week 1 energy ladder that look for something extremely rare at low energy: a dark matter particle bumping a nucleus, a nucleus decaying in a way never yet seen, a neutrino scattering coherently from a whole nucleus. For these experiments the difficulty is not making events but recognising the few that matter among everything else the detector sees. In Week 1 we called the things we look for signal and the look-alikes background. This week makes that distinction quantitative.

The chapter follows the two lecture decks in order. The first, Laboratories for rare event search experiments, designs an ideal dark matter detector: how many signal events to expect, where the background comes from, and the five steps used to suppress it. The second, Battle with noise: cooling it down, explains why many of these detectors run at temperatures of a few thousandths of a kelvin, which material properties matter on the way down, and the machines (pulse tubes, dilution refrigerators, magnetic refrigerators) that get them there. The running example throughout is the slides’ own: the SuperCDMS dark matter experiment at SNOLAB and the cryostats that cool it.

The related problem set is eHEP Homework 2, items 1 to 3 (the heat load of a cryostat cable, a stainless steel line into the mixing chamber, and the cavern gamma background in DRU). Items 4 and 5 of HW4 and item 5 of the Third Problem Set also lean on ideas introduced here. These notes explain the concepts those items depend on. The specific calculations are left to the assignments.

Rare event searches

What do the experiments of the low-energy half of the course have in common?

They all search for a process so rare that, in a detector of tens or hundreds of kilograms, only a handful of events are expected per year. The lecture calls this finding a needle in a haystack, and gives three examples, with numbers benchmarked for xenon-based experiments:

Search Expected signal Signal region
Weakly interacting massive particle (WIMP) dark matter, direct detection fewer than about 1 event per 30 kg·year below 10 keV
Neutrinoless double beta decay (0\nu\beta\beta) fewer than about 1 event per 500 kg·year a few MeV
Coherent elastic neutrino-nucleus scattering (CEvNS) about 1 event per 10 kg·year below 1 keV

Two features recur. The expected number of signal events is quoted per unit of mass times time, because doubling the detector mass or the running time doubles the number of chances for the rare process to happen. And the signal sits in a narrow band of energy, which is where all of the effort to remove background is concentrated.

Background and the DRU

How do we compare the background in two different experiments, with different masses, running times and energy windows?

Definition. The exposure of an experiment is its target mass times its live time, for example in kg·day. The region of interest (ROI) is the window of deposited energy in which the signal is expected.

For a process with a small cross section, the number of events grows in proportion to the exposure, and over a narrow ROI the spectrum is often roughly flat. It is therefore natural to quote a background rate per unit exposure and per unit energy.

Definition. The differential rate unit (DRU) is one event per keV of deposited energy, per kg of detector, per day: 1\ \text{DRU} = 1\ \text{event}/(\text{keV}\cdot\text{kg}\cdot\text{day}).

The lecturer’s benchmark numbers for the background level of an environment:

  • Unshielded laboratory: about 10,000 DRU. This is dominated by gamma rays from the radioactive isotopes potassium-40, thorium-232 and uranium-238 in the walls. At sea level, cosmic-ray muons also arrive at about 1 per minute per cm².
  • Useful environment: about 100 DRU.
  • Good environment: below 10 DRU, and ideally far below.
Note

Worked example. How many background events does a “useful” 100 DRU environment give in one kilogram running for one year, in a 10 keV-wide ROI? Multiply the rate by each factor of the exposure and the window: N = (100\ \text{keV}^{-1}\,\text{kg}^{-1}\,\text{day}^{-1})(10\ \text{keV})(1\ \text{kg})(365\ \text{days}) \approx 3.7\times10^{5}. A “good” 10 DRU environment gives about 3.7\times10^{4}, and an unshielded lab about 3.7\times10^{7}. A WIMP search expects fewer than one signal event in thirty such kilogram-years. Even a good environment leaves the signal outnumbered by about a million to one, which is why the rest of the first deck is about removing background and telling the remainder apart from signal.

The slides show the same comparison for a CEvNS search. The predicted Standard Model rate is a few hundred DRU at recoil energies of 1 eV, falls steeply above about 0.1 keV, and vanishes near 1.3 keV. The plot marks an assumed electron-recoil background at 100 DRU and a nuclear-recoil background near 5 DRU: the signal rises above the electron-recoil level only below about 0.1 keV. Dotted curves show how possible new physics (a neutrino magnetic moment, a sterile neutrino, a light Z' boson) would change the spectrum, which is what such an experiment hopes to measure. A low energy threshold is what makes the signal visible at all.

Noise and background

The words “noise” and “background” are sometimes used interchangeably, both meaning “whatever is not signal”. The lecture separates them when it matters:

  • Noise is present continuously. It comes from physics (thermal motion of electrons, for instance), but usually not from particle interactions. It is the subject of the second half of this chapter and of a later lecture on noise modelling.
  • Background comes mostly from particle interactions, known or unknown. Each background event produces a pulse in the detector, just as a signal event does.

The dark matter wind

Where would a WIMP signal come from?

Our galaxy is thought to sit inside a roughly spherical halo of dark matter. The Sun moves through this halo at about 220 km/s, so in the frame of the Earth the dark matter arrives as a wind, which happens to blow from the direction of the constellation Cygnus. The slides quote a flux of about 10^{5} particles per cm² per second, or about 20 million passing through a hand every second.

Where the rate comes from

The flux follows from a density and a speed. If the local dark matter mass density is \rho_0 and each particle has mass m_\chi, the number density is n = \rho_0/m_\chi, and the flux is n v.

Note

Worked example. A commonly used value of the local density is \rho_0 \approx 0.3 GeV/cm³ (this number is not on the slides). For a WIMP of mass m_\chi = 100 GeV/c^2, n = 0.3/100 = 3\times10^{-3} cm^{-3}. With v = 220 km/s = 2.2\times10^{7} cm/s, n v \approx (3\times10^{-3}\ \text{cm}^{-3})(2.2\times10^{7}\ \text{cm/s}) \approx 7\times10^{4}\ \text{cm}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}, which is the slides’ \sim10^{5}. The “20 million per hand” corresponds to a hand of about 2\times10^{7}/10^{5} = 200 cm².

Each nucleus in the detector then interacts at a rate n v \sigma, where \sigma is the WIMP-nucleus cross section. With twenty million particles crossing a hand each second and only about one interaction per kilogram per year, \sigma must be extraordinarily small.

The signal rate

How does the expected signal spectrum depend on the WIMP, the target and the galaxy?

The lecture (adapted from a TASI 2016 lecture) writes the differential rate of nuclear recoils, in events/(keV·kg·day), as a product of three factors:

\frac{dR}{dE_R} = \frac{\sigma_0}{m_\chi}\;\frac{F^2(E_R)}{m_r^2}\;\frac{\rho_0\,T(E_R)}{v_0\sqrt{\pi}}

The slides colour the three fractions, from left to right, as particle theory, nuclear structure and astrophysics. Here E_R is the kinetic energy given to the recoiling nucleus, \sigma_0 is the WIMP-nucleus cross section at zero momentum transfer, and m_r is the reduced mass of the WIMP and the nucleus, m_r = \frac{m_\chi m_N}{m_\chi + m_N}. The formula is written in units where constants such as c are absorbed. Each factor collects what one community would need to know: particle theorists predict \sigma_0 and m_\chi, nuclear physicists the shape of the nucleus, and astrophysicists the density and speeds of the halo.

The astrophysics factor

The velocity distribution of the halo enters through T(E_R) = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\,v_0\int_{v_{\min}}^{\infty}\frac{f_1(v)}{v}\,dv, an integral over the local distribution f_1(v) of WIMP speeds, where v_0 is the characteristic speed of the halo (about 220 km/s). The lower limit is the slowest WIMP that can produce a recoil of energy E_R: v_{\min} = \sqrt{\frac{E_R\,m_N}{2m_r^2}}. For a pure Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution of speeds, the integral gives T(E_R) \simeq \exp(-v_{\min}^2/v_0^2).

Since v_{\min}^2 \propto E_R, this factor falls exponentially with recoil energy. This is the origin of the featureless, falling spectrum we will meet below: a large recoil needs a fast WIMP, and fast WIMPs are rare.

The nuclear structure factor

A WIMP scatters from the nucleus as a whole only if the momentum it transfers, q = \sqrt{2 m_N E_R}, is small enough that it cannot resolve the nucleus’s internal structure. At larger q, the waves scattered from different parts of the nucleus begin to cancel, and the form factor F(E_R) drops below 1. The slides use the Helm form factor, an approximation to a Woods–Saxon nuclear density: F^2(E_R) = \left[\frac{3\,j_1(qR_1)}{qR_1}\right]^2 e^{-(qs)^2}, where R_1 is an effective nuclear radius, s is the nuclear skin thickness (the distance over which the nuclear density falls to zero, a gradual fall rather than a step), and j_1 is the spherical Bessel function of order one. The slides label the expression F(E_R) and describe J_1 as the cylindrical Bessel function. The expression as written is F^2, and the Helm form uses the spherical function j_1.

The form factor matters most for heavy nuclei, which are large and so are resolved at smaller q.

The particle physics factor

In the simplest case, a spin-independent interaction, the WIMP couples to protons with strength f_p and to neutrons with f_n. At zero momentum transfer the amplitudes from all the nucleons in the nucleus add coherently, so \sigma_0 = \frac{4m_r^2}{\pi}\big[Z f_p + (A-Z) f_n\big]^2 \simeq \frac{4m_r^2}{\pi}\,f^2A^2, where Z is the proton number, A is the mass number (the slides call it the atomic mass), and the last form assumes f_p = f_n = f. The slides’ approximate form writes f A^2; with equal couplings the square applies to f as well.

The factor A^2 is the coherence: a nucleus of A nucleons scatters A^2 times as strongly as a single nucleon, not A times. The slides call this an enormous enhancement for heavy targets.

Different targets

Which target material should we use?

The slides plot dR/dE_R for a 100 GeV/c^2 WIMP with \sigma = 10^{-45} cm² in xenon, germanium, argon, silicon and neon. At zero recoil energy the curves are ordered by mass number, and at higher energies xenon falls fastest.

Note

Worked example. Near E_R = 0 the form factor is 1 and T(0) is the same for every target, so the rate per kilogram scales as \sigma_0/m_r^2 \propto A^2. The ratio of xenon (A \approx 131) to germanium (A \approx 72.6) should then be \left(\frac{131}{72.6}\right)^2 \approx 3.3. Reading the slide’s plot, the xenon curve starts near 0.18 and germanium near 0.055 (in counts per 10 kg per keV per year), a ratio of about 3.3. The same scaling places argon (A \approx 40) near 0.055\times(40/72.6)^2 \approx 0.017, which matches its curve as well.

Xenon’s steeper fall is the form factor of the previous section: a large nucleus is resolved at lower momentum transfer.

The lecture adds a further reason to use more than one target. More general WIMP-nucleon interactions exist than the spin-independent case, and they change not only the relative rates between targets but the shapes of the spectra. The slides show two such models in which fluorine or germanium is enhanced instead of xenon. If a signal is ever seen, a programme of several target materials will be needed to work out which interaction produced it.

Exposure and threshold

The slides then start a list of requirements for an ideal nuclear-recoil detector. The first two are:

  1. Large exposure (mass times time), because the rate per kilogram per year is so small.
  2. Low energy threshold, because the spectrum is largest at the lowest recoil energies.

Definition. The energy threshold of a detector is the smallest deposited energy it can reliably record. Events below it are lost.

Why does the threshold matter so much for light WIMPs? In an elastic collision, the most energy the WIMP can give the nucleus is in a head-on collision, which transfers momentum q_{\max} = 2m_r v. The recoil energy is then q^2/2m_N, so E_{R,\max} = \frac{2m_r^2 v^2}{m_N}. This is the slides’ \Delta E \lesssim 2M_{DM}^2v^2/M_N in the limit m_\chi \ll m_N, where m_r \approx m_\chi. Turned around, it gives the v_{\min} of the astrophysics factor: a WIMP must have at least a minimum velocity to produce a recoil of a given energy.

Note

Worked example. Take a germanium nucleus, m_N \approx 72.6\ \text{u} \approx 67.6 GeV/c^2, and a WIMP at v = 220 km/s, so \beta = v/c \approx 7.3\times10^{-4} and \beta^2 \approx 5.4\times10^{-7}. With masses in GeV/c^2, E_{R,\max} = 2(m_rc^2)^2\beta^2/(m_Nc^2).

For m_\chi = 100 GeV/c^2: m_r = (100)(67.6)/167.6 \approx 40.3 GeV/c^2, and E_{R,\max} \approx \frac{2(40.3)^2(5.4\times10^{-7})}{67.6}\ \text{GeV} \approx 2.6\times10^{-5}\ \text{GeV} = 26\ \text{keV}. For m_\chi = 5 GeV/c^2: m_r = (5)(67.6)/72.6 \approx 4.66 GeV/c^2, and E_{R,\max} \approx \frac{2(4.66)^2(5.4\times10^{-7})}{67.6}\ \text{GeV} \approx 3.5\times10^{-7}\ \text{GeV} \approx 0.35\ \text{keV}. A 100 GeV WIMP at a typical speed can leave a few tens of keV, which is the “few 10s of keV” on the slides. A 5 GeV WIMP leaves at most a few hundred eV. Only the fastest WIMPs in the tail of the distribution, several times faster than 220 km/s, can push it to a few keV.

The slides’ threshold plots show the consequence. For a germanium target, the total rate above threshold for WIMPs of 3 to 10 GeV falls by orders of magnitude as the threshold rises from zero to a few keV, and falls fastest for the lightest WIMPs. For a 5 GeV WIMP, lighter targets (silicon, oxygen) keep their rate to higher thresholds than heavy ones (xenon, tungsten), because a light nucleus takes a larger share of the WIMP’s energy. The summary slide later pairs the threshold requirement with good energy resolution (the Week 1 notion of how finely a detector measures energy).

Signal against background: electron and nuclear recoils

How large is the background compared with the signal, event for event?

The slides set two spectra side by side. A 20 GeV WIMP with \sigma = 10^{-45} cm² gives an exponentially falling spectrum below about 30 keV, with no peak, knee or break to recognise it by, and about 1 event per kg per year. A banana, through the potassium-40 it contains, gives a clear gamma-ray peak in a nearby detector and about 100 events per kg per second.

Note

Worked example. A year is about 3.2\times10^{7} s, so the ratio of the two rates is \frac{100\ \text{kg}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}\times 3.2\times10^{7}\ \text{s/yr}}{1\ \text{kg}^{-1}\,\text{yr}^{-1}} \approx 3\times10^{9}. Ordinary radioactivity at the level of a banana outnumbers the signal by billions. The radioactive background of most materials is higher than the WIMP event rate.

The two rates differ in kind as well as in size, and this is what makes the problem solvable.

Definition. In an electron recoil (ER), the incoming particle scatters from an atomic electron. Gamma rays and beta electrons produce electron recoils. In a nuclear recoil (NR), it scatters from an atomic nucleus. WIMPs, CEvNS neutrinos and neutrons produce nuclear recoils.

The slides list the typical backgrounds in each class:

  • Electron recoils. Gammas are the most prevalent background. Betas arise both on detector surfaces and in the bulk.
  • Nuclear recoils. Neutrons are not distinguishable from WIMPs, event by event. Alphas are almost always surface events. A nucleus recoiling after emitting an alpha (the “recoiling parent nucleus”) is another surface event.

Most backgrounds come either from trace radioactivity (uranium, thorium and potassium contamination) or are induced by cosmic rays (cosmogenic background). Since the signal is a nuclear recoil and most of the background is electron recoils, the ability to tell the two apart helps enormously.

Managing backgrounds in five steps

How do we get from an unshielded lab at 10^{4} DRU to a detector in which a WIMP could be seen? The lecture gives five steps, built up one at a time on the slides.

Step 1: radiopure materials

Choose highly radiopure materials for the detector and everything around it, and build it in a cleanroom (class 1000 or better is common) so that dust does not bring radioactivity back in.

Screening and assay. Every material is measured for radioactivity before use. The slides show the instruments: a germanium gamma spectrometer at SNOLAB, a large-area alpha detector, a beta screener (a multiwire proportional chamber), and an inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer, which counts the atoms of an isotope directly. The contamination being looked for is often at the level of parts per billion (ppb), so the screening detector must itself be almost as well shielded and low in background as the dark matter detector.

Results are collected in a shared database (radiopurity.org, run by Pacific Northwest National Laboratory and SNOLAB). Its conversion table relates an activity in becquerels (decays per second) to a concentration: 1 Bq of uranium-238 per kg corresponds to 81 ppb of uranium.

Note

Worked example. Where does “1 Bq/kg = 81 ppb” come from? Activity is \lambda N, with decay constant \lambda = \ln 2/t_{1/2}. Uranium-238 has a half-life of 4.47\times10^{9} years = 1.41\times10^{17} s (not on the slides), so \lambda \approx 4.9\times10^{-18} s^{-1}. One decay per second needs N = \frac{1\ \text{s}^{-1}}{4.9\times10^{-18}\ \text{s}^{-1}} \approx 2.0\times10^{17}\ \text{atoms}, of mass (2.0\times10^{17})(238\ \text{g/mol})/(6.02\times10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}) \approx 8.1\times10^{-5} g. In one kilogram that is 8.1\times10^{-8} by mass, or 81 ppb. A long half-life means a large number of atoms per becquerel.

If you cannot find it, make it. When no available material is clean enough, experiments purify their own. The slides show a krypton and radon removal system for XENON1T, a distillation tower at Fermilab that extracts argon depleted in the radioactive isotope argon-39 from underground natural gas wells, and a copper electroforming setup at PNNL, which grows very pure copper atom by atom.

Step 2: going underground

Cosmic-ray muons passing near the experiment knock fast neutrons out of nuclei in the surrounding rock and material, a process called spallation. Fast neutrons are hard to shield against and produce nuclear recoils, exactly like WIMPs, so they are an irreducible background. The remedy is to go deep underground, where the rock above removes most of the muons, and with them the fast neutrons.

Definition. Depth underground is quoted in metres water equivalent (m.w.e.): the depth of water that would stop muons as well as the actual rock overhead. Because rock is denser than water, the m.w.e. depth is larger than the true depth.

The slides plot the muon flux and the fast-neutron flux against effective depth from 1 to 7 km.w.e., with underground labs marked: WIPP, Soudan and Kamioka near 1.5–2 km.w.e.; Boulby and Gran Sasso near 3; Modane and Homestake near 4; SNOLAB near 6; and China’s Jinping laboratory (CJPL), the deepest, near 6.7. Both fluxes fall roughly exponentially with depth. At SNOLAB, about 2 km underground in a mine near Sudbury, the plot reads a muon flux of a few times 10^{-10} cm^{-2}s^{-1}. Against the sea-level rate of 1 per minute per cm² (1.7\times10^{-2} cm^{-2}s^{-1}), that is a reduction of roughly 10^{7} to 10^{8}.

Step 3: shielding

Unless the detector is buried 2 km deep in pristine glacial ice, the rock of the laboratory itself is radioactive. Trace uranium, thorium and potassium in the cavern walls produce a steady flux of gammas, and of neutrons (through spontaneous fission and (\alpha, n) reactions, in which an alpha particle knocks a neutron out of a light nucleus). The experiment is surrounded by several tons of radiopure shielding.

Passive shielding absorbs the radiation. The slides use the SuperCDMS shield at the Soudan mine:

  • Lead stops gammas: about 22 cm reduces the gamma rate by about 10^{6}.
  • Ancient lead or copper forms the innermost layer. Freshly made lead contains lead-210, a radioactive isotope of lead whose decay chain emits betas and gammas close to the detector. Lead that has sat for centuries (recovered, for example, from old shipwrecks) has lost most of its lead-210.
  • Polyethylene or water slows neutrons down. Hydrogen nuclei have nearly the neutron’s mass, so each collision removes a large fraction of the neutron’s energy. This is the moderator principle, used here so that radiogenic and cosmogenic neutrons can no longer make recoils above the experimental threshold. About 0.5 m of polyethylene reduces the neutron scattering rate by about 10^{4}.

Attenuation in a uniform shield is exponential: each thickness \lambda reduces the intensity by a factor e, so a thickness x transmits a fraction e^{-x/\lambda}.

Note

Worked example. What attenuation length do the slides’ lead numbers imply? A reduction of 10^{6} in 22 cm means 22\ \text{cm}/\lambda = \ln(10^{6}) \approx 13.8, so \lambda \approx 1.6 cm. The same reasoning for polyethylene, 10^{4} in 50 cm, gives \lambda \approx 50/9.2 \approx 5.4 cm for neutrons. These are effective values averaged over the spectrum of the cavern radiation. How a particular shield, solid angle and detector translate a cavern gamma flux into DRU is a calculation the assignment asks for, so it is left there.

Active shielding detects the radiation and vetoes the events it causes.

  • A muon veto, made of water (which detects muons through their Cherenkov radiation) or of scintillator, rejects events in which a muon passed through or near the experiment, together with the fast neutrons it brings. The slides show the LUX water tank.
  • A neutron veto, liquid scintillator doped with an isotope that readily captures neutrons, tags neutrons from contaminated material close to or inside the experiment. A neutron that scatters in the detector and then is captured in the veto is identified and rejected. The slides show the DarkSide veto and a SuperCDMS schematic.
Note

Figure 1. A schematic of the shielding layers, outermost first: an active veto, a moderator for neutrons, lead for gammas, and an inner layer free of lead-210. Real shields such as SuperCDMS at Soudan are cylinders, and the whole assembly sits in an underground laboratory whose rock overburden has already removed most cosmic-ray muons.

Steps 4 and 5: discrimination and analysis

  1. Even after all this, the detector will likely still record about 10^{6} more electron recoils than expected WIMP scatters. The experiment must therefore be able to tell electron recoils from nuclear recoils, ideally rejecting all but one electron recoil in 10^{6} or 10^{7}.
  2. A team of students and postdocs who tune the rejection of background while keeping as much signal as possible (the signal acceptance) will extract the most from the data.

Separating signal from background

What physical differences between signal and background can a detector use?

The lecture groups them in two families. Detector response: the detector’s own response to an electron recoil differs from its response to a nuclear recoil, which gives particle identification. Astrophysical modulation: the WIMP signal changes with the Earth’s motion through the halo, while backgrounds do not. Either can be applied event by event (each event is classified) or statistically (only the population as a whole shows the difference).

Particle identification from the detector response

A particle that deposits energy in a crystal or liquid shares it among three channels, which the slides draw as three overlapping regions around the interaction:

Channel Energy per quantum Share of the deposited energy
Phonons (heat) about 10 meV per phonon eventually 100%
Ionization (free charges) about 10 eV per charge about 20%
Scintillation (light) about 1 keV per photon a few %
Note

Worked example. For a 10 keV deposit, the energy per quantum gives roughly 10\ \text{keV}/10\ \text{meV} = 10^{6} phonons, 10\ \text{keV}/10\ \text{eV} = 10^{3} charges, and 10\ \text{keV}/1\ \text{keV} = 10 photons. The more quanta a channel produces, the smaller its relative statistical fluctuation, which is one reason phonon-sensing detectors reach the lowest thresholds and why they must be so cold (the second half of the chapter).

Electron and nuclear recoils divide their energy differently. A recoiling nucleus loses a larger share of its energy directly to atomic motion (heat), and so produces less ionization and less light than an electron recoil of the same total energy. Measuring two channels at once separates them.

Definition. The ionization yield of an event is the ratio of the energy measured in ionization to the total recoil energy measured in phonons, Y = E_{\text{ionization}}/E_{\text{phonon}}, normalised so that electron recoils have Y \approx 1.

The textbook example is the Cryogenic Dark Matter Search (CDMS), which measured both channels in germanium crystals. Its calibration plot shows gammas from a barium-133 source in a band at Y \approx 1 and neutrons from a californium-252 source (the slide’s “¹³²Cf” is a typo) in a band near Y \approx 0.3. The nuclear-recoil band is the signal region. Ionization yield alone rejected gammas at the level of 1 in 10^{4}.

Note

Figure 2. A sketch of the CDMS discrimination plane. Electron recoils form a band near yield 1 and nuclear recoils a band near 0.3. Events with incomplete charge collection near the detector surface leak into the space between the bands and into the signal region.

Surface events. A rejection of 1 in 10^{4} sounds sufficient, but the slides point to events scattered between the two bands. These are surface events: betas, alphas, recoiling parent nuclei and X-rays that deposit their energy near a detector surface, where the charge is collected incompletely and the yield is too low. They are a near-universal problem in direct detection. The cure is fiducialization: use only a central (fiducial) volume of the target, which requires a detector that measures where an event happened as well as how much energy it deposited.

Other ways to identify particles listed on the slides:

  • Pulse-shape discrimination. In liquid argon, the time profile of the scintillation light differs for electron and nuclear recoils (used by DEAP/CLEAN and DarkSide).
  • A trigger that only nuclear recoils can fire. In a bubble chamber run at the right conditions, only the dense energy deposit of a nuclear recoil creates a bubble (COUPP, PICASSO, PICO). HW4 item 5 asks about the physics that sets which deposits form a bubble; that derivation is left to the assignment.
  • Self-shielding. In a large liquid xenon detector (XMASS), the outer layers of xenon absorb external gammas, and only the inner volume is used.

Astrophysical modulation

Annual modulation. The Earth orbits the Sun at an angle of about 60^\circ to the galactic plane, so for half the year its orbital velocity adds to the Sun’s motion through the halo and for half the year it subtracts. The WIMP speeds seen on Earth, and therefore the rate and spectrum, should vary with a period of one year, with extremes in June and December. Whether June is the maximum or the minimum depends on the target and the threshold. The slides quote a seasonal effect of about 2%.

Note

Worked example. Why only a few percent? The Earth’s orbital speed is about 30 km/s (not on the slides). At 60^\circ to the Sun’s direction of motion, the component along it is about 30\cos 60^\circ = 15 km/s. Against the Sun’s 230 km/s (the value on this slide), that is a variation of about 15/230 \approx 6.5\% in speed between June and December, and a smaller fractional change in the rate above a given threshold.

There are two ways to use the modulation. If you see a signal, check that it modulates. If your backgrounds are irreducible, use the modulation to pick the signal out statistically.

The slides show the best-known example: DAMA/LIBRA and its predecessor DAMA/NaI, large arrays of sodium iodide crystals (about 250 kg, 0.87 ton·year in the plotted data). Their residual rate oscillates with a one-year period, peaks in the expected season, and is concentrated at 2–6 keV. The combined data show a 9\sigma modulation consistent with dark matter. It has never been confirmed by another experiment, and no one has a convincing alternative explanation. The slide notes this was written in 2016 and is still true.

Diurnal modulation, or directional detection. Because the WIMP wind has a direction, the nuclear recoils it causes point preferentially opposite to the Sun’s motion: the distribution of the angle \alpha between the solar motion and the recoil direction peaks at \alpha = 180^\circ. As the Earth turns, the wind direction in the laboratory frame rotates once per sidereal day. Measuring this requires seeing the direction of a recoil track that is only millimetres long even in a gas. A low-pressure time projection chamber (DM-TPC) preserves the energy-loss profile along the track, so it can tell the head of the track from its tail.

The requirements of a rare event search

The first deck ends with the generalized requirements for any rare event search, not only for WIMPs:

  1. Large exposure (mass times time).
  2. Low energy threshold and good energy resolution.
  3. Low backgrounds.
  4. Discrimination between signal and backgrounds.

The slides suggest two papers for further reading: a characterization of the background spectrum of the DAMIC experiment at SNOLAB (arXiv:2110.13133), and the design of the Cryogenic Underground TEst (CUTE) facility at SNOLAB (arXiv:2310.07930).

Noise and why we cool

Why do so many rare event detectors run at temperatures near absolute zero?

The second deck starts from the observation that noise often limits a particle detector, and especially a rare event detector looking for a few eV or keV. Its first slide shows measured noise spectra from SuperCDMS readout and a published comparison of a measured noise spectrum with a noise model. Understanding and reducing noise is a constant topic of the field.

Reading a noise spectrum

The plots on that slide show “current noise” in A/\sqrt{\text{Hz}} against frequency.

Definition. The noise spectral density describes how the power of a fluctuating signal is distributed over frequency. Plotted as an amplitude, it has units of volts or amperes per \sqrt{\text{Hz}}: the root-mean-square noise in a frequency band \Delta f is the spectral density times \sqrt{\Delta f}, when the density is flat across that band.

The square root appears because powers in independent frequency bands add, so the noise power grows in proportion to the bandwidth and the noise amplitude grows as its square root. Narrow peaks in such a spectrum usually come from the environment (pickup at particular frequencies); the broad floor comes from the physics of the sensor and its circuit. See spectral density.

Types of noise

The slides list three kinds.

Johnson–Nyquist noise, from the random thermal motion of electrons in any resistor. It cannot be removed by better design, only by changing T, R or the bandwidth:

v_n = \sqrt{4k_BTR\,\Delta f}

Here v_n is the root-mean-square noise voltage, k_B is the Boltzmann constant, T the temperature, R the resistance and \Delta f the bandwidth. The noise power v_n^2/R = 4k_BT\Delta f is proportional to temperature: it is the thermal energy k_BT showing up in the circuit.

Shot noise, which arises because a current is made of discrete charges that cross a gap (such as a junction) at random times: i_n = \sqrt{2Iq\,\Delta f}, where I is the mean current and q the charge of each carrier.

Flicker noise, or 1/f noise, whose power falls inversely with frequency. It is not discussed further in this lecture.

Note

Worked example. A 10 kΩ resistor read out over a 1 kHz bandwidth. At room temperature, T = 300 K, v_n = \sqrt{4(1.38\times10^{-23})(300)(10^{4})(10^{3})}\ \text{V} \approx 4.1\times10^{-7}\ \text{V} = 0.41\ \mu\text{V}, a spectral density of 0.41\ \mu\text{V}/\sqrt{1000} \approx 13 nV/\sqrt{\text{Hz}}. At 4 K the same resistor gives 0.41\ \mu\text{V}\times\sqrt{4/300} \approx 47 nV. Cooling by a factor of 75 lowers the noise voltage by \sqrt{75} \approx 9.

The lecture’s conclusion: one general method of reducing noise is to cool it down. The rest of the chapter is about how.

The SuperCDMS tower

The slides illustrate what “cold” means in practice with a SuperCDMS detector tower, which steps down in temperature from top to bottom. A cable from room temperature (300 K) enters at the top. The upper stages, near 1 K (the slide shows an earlier 4 K label crossed out), carry the first amplifiers, HEMTs (high-electron-mobility transistors) and SQUIDs (superconducting quantum interference devices, very sensitive current amplifiers). A stage near 100 mK carries the shunt resistors of the sensor circuits, and the detector packages at the bottom sit near 10 mK. Copper housings and flexible cables connect the stages.

Each piece of electronics sits at the warmest stage where it works, because the colder a stage is, the less heat it can absorb. Every wire running down the tower carries heat with it. The rest of the deck is about the material properties and the refrigerators that make this staircase of temperatures possible. The readout devices themselves return in later weeks.

Material properties at low temperature

Every material in a cryostat must be judged by four properties: heat capacity, thermal conductivity, thermal contraction, and, for superconductors, the critical temperature below which they superconduct.

Specific heat

Definition. The specific heat c is the energy needed to raise the temperature of a unit mass (or a mole) of a material by one kelvin. The heat capacity C of a particular object is its specific heat times its mass, in J/K.

At temperatures far below a material’s Debye temperature (the temperature scale of its highest-frequency lattice vibrations; see the Debye model), the specific heat follows a power law, and the exponent reveals what holds the heat:

  • C \propto T: the heat is held by conduction electrons (metals).
  • C \propto T^3: the heat is held by phonons, the quantized lattice vibrations (insulators and crystals).

The slides’ plot of specific heat from 0.02 to 1 K shows these power laws for many materials (copper, aluminium, silver, gold, quartz, graphite, liquid and solid helium), and some exceptions with much larger heat capacities (CMN, manganin, constantan).

The T^3 law is why low temperature helps a detector that measures heat. Cooling a crystal from 1 K to 10 mK lowers its phonon heat capacity by (100)^3 = 10^{6}, so the same small deposit of energy raises its temperature a million times more.

Thermal conductivity

How easily does heat flow through a material?

Definition. The thermal conductivity \kappa relates the heat flux (power per unit area) to the temperature gradient through Fourier’s law of thermal conduction: \dot q = \frac{\dot Q}{A} = -\kappa\,\nabla T.

For a rod or wire of cross section A and length L, with ends held at T_1 and T_2, the steady heat flow is the same at every point along it. Rearranging Fourier’s law as \dot Q\,dx = A\,\kappa(T)\,dT and integrating along the rod gives

\dot Q = \frac{A}{L}\int_{T_1}^{T_2}\kappa(T)\,dT.

This is the form to use at low temperature, where \kappa itself changes strongly with T. A steady power through a material sustains a temperature difference across it, and the integral says how large.

Below the Debye temperature \kappa also follows power laws, and the integral can be done in closed form:

  • Electrons, \kappa = \kappa_0T: \dot Q = \frac{A\kappa_0}{2L}\left(T_2^2 - T_1^2\right), which for a small difference \Delta T reduces to \dot Q \approx (A/L)\,\kappa(T)\,\Delta T.
  • Phonons, \kappa = bT^3: \dot Q = \frac{Ab}{4L}\left(T_2^4 - T_1^4\right).
  • Electron-phonon coupling. Inside a metal at very low temperature the electrons and the lattice can themselves be at different temperatures, and the heat exchanged between them in a volume V is \dot Q = \kappa V\left(T_{\text{phonon}}^5 - T_{\text{electron}}^5\right), with \kappa here a coupling constant. Its derivative, the thermal conductance of this link, scales as T^4, which is the “k \sim T^4” on the slide.

Sometimes the limit is not the material but the boundary between two materials. Phonons are partly reflected at an interface because the two materials carry sound differently (an “acoustic mismatch”). The resulting boundary resistance is called Kapitza resistance.

The slides’ plot of \kappa from 1 to 1000 K shows the spread: copper and aluminium near 10^2–10^3 W/(m·K) at 10–100 K, stainless steel and alloys such as constantan and manganin 10 to 100 times lower, and plastics and glasses (G10, Pyrex) lower again.

Note

Worked example. The temperature dependence also tells us where to intercept heat. For a metal wire with \kappa = \kappa_0 T, the load is proportional to T_2^2 - T_1^2. A wire of fixed A/L running from 4 K to 10 mK carries a load proportional to 16 - 10^{-4} \approx 16 (in K²). The same wire running from 1 K to 10 mK carries a load proportional to about 1. Anchoring the wire at an intermediate stage near 1 K therefore cuts the heat that reaches the coldest stage by about a factor of 16, and the intermediate stage absorbs the rest where cooling is cheaper. Sizing real cables between real stages, with their actual lengths and materials, is the subject of eHEP Homework 2, items 1 and 2, and is left there.

The Wiedemann–Franz law

In a metal, both heat and charge are carried mainly by the conduction electrons, so the two conductivities are closely related. The Wiedemann–Franz law states

\frac{\kappa}{\sigma} = L_0T,\qquad L_0 = \frac{\pi^2}{3}\left(\frac{k_B}{e}\right)^2 = 2.44\times10^{-8}\ \text{W}\,\Omega/\text{K}^2,

where \sigma is the electrical conductivity and L_0 is the Lorenz number. Electrical conductivity is much easier to measure than thermal conductivity, so this law is the practical way to estimate \kappa.

At low temperature, both conductivities of a metal are usually limited by electrons scattering from impurities and defects, so they measure its purity. The standard measure is the residual-resistance ratio: \text{RRR} = \frac{\sigma_{4\,\text{K}}}{\sigma_{300\,\text{K}}} = \frac{R_{300\,\text{K}}}{R_{4\,\text{K}}}. For copper the slides give RRR of about 10 for a random piece, about 50 for very pure commercial copper, and more than 1000 for very pure copper that has been repeatedly annealed (heated to remove defects).

Note

Worked example. Copper’s resistivity at 300 K is about 1.7\times10^{-8} Ω·m (not on the slides). With RRR = 50, its resistivity at 4 K is 3.4\times10^{-10} Ω·m, so \sigma \approx 2.9\times10^{9} S/m. The Wiedemann–Franz law then gives \kappa = L_0 T\sigma \approx (2.44\times10^{-8})(4)(2.9\times10^{9})\ \text{W/(m·K)} \approx 290\ \text{W/(m·K)}. Doubling the RRR doubles \kappa at 4 K. The purity of the copper is what makes it a good thermal link.

The cooling time constant

How long does a part take to cool once it is connected to a cold stage?

Consider an object of heat capacity C connected to a cold “bath” at T_b by a link of thermal conductance G (in W/K, the heat flow per kelvin of temperature difference). Energy conservation gives C\,\frac{dT}{dt} = -G\,(T - T_b), whose solution is an exponential approach to the bath temperature, T - T_b \propto e^{-t/\tau}, with

\tau = \frac{C}{G}.

A large heat capacity or a poor link makes a long time constant. Both C and G change with temperature, so \tau does too.

Note

Worked example. A part with C = 0.5 J/K, attached to a stage through a link of G = 1 mW/K, has \tau = 0.5/10^{-3} = 500 s, about 8 minutes. Reducing its temperature difference from the stage by a factor of 100 takes \ln(100)\,\tau \approx 4.6\,\tau \approx 40 minutes. Halve the conductance of the link and every one of these times doubles.

The slides’ rules of thumb: plastics, and superconductors below their critical temperature, are hard to cool (in a superconductor the electrons that would carry heat are bound into pairs). Stainless steel parts with an extreme aspect ratio, long and thin, can have time constants of days to weeks. Copper is much easier to cool and is used throughout cryogenic apparatus; gold is better still.

The slides give a cautionary case from the HeRALD experiment. Its cell takes weeks to reach equilibrium, and one run ended early because a stainless steel capillary was poorly anchored thermally: the system could not get below 200 mK. A lab down the hall had the same problem with an unanchored stainless steel coaxial cable.

Thermal contraction

Objects change size as they cool (thermal expansion run in reverse). The slides’ rules of thumb:

  • Most of the contraction happens by about 77 K, the temperature of liquid nitrogen, so dunking a part in liquid nitrogen is a good test.
  • Plastics contract by about 1%, metals by about 0.1%. The slides’ plot shows Teflon contracting by more than 2% between 300 K and 4 K, and copper by about 0.3%.
  • A joint in which stainless steel bolts hold copper parts loosens when cold, because the copper shrinks more than the bolts. A joint with brass bolts tightens, because brass shrinks more than copper.
  • Spring washers (Belleville washers) are commonly used to keep joints tight as they contract.
Note

Worked example. A plastic ring 40 cm across contracting by 1% shrinks by 4 mm. A copper piece of the same size contracting by 0.1% shrinks by only 0.4 mm. The difference, a few millimetres, must be taken up somewhere.

The slides show what happens when it is not: polyethylene shielding rings for the Ricochet cryostat, held by copper screws, contracted radially while the screws did not. The tension shattered the polyethylene.

How to cool things down

Which machines take a detector from room temperature to 10 mK?

The lecture splits the problem at about 4 K, because different physics is available above and below it.

From room temperature to about 4 K, there are two approaches.

  • Wet: immerse in a cryogenic liquid. Liquid nitrogen boils at 77 K and liquid helium at 4.2 K. Pumping on liquid helium lowers its vapour pressure and so its boiling point, reaching about 1 K, which the slides reserve for “if you’re desperate”.
  • Dry: use a closed-cycle cryocooler, which recirculates helium gas driven by a compressor and needs only electricity. A Gifford–McMahon (GM) cooler reaches about 30 K; a pulse tube cryocooler reaches about 2.5 K.

Below about 4 K, the two methods in the lecture are the dilution refrigerator and the adiabatic demagnetization refrigerator.

The wet approach at accelerator scale is shown with the Tevatron cryogenic system: a 6500 m ring of superconducting magnets (777 dipoles, 216 quadrupoles and other components), divided into 48 strings cooled by 24 satellite refrigerators. A central liquefier supplied 154 litres of liquid helium per hour to each satellite, 3696 L/hour in total. This is the same problem that Week 3 met at the LHC, whose magnets run at 1.9 K.

The pulse tube cryocooler

A pulse tube refrigerator has, in order: a compressor, a heat exchanger (X_1) at room temperature T_H, a regenerator (a porous solid that stores heat from the gas on one half of the cycle and returns it on the other), a heat exchanger (X_2) at the cold temperature T_L, the pulse tube itself, a heat exchanger (X_3) back at T_H, and a flow restriction (orifice) leading to a buffer volume. The slides’ overly simplified description of the cycle:

  • When the compressor pushes, the gas in the pulse tube is compressed and heats up, and the extra heat is dumped out through X_3 at room temperature.
  • When the compressor pulls, the gas expands and cools, and T_L gets colder.

The cold end has no moving parts. Commercial units have two stages: a first pulse tube and regenerator from room temperature to a first stage near 50 K, and a second pair extending to a second stage near 3 K. In practice the “piston” is a rotary valve motor that alternately connects the pulse tubes to the high- and low-pressure lines of a helium compressor, with one reservoir volume per stage. The slides show the compressors and cryostat installed at SNOLAB.

Reading a capacity map. A cryocooler’s performance is given as a grid: for each pair of heat loads on the two stages, the temperatures the stages settle at. In the slides’ map for the Cryomech PT415, with no load the first stage sits near 31 K and the second near 2.6 K. With 25 W on the first stage and 0.5 W on the second, they settle near 34 K and 3.3 K. Loading either stage raises both temperatures, most strongly the one being loaded. The base temperature of a pulse tube therefore depends on the machine and its load, which is why the slides quote 2.5 K, about 3 K and 2.7 K in different places. Using such a map to decide how much cable heat load a stage can accept is part of eHEP Homework 2, item 1, and is left there.

The dilution refrigerator

How do we get from about 3 K to 10 mK, and keep a detector there continuously?

A dilution refrigerator (DR) of the kind used at CUTE is a stack of plates. The top two, near 50 K and 2.7 K, are cooled by a pulse tube. Below them, three stages are cooled by the dilution unit: the still (about 0.7 K), the cold plate (about 100 mK), and the mixing chamber (MC, about 10 mK), where the experiment is mounted.

Note

Figure 3. The stages of a dry dilution cryostat. Each plate is colder than the one above and can absorb less heat. A wire from room temperature is anchored (heat-sunk) at every plate, so that most of the heat it conducts is removed at the warmer stages where cooling power is larger.

How dilution cooling works

The working fluid is a mixture of the two isotopes of helium, helium-3 and helium-4. Below about 0.87 K (the slides say about 1 K), the liquid mixture separates into two phases, the way oil and water do: a concentrated phase that is nearly 100% helium-3, floating on top of a dilute phase that keeps about 6.6% helium-3 in helium-4 even as the temperature approaches zero. The helium-4 of the dilute phase is a superfluid and is essentially inert.

Moving a helium-3 atom from the concentrated to the dilute phase absorbs heat from the surroundings (endothermic), much as evaporating a liquid absorbs its latent heat. The difference is in how the cooling power behaves as the temperature drops. Evaporative cooling depends on the vapour pressure, which falls exponentially with temperature, so it dies away quickly. The dilution process falls only as T^2, which is what allows much lower ultimate temperatures.

The circulation, in steady state:

  1. The mixture (typically about 30% helium-3, 65 to 100 litres in total) is condensed into the unit. Then nearly pure helium-3 circulates.
  2. Helium-3 gas is condensed by flow restrictions (impedances) and precooled through heat exchangers at the still and the cold plate.
  3. Liquid helium-3 arrives in the mixing chamber in the concentrated phase and crosses into the dilute phase, which cools the mixing chamber.
  4. In the dilute phase, the helium-3 is drawn up to the still by a difference in osmotic pressure, passing through the heat exchangers and cooling the incoming helium-3 on the way.
  5. In the still, helium-3 evaporates (evaporative cooling, at about 0.7 K) far more readily than helium-4. The gas is pumped out at room temperature and sent back down.

A gas handling system of pumps and valves controls this circulation. It uses fast injection and pumping paths for precooling, and a normal injection path with a turbo pump for condensing and running at base temperature, and it includes pumps for the high vacuum around the stages.

The cooling power budget

The ideal cooling power of the mixing chamber is proportional to the circulation rate \dot n_3 of helium-3 (in mol/s) and to the square of the mixing chamber temperature:

\dot Q_{\max} = 84\,\dot n_3\,T_{\text{mc}}^2

with \dot Q in watts and T_{\text{mc}} in kelvin (from Pobell, Chapter 7). The T^2 says that cooling power is scarce exactly where it is needed most: halving the temperature quarters the heat the mixing chamber can absorb.

Note

Worked example. At a circulation rate of \dot n_3 = 300\ \mumol/s, the ideal cooling power is 84\,(3\times10^{-4})\,(0.050)^2\ \text{W} \approx 63\ \mu\text{W}\quad\text{at } 50\ \text{mK}, 84\,(3\times10^{-4})\,(0.010)^2\ \text{W} \approx 2.5\ \mu\text{W}\quad\text{at } 10\ \text{mK}. A heat load of a few microwatts decides whether the mixing chamber can reach 10 mK at all. A real refrigerator does less well than this ideal. Finding the temperature at which a measured cooling curve balances a given heat load is eHEP Homework 2, item 2, and is left there.

The still temperature is a compromise, ideally 600–700 mK. Too cold, and the helium-3 vapour pressure is too low to pump enough gas. Too hot, and too much helium-4 evaporates with it, lowering the helium-3 fraction of the circulating gas (it falls noticeably above about 600 mK). Tuning the operating point is, in the slides’ words, an art. Typical operating ranges and cooling powers from the slides:

Stage Temperature Cooling power
Still 400–900 mK below 10 mW
Cold plate 60–200 mK ill-defined
Mixing chamber 10–100 mK below 200 µW

Keeping heat out: wiring

With a few hundred microwatts at most available at the mixing chamber, it is imperative to limit the heat brought down by wiring and support structures.

Note

Worked example. The slides’ example: a 10 cm length of typical magnet wire, 30 AWG (diameter 0.255 mm), in copper with \kappa \approx 400 W/(m·K). Its cross section is A = \pi(0.255\times10^{-3}\ \text{m})^2/4 \approx 5.1\times10^{-8} m², so for a 1 K difference between its ends \dot Q = \frac{\kappa A}{L}\,\Delta T = \frac{(400)(5.1\times10^{-8})}{0.1}\,(1)\ \text{W} \approx 2.0\times10^{-4}\ \text{W} = 200\ \mu\text{W}. One short copper wire, with one kelvin across it, would use the entire cooling power of the mixing chamber. (The value 400 W/(m·K) is copper’s room-temperature conductivity, used here as an order of magnitude; at low temperature \kappa depends on T and on the copper’s RRR, as in the conduction integral.)

The remedies on the slides:

  • Heat sink well at each stage: wrap the wire around a post and glue it with a material of high thermal conductivity, so the heat is removed at the warmer stages (Figure 3).
  • Use thinner wire to reduce the cross section (fragile).
  • Add loops to increase the length (difficult for many wires).
  • Use wire of low thermal conductivity, such as phosphor bronze or stainless steel. By the Wiedemann–Franz law this also raises the electrical resistance, unless the wire is superconducting.
  • All of the above.

The adiabatic demagnetization refrigerator

Is there a way to reach millikelvin temperatures without circulating helium?

An adiabatic demagnetization refrigerator (ADR; see magnetic refrigeration) uses a paramagnetic salt, whose atoms carry magnetic moments (spins) that point in random directions unless a field aligns them. The salt sits inside a magnet, connected to a heat bath through a heat switch that can be opened or closed, and supported by mounts of low thermal conductivity. The cycle on the slides:

Note

Figure 4. The ADR cycle. Magnetizing the salt orders its spins and releases heat, which is carried to the bath through the closed heat switch. With the switch open, ramping the field down lets the spins disorder again; the energy for this comes from the salt’s own thermal motion, so it cools, and then absorbs heat from the experiment until it must be recycled.

The salt is cold only while it is warming back up, so an ADR is a single-shot refrigerator that must be recycled periodically. Pobell (Chapter 9) notes that ADRs work from about 2 mK to 1 K, and that they were largely replaced by the dilution refrigerator, which has the substantial advantage of running continuously. The lecture points out that ADRs are nonetheless still widely used:

  • They are much smaller than a dilution refrigerator.
  • They need little or no gas handling, so they are far less prone to leaks.
  • They do not rely on gravity (a dilution refrigerator needs its concentrated phase to float on the dilute phase), so they work on rockets, balloons and satellites.

The disadvantages: the heat switch is awkward, the large magnet is bad news for devices sensitive to magnetic fields, and the cycle must be repeated. The slides show a small ADR test setup with SQUID readout near 1.3 K, a salt pill (GGG) heat sinking near 300 mK, and a detector box near 50 mK.

The lecture closes with standard references for the techniques of this half: Pobell, Matter and Methods at Low Temperatures; Ekin, Experimental Techniques for Low-Temperature Measurements; Balshaw, Practical Cryogenics; the NIST cryogenic material properties database; and Duthil, Material Properties at Low Temperature.

Summary

Note

Key takeaways from Week 4:

  • Rare means per kilogram-year. WIMP, 0\nu\beta\beta and CEvNS searches expect of order one event per tens to hundreds of kg·year, in a narrow energy window.
  • Background is counted in DRU. One event per keV per kg per day. An unshielded lab is about 10^{4} DRU; a good environment is below 10, still about a million times the WIMP signal.
  • The signal rate factorizes. dR/dE_R is particle physics (\sigma_0 \propto A^2 for coherent spin-independent scattering), nuclear structure (the form factor) and astrophysics (the halo speeds, giving a falling exponential spectrum).
  • Light WIMPs need low thresholds. The maximum recoil energy 2m_r^2v^2/m_N is tens of keV for a 100 GeV WIMP on germanium but only a few hundred eV for 5 GeV.
  • Five steps against background. Radiopure materials, going underground, passive and active shielding, ER/NR discrimination at 10^{6}–10^{7}, and careful analysis.
  • Signal can be separated by response or by astrophysics. Ionization yield, pulse shape, nuclear-recoil-only triggers and fiducialization; annual and directional modulation.
  • Cooling reduces noise. Johnson noise power is 4k_BT\Delta f. Heat capacity, the conduction integral \dot Q = (A/L)\int\kappa\,dT, the time constant \tau = C/G and thermal contraction govern how parts behave on the way down.
  • Refrigerators in stages. Liquids or pulse tubes to about 3 K, then a dilution refrigerator, whose cooling power 84\,\dot n_3T^2 is only microwatts at 10 mK, or an ADR. Every wire must be heat-sunk at each stage.
