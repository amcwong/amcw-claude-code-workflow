Week 3: Future Colliders

Week 3: Future Colliders

Overview. Week 2 built a circular collider from dipoles, quadrupoles and RF cavities, and showed that a collider wins over a fixed target because the centre-of-mass energy is \sqrt{s}\approx 2E. This week asks what stops us from simply building the next ring at a higher energy. The answer for electrons is synchrotron radiation: a charged particle bent onto a circle radiates, and the loss grows so fast with energy that it sets the size of the machine. The lecture uses this one effect to sort the proposals for the next generation of colliders: linear electron colliders that never bend the beam, hadron colliders that bend heavier particles, and a muon collider that tries to keep the advantages of both.

The running examples are the proposals the slides compare: the ILC and CLIC linear colliders, the FCC-hh and HE-LHC hadron colliders measured against the LHC, and a muon collider. Two Week 2 results are restated where we need them: the bending radius \rho = p/0.3B (momentum in GeV/c, field in tesla, radius in metres), and the interaction rate \mathcal{L}\,\sigma.

The related problem set is the Accelerator Problems sheet, item 4 (a 50 TeV on 50 TeV proton collider). These notes explain the concepts that item depends on. The specific calculations it asks for are left to the assignment.

Why a bigger ring is not enough

Week 2 gave a direct recipe for a higher-energy ring. At fixed dipole field B, the bending radius grows in proportion to the momentum,

\rho = \frac{p}{0.3\,B},

so doubling the beam energy at the same field doubles the radius of the arcs. Stronger magnets shrink the ring, and a bigger tunnel raises the energy. If that were the whole story, the energy of a collider would be limited only by money and magnet technology.

For protons it nearly is, and the hadron proposals below are built along exactly these lines. For electrons a second limit arrives first. The particle loses energy every time it is bent, and that loss grows with the fourth power of the beam energy.

Synchrotron radiation

Why does bending a beam cost energy?

A direct consequence of Maxwell’s equations is that an accelerated charge radiates. A particle moving on a circle is accelerating even at constant speed, because its direction changes, and the acceleration points toward the centre of the circle, perpendicular to the motion.

Definition. Synchrotron radiation is the electromagnetic radiation emitted by a charged particle moving at relativistic speed with an acceleration perpendicular to its velocity, as in the magnetic field of a dipole.

For a particle of charge q on an arc of radius \rho, the radiated power is given by the relativistic form of the Larmor formula, which the slides quote in SI units (the source cited is Jackson, Classical Electrodynamics, Chapter 14):

P_\gamma = \frac{q^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0 c^3}\,a^2\gamma^4 = \frac{q^2 c}{6\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\beta^4\gamma^4}{\rho^2}.

Here \varepsilon_0 is the vacuum permittivity, a is the magnitude of the acceleration, \beta = v/c, and \gamma is the Lorentz factor, \gamma = E/mc^2. The two forms agree because the acceleration on a circle is a = v^2/\rho = \beta^2c^2/\rho.

The factor \gamma^4 carries the physics. At a fixed beam energy E, a lighter particle has a larger \gamma, and the power climbs as the fourth power of \gamma. A tighter bend (smaller \rho) also raises the power.

Note

Figure 1. The velocity is tangent to the orbit and the acceleration points to the centre. A relativistic particle emits its radiation into a narrow cone of opening angle about 1/\gamma around its direction of motion, and it does so all along the bent part of its path.

Energy lost per turn

Power is energy per unit time. What matters to the RF system of Week 2 is the energy a particle loses on each trip around the ring, because the cavities must put that much back every turn. If the bends all have radius \rho, the particle spends a time 2\pi\rho/\beta c in the dipoles per turn, so the loss per turn is

U_0 = P_\gamma\,\frac{2\pi\rho}{\beta c} = \frac{q^2\,\beta^3\gamma^4}{3\,\varepsilon_0\,\rho}.

Only the bends count here. Straight sections, where the particle is not accelerated, add circumference but not loss, which is one reason real rings are larger than 2\pi\rho.

Note

Worked example. Take an electron ring with beam energy E = 100 GeV and bending radius \rho = 3000 m. Then \gamma = 100\ \text{GeV}/0.511\ \text{MeV} \approx 1.96\times10^{5} and \gamma^4 \approx 1.47\times10^{21}. With q = e and \beta \approx 1, U_0 \approx \frac{(1.60\times10^{-19})^2\,(1.47\times10^{21})}{3\,(8.85\times10^{-12})\,(3000)}\ \text{J} \approx 4.7\times10^{-10}\ \text{J}, with the charge in coulombs, \varepsilon_0 in F/m and \rho in metres. That is about 3 GeV, so each electron loses roughly 3% of its energy on every turn, and the RF system must restore it every turn. Doubling the beam energy at the same radius multiplies the loss by 2^4 = 16.

This steep growth is why the proposals below either stop bending electrons or bend something heavier.

Electrons, protons and muons

Which particle should we collide? The slides compare three choices, and each trades one advantage against another.

Electrons are point-like. When an electron meets a positron, the whole beam energy goes into the collision, so \sqrt{s} = 2E is the energy actually available for making new particles. Their limit is the energy we can reach. A circular electron collider is limited by synchrotron radiation. A linear collider avoids bending, but can collide each accelerated bunch only once.

Protons are not point-like. A proton is made of quarks and gluons, collectively called partons, and a collision is really a collision between one parton from each proton. Each parton carries only a fraction of the proton’s energy, so the energy of the hard collision is a fraction of \sqrt{s}, and it varies from event to event. In exchange, the synchrotron loss is much lower. It is still not negligible at the LHC, where the radiated power ends up in the superconducting magnets.

Muons might give the best of both. A muon is point-like like the electron, but about 200 times heavier, so it radiates far less. Its problem is that it decays, with a lifetime at rest of about 2 \mus.

How much less does a heavier particle radiate? At a fixed beam energy E and radius \rho, with \beta \approx 1, the loss per turn depends on the mass only through \gamma = E/mc^2:

U_0 \propto \frac{\gamma^4}{\rho} = \frac{1}{\rho}\left(\frac{E}{mc^2}\right)^{4} \propto \frac{1}{m^4}.

The fourth power of the mass ratio sets the suppression.

Particle Mass relative to the electron Loss relative to an electron of the same energy
Electron 1 1
Muon \approx 207 \approx 1/(1.8\times10^{9})
Proton \approx 1836 \approx 1/(1.1\times10^{13})

The slides round the muon factor to 10^{9}. A muon ring at the energy of an electron ring therefore loses a billionth as much to radiation, and a proton ring ten thousand times less again.

Figure 2. The loss per turn scales as 1/m^4 at fixed energy and radius. The horizontal axis is logarithmic, so each tick is a factor of 1000.

Linear colliders

If bending an electron beam is what costs energy, why not accelerate it in a straight line?

Definition. A linear collider accelerates two beams in long straight accelerators (linacs) aimed at each other, and collides them once at a single interaction point. The beams are then dumped.

A linear collider has no synchrotron radiation limit, because the beam is never bent in the main linacs. The cost is that each accelerated bunch is used once. In a ring of Week 2 the same bunch returns and collides at the revolution frequency f, so the luminosity carries the factor n_b f (bunches times revolutions per second). A linear collider replaces that with the rate at which new bunches are accelerated, which is far lower. To still reach a useful luminosity, the beams must be squeezed to extremely small sizes at the collision point.

The two proposals in the slides are the International Linear Collider (ILC), with superconducting cavities, and the Compact Linear Collider (CLIC), with normal-conducting copper structures driven by a second, high-current drive beam. The slides compare them using a table from a CERN report (Zimmermann, CERN-ATS-2010-056); selected rows are below.

Parameter ILC 500 GeV CLIC 500 GeV CLIC 3 TeV
Total luminosity (10^{34} cm^{-2}s^{-1}) 2 2.3 5.9
Luminosity within 1% of peak energy (10^{34} cm^{-2}s^{-1}) 1.5 1.4 2.0
Repetition rate (Hz) 5 50 50
Accelerating gradient (MV/m) 32 80 100
Main linac RF frequency (GHz) 1.3 12 12
Particles per bunch (10^{9}) 20 6.8 3.7
Bunch separation (ns) 370 0.5 0.5
Horizontal, vertical beam size at the IP (nm) 639, 5.7 200, 2.3 40, 1.0
Total site length (km) 31 13 48
Total electrical power (MW) 230 130 415

Three rows explain the design. The accelerating gradient is the energy gained per metre of accelerating structure, so it sets how long the linacs must be. The beam sizes at the interaction point are a few hundred nanometres wide and a few nanometres tall, much smaller than in a ring; this is how a linear collider recovers luminosity at a repetition rate of a few tens of hertz. The site length follows from the gradient and the beam energy. Other slides in the deck quote slightly different bunch spacings for the ILC (360 ns and 333 ns), because they come from different versions of the design.

Note

Worked example. How long must the accelerating structure of one linac be? Each beam must reach half the centre-of-mass energy. For CLIC at 3 TeV, one beam needs 1.5 TeV = 1.5\times10^{6} MeV, and at 100 MV/m an electron gains 100 MeV per metre, so the structure needs at least \frac{1.5\times10^{6}\ \text{MeV}}{100\ \text{MeV/m}} = 1.5\times10^{4}\ \text{m} = 15\ \text{km}. The CLIC layout on the slides shows each main linac as 21 km long. The difference is room for focusing magnets, instrumentation and the gaps between structures. At the ILC gradient of about 32 MV/m, the same 1.5 TeV beam would need about 47 km of structure for one linac alone, which is one reason the ILC is proposed at a lower energy.

The energy spread of the collisions

Is every collision at the nominal energy? Not quite. Because the bunches are so dense at the collision point, each particle feels the strong field of the oncoming bunch and radiates as it passes through it. This radiation is called beamstrahlung. A particle that has radiated collides at slightly less than the full beam energy.

The slides show the resulting spectrum of collision energies for CLIC at 0.5, 1, 3 and 5 TeV. Each has a sharp peak at the nominal energy, E/E_0 = 1, and a tail toward lower energies. The tail is larger at higher energies. This is what the second luminosity row of the table measures: at 3 TeV only about a third of the total luminosity (2.0 out of 5.9) is within 1% of the peak energy.

Muon colliders

Can we have point-like collisions without the radiation of electrons? A muon collider is the slides’ answer.

  • Muons, like electrons, are point-like projectiles. All of the beam energy is available in the collision for making new particles.
  • Their bending radiation is smaller than that of electrons by the factor \approx(m_\mu/m_e)^4 \approx 200^4, the 10^{9} of the previous section. A muon beam can therefore be bent around a ring, and reused turn after turn.

The obstacle is the lifetime. A muon at rest decays in about 2.2 \mus (the slides round this to 2 \mus). A muon moving at nearly c lives longer in the laboratory because of time dilation: its laboratory lifetime is \gamma\tau.

Note

Worked example. Take a muon of energy 1.5 TeV. With m_\mu c^2 = 105.7 MeV, \gamma = 1.5\times10^{6}/105.7 \approx 1.4\times10^{4}. Its laboratory lifetime is \gamma\tau \approx (1.4\times10^{4})(2.2\ \mu\text{s}) \approx 31\ \text{ms}, in which it travels c\gamma\tau \approx 9000 km. In a ring 10 km around, that is about 900 turns on average before it decays. That is long enough to make collisions, but the whole chain of producing, preparing and accelerating the muons has to happen quickly.

The slides show the stages of a muon collider in order:

  1. A proton driver (a superconducting linac, an accumulator and a buncher) makes intense proton pulses.
  2. In the front end, the protons hit a target. The pions produced decay into muons, which are captured in a solenoid.
  3. The muons come out with a large spread in position and momentum. Cooling reduces this spread, which is the emittance of Week 2, before the beam is accelerated.
  4. Acceleration uses linacs and fast-acting rings (recirculating linacs, fixed-field alternating gradient rings, or rapid cycling synchrotrons).
  5. The collider ring brings \mu^+ and \mu^- into collision, at energies from a Higgs factory up to about 10 TeV.

The slides also show an alternative source, LEMMA, which would make muon pairs from positrons hitting electrons in a target. The muons come out with a small emittance, so less charge is needed in the collider rings, which gives lower backgrounds in the detector.

Decays also cause a detector problem. Muons that decay near the interaction region produce electrons, and these shower in the machine components and send a spray of particles into the detector. The slides call this the muon beam halo background, and show a simulation of tracks entering the detector from the beam line.

Hadron colliders

For protons the limits of the first section apply: field strength and tunnel size. The slides compare two proposals with the LHC. The HE-LHC would reuse the LHC tunnel with stronger magnets. The Future Circular Collider in its hadron version (FCC-hh) would need a new tunnel of about 100 km around Geneva, shown on the slides as a dashed ring enclosing the LHC. The electron counterpart in the slides is the Circular Electron Positron Collider proposed in China.

Selected rows from the slides’ parameter table:

Bunch spacing (ns) 25 25 25
Stored energy per beam (GJ) 8.4 1.4 0.36
Synchrotron radiation power per beam (kW) 2400 100 3.6

The HE-LHC row shows the recipe of Week 2 at work: the same 26.7 km ring with twice the dipole field roughly doubles the beam energy, since p = 0.3\,B\rho. FCC-hh combines the stronger field with a ring almost four times larger. Using \rho = p/0.3B to size a ring for a given energy and field, and estimating how much of the circumference the dipoles fill, is a calculation the assignment asks for, so it is left there.

Synchrotron radiation in a hadron ring

Protons radiate far less than electrons, but the factor \gamma^4 catches up at these energies. The radiated power per beam rises from 3.6 kW at the LHC to 2400 kW at FCC-hh. The slides give the radiation in the arcs per metre of ring, and the critical photon energy, a characteristic energy of the emitted photons:

Arc radiation LHC HE-LHC FCC-hh
Power per metre (W/m) 0.25 5.5 35
Critical photon energy (eV) 44 320 4300

Why does this matter for a superconducting machine? The magnet cold mass of the LHC runs at 1.9 K, and heat removed at that temperature is expensive. The radiation is therefore intercepted by a beam screen, a liner inside the beam pipe held at 40–60 K, where cooling is much cheaper. The slides show the LHC dipole cross-section with its beam screens and thermal shields, and the screen design proposed for FCC-hh and HE-LHC.

Pileup

How many proton collisions happen each time two bunches cross? Week 2 gave the interaction rate as \mathcal{L}\,\sigma. Dividing by the rate at which bunches cross gives the mean number of collisions per crossing.

Definition. Pileup is the set of extra proton-proton collisions that occur in the same bunch crossing as the collision of interest. Its mean number per crossing is \mu = \frac{\mathcal{L}\,\sigma_{\text{inel}}}{f_{\text{BC}}}, where \sigma_{\text{inel}} is the inelastic proton-proton cross section and f_{\text{BC}} is the bunch-crossing rate, the n_b f of Week 2.

Every extra collision adds tracks and energy to the detector that have nothing to do with the event being studied.

Note

Worked example. The slides’ table of detector challenges gives, for the LHC, \mathcal{L} = 10^{34} cm^{-2}s^{-1}, \sigma_{\text{inel}} = 80 mb and f_{\text{BC}} = 31.6 MHz. With 1\ \text{mb} = 10^{-27} cm^2, \mu = \frac{(10^{34})(80\times10^{-27})}{31.6\times10^{6}} \approx 25. For FCC-hh the same table gives \mathcal{L} = 30\times10^{34} cm^{-2}s^{-1}, \sigma_{\text{inel}} = 103 mb and f_{\text{BC}} = 32.5 MHz, which gives \mu \approx 950. Both match the pileup row of that table. Another slide quotes about 1000 for the same FCC-hh configuration, the same value rounded.

The bunch-crossing rate is lower than the 40 MHz that a 25 ns spacing suggests because not every bunch slot around the ring is filled.

The same table lists the other detector challenges that grow with energy and luminosity. The rate of charged tracks rises from about 59 GHz at the LHC to about 3900 GHz at FCC-hh. The total ionising dose expected at 2.5 cm from the beam rises from about 1.3 MGy to about 270 MGy. A detector at FCC-hh would have to separate about a thousand collisions per crossing and survive two hundred times the radiation dose of an LHC detector.

Electron-proton colliders

The last option in the slides collides an electron beam with a proton beam. The proposals add a 60 GeV electron accelerator to an existing or future proton ring: the LHeC with the LHC’s 7 TeV protons, and FCC-he with 50 TeV protons.

For two head-on beams of energies E_1 and E_2 much larger than the masses, the centre-of-mass energy from Week 2 reduces to s \approx 4E_1E_2.

Note

Worked example. For LHeC, \sqrt{s} \approx 2\sqrt{(60\ \text{GeV})(7000\ \text{GeV})} \approx 1.3 TeV. For FCC-he, \sqrt{s} \approx 2\sqrt{(60)(50\,000)}\ \text{GeV} \approx 3.5 TeV. Both match the slides’ table.

Summary

Note

Key takeaways from Week 3:

  • Bending costs energy. A relativistic charge on an arc radiates P_\gamma \propto \gamma^4/\rho^2 and loses U_0 \propto \gamma^4/\rho per turn, which the RF must replace every turn.
  • Mass decides the loss. At fixed energy and radius the loss scales as 1/m^4: muons radiate about 10^{9} times less than electrons, and protons about 10^{13} times less.
  • Three ways forward. Linear electron colliders (ILC, CLIC) avoid bending but use each bunch once, so they need nanometre beams. Hadron colliders (HE-LHC, FCC-hh) bend protons but deliver only a fraction of \sqrt{s} to each parton collision. Muon colliders are point-like and can be bent, but the muons decay.
  • Linac length. The accelerating gradient sets the length of a linear collider: CLIC at 100 MV/m needs at least 15 km of structure for each 1.5 TeV beam.
  • Hadron rings still radiate. At FCC-hh the synchrotron power reaches megawatts per beam, intercepted by beam screens above the magnet temperature.
  • Pileup grows with luminosity. \mu = \mathcal{L}\sigma_{\text{inel}}/f_{\text{BC}} is about 25 at the LHC and about 950 at FCC-hh.
