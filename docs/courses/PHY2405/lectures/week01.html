Week 1: What an Experiment in High Energy Physics Is

Week 1: What an Experiment in High Energy Physics Is

Overview. The first week does not introduce a technique. It sets out what kind of subject this is, what every experiment in it is made of, and why the course covers both collider experiments and experiments buried deep underground. Two sets of material come together: an introduction to particle physics experiments in general, and an introduction to the second half of the course, which covers experiments that do not use a collider. Later weeks take each piece in turn. Week 2 starts with the accelerator, and its two main quantities, beam energy and luminosity, first appear here as the accelerator’s contribution to an experiment.

The running examples are the ones the handouts use: Rutherford’s scattering experiment set beside the CDF experiment at the Tevatron, the LEP ring with its four experiments, and an underground dark matter experiment at SNOLAB.

What high energy physics is

What is the subject for? The handout gives a plain answer.

Definition. High energy physics is the discovery and measurement of the fundamental constituents of matter and their basic interactions.

The handout adds a view about method. Physics is concerned with the universe we live in, and experiments are how we look at it. On this view, ideas with no connection to experimental observation are not physics. Theory alone belongs with mathematics and philosophy, and experiment alone belongs with engineering and computer science. Physics needs both, and this course is about the experimental side.

The handout sums up a high energy physics experiment in a sentence attributed to D. C. Bailey (circa 1990): “smash things together, see what flies out, figure out what happened.” The next section turns that sentence into a list of parts.

Anatomy of an experiment

What does every particle physics experiment need, from the earliest to the largest?

Definition. The handout lists five parts of what it calls the archetypal particle physics experiment.

  • The beam is a stream of particles that is aimed at something.
  • The target is what the beam hits. In a collider it is the other beam.
  • The detector records what comes out of the collision.
  • Data acquisition (DAQ) is the electronics and computing that read the detector out and store the data.
  • Analysis is the work of turning the recorded data into a measurement.

A sixth item, the team of people who do all this, is listed alongside them.

Figure 1. The five parts of an experiment, in the order the particles and then the information pass through them.

One structure at two scales

The handout fills in the five parts for two experiments a century apart. The first is Rutherford’s, in which alpha particles from radium were scattered from a thin metal foil, the Geiger-Marsden experiment. The second is CDF, the Collider Detector at Fermilab, at the Tevatron.

Part Rutherford CDF
Team Rutherford, Geiger, Marsden A cast of thousands
Beam Alpha particles from radium, about 5 MeV Protons and antiprotons from the Tevatron, 900 GeV
Target A foil (gold or silver) The oncoming proton or antiproton beam
Detector A zinc sulfide screen and an eyeball A hermetic detector (the handout says 10 000 tons; other sources give about 5000 tons)
DAQ A pencil CAMAC and Fastbus electronics, workstations, microprocessors, tapes
Analysis A student and a pencil About 100 students, plus workstations, IBMs and Crays

The table shows what changed and what did not. Energy went up by a factor of about 2\times10^{5} (from about 5 MeV to 900 GeV, per beam). The record went from a person watching a screen to an electronic system that must decide what to keep. The five-part structure is the same in both columns. The word hermetic in the CDF row means that the detector surrounds the collision point as completely as possible, so that little escapes unseen.

Accelerators, detectors and analysis

The five parts group into three areas of work, each with its own figure of merit. The handout gives a rough share of the effort for each.

Area Figure of merit Rough share of the effort
Accelerators and beams Energy E and luminosity \mathcal{L} 25%
Detectors Resolution, written \sigma/p 50%
Analysis Signal to background, S/B 25%

The handout marks the analysis share as the part where theory enters. The subscripts in the resolution entry are hard to read in the scan, so we take \sigma/p to mean the spread of a measured momentum relative to the momentum itself.

Definition. The luminosity \mathcal{L} of a collider is the factor that turns a cross section \sigma (an effective area for a process) into an interaction rate: \text{rate} = \mathcal{L}\,\sigma .

A higher E opens up heavier particles, and a higher \mathcal{L} makes rare processes happen often enough to record. Week 2 works out what limits both.

Definition. The resolution of a detector is how finely it can measure a quantity. For momentum, \sigma/p is the typical size of the measurement error \sigma divided by the momentum p being measured. A smaller number is a better measurement.

Definition. Signal is the kind of event we are looking for, and background is everything else that looks similar enough to be mistaken for it. The ratio S/B says how prominent the signal is against the background.

Every later topic in the course improves one of these three: a better beam, a better detector, or a cleaner separation of signal from background.

Machines and detectors used as examples

Where do the parts sit in a real facility? The handout shows two.

LEP and DELPHI

The LEP ring was an electron-positron collider at CERN. The handout’s layout of its underground structures shows a single ring with four experimental halls, at points 2, 4, 6 and 8, holding the experiments L3, Aleph, Opal and Delphi. The other points around the ring are access points.

The handout also gives cross-sections of the DELPHI detector. Read from the beam pipe outward, the barrel region contains these layers.

Note

Figure 2. The barrel layers of DELPHI. Each colour marks a role: green for tracking, purple for particle identification, orange for energy measurement, yellow for the magnet and its scintillator, blue for the muon system.

The layers are ordered by what a particle meets first. Tracking layers close to the beam record where charged particles go. A magnet bends those tracks, which is what lets their momentum be measured. Calorimeters, which absorb particles and measure their energy, sit outside the tracking, and muon chambers form the outermost layer. The handout also labels forward detectors near the beam pipe in each direction, a small angle tagger (SAT), and the superconducting quadrupoles of the machine itself. Each of these systems has a week of its own later in the course, and the acronyms (TPC, RICH, HPC) are defined there.

CDF at the Tevatron

The CDF pages show the same design in a proton-antiproton experiment: a solenoid magnet and tracking chambers in the middle, calorimeters around them, and muon systems on the outside. The cross-section labels a silicon vertex detector and a vertex tracking chamber closest to the interaction point, then a central tracking chamber, the solenoid, and the central electromagnetic and hadron calorimeters. Plug and forward calorimeters cover the regions nearer the beam.

The CDF cross-section marks the extent of its detector regions by a quantity called \eta, with labels at \eta = 0, 0.9, 2.4 and 4.2, and the forward calorimeters at the largest value. The handout does not say what \eta is, so we add it here.

Definition. The pseudorapidity \eta of a direction is \eta = -\ln\tan\frac{\theta}{2}, where \theta is the angle between that direction and the beam axis.

Perpendicular to the beam (\theta = 90^\circ) gives \eta = 0, and directions closer to the beam give larger \eta. The formula is a way to label angles so that detector coverage is easy to state.

Note

Worked example. The three CDF values correspond to these angles from the beam axis, using \theta = 2\arctan(e^{-\eta}):

  • \eta = 0.9 gives \theta \approx 44^\circ.
  • \eta = 2.4 gives \theta \approx 10^\circ.
  • \eta = 4.2 gives \theta \approx 1.7^\circ.

The forward calorimeters therefore look almost straight down the beam pipe.

The non-collider half of the course

The second instructor’s introduction opens with a question: why two professors this term? The slides answer with a pair of pictures. On one side is a collider experiment, ATLAS at the LHC. On the other is SuperCDMS at SNOLAB, which sits 2 km underground. The course is called eHEP on the slides, and the slides then ask whether these are really all High Energy Physics.

An energy ladder

The slides answer by showing a measurement from each kind of experiment, each at a very different energy.

Note

Figure 3. The energy attached to each measurement on the slides. The horizontal axis is logarithmic, so each tick is a factor of 1000 in energy.

Each marker refers to a measurement the second half of the course discusses.

  • 125 GeV. A collider measurement: the ATLAS plot shows a bump in a mass spectrum at about 125 GeV, which is the Higgs boson.
  • Below 10 GeV. The plot for dark matter direct detection has the dark matter particle’s mass on its horizontal axis. Dark matter is invisible matter whose presence is inferred from its gravity, and direct detection tries to catch one of its particles scattering in a detector. The slide’s arrow marks the mass region below about 10 GeV. The candidate particle in this kind of search is a weakly interacting massive particle (WIMP).
  • About 3 MeV. The plot for neutrinoless double beta decay shows a continuous spectrum for ordinary double beta decay, and marks where a sharp line for the neutrinoless version would appear at the high-energy end, at about 3 MeV in this example.
  • keV. The plot for coherent elastic neutrino-nucleus scattering (CEvNS) shows a spectrum of nuclear recoil energies that reaches the keV scale. In CEvNS a neutrino scatters from a nucleus as a whole.
  • eV. The last plot is a phonon energy spectrum at the eV scale, from a detector that reads out vibrations in a crystal.

These short descriptions are for orientation only. The non-collider experiments are described in later weeks, and the slide plots are not reproduced here.

What is shared and what differs

The slide called “So…” places the two kinds of experiment side by side.

Collider experiments want Underground experiments want
Higher energy Better energy resolution
Higher luminosity A bigger detector
Faster detection Lower background

Underneath the differences, the slide lists common threads:

  • particle interaction with materials,
  • particle identification and signal-background discrimination,
  • the trigger, data acquisition and event reconstruction.

Those threads are why one course covers both. A detector that measures a 125 GeV photon pair and a detector that measures an eV-scale crystal signal both depend on how particles deposit energy in matter, on telling signal from background, and on reading out and reconstructing events. The second instructor’s part of the course covers three non-collider topics: dark matter direct detection, CEvNS, and neutrinoless double beta decay. The slide says the selection is not exhaustive and is limited to what the instructor knows well.

Summary

Note

Key takeaways from Week 1:

  • What the subject is. High energy physics is the discovery and measurement of the fundamental constituents of matter and their basic interactions, and this course is about the experimental side.
  • Five parts. Every experiment, from Rutherford’s to CDF’s, has a beam, a target, a detector, data acquisition and analysis, run by a team.
  • Three figures of merit. Accelerators are judged by energy E and luminosity \mathcal{L}, detectors by resolution \sigma/p, and analysis by signal to background S/B.
  • Detectors are layered. Tracking sits closest to the beam, then particle identification, calorimeters and, outermost, muon chambers, as in DELPHI and CDF.
  • Two halves, one toolkit. Collider and underground experiments span eV to hundreds of GeV, and they share particle interaction with matter, signal and background discrimination, and trigger and readout.

Quick self-check

If you can explain what each part of an experiment does, and why colliders and underground experiments still share a toolkit, using only “beam,” “detector,” “resolution,” “luminosity,” and “signal and background,” you have understood the core of Week 1.
