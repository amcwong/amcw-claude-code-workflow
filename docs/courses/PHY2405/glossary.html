Week 1

  • High energy physics. The discovery and measurement of the fundamental constituents of matter and their basic interactions. See What high energy physics is.
  • Beam and target. The particles aimed at something, and the thing they hit; in a collider the target is the other beam. See Anatomy of an experiment.
  • Detector. The part of an experiment that records what comes out of a collision. See Anatomy of an experiment.
  • Data acquisition (DAQ). The electronics and computing that read the detector out and store the data. See Anatomy of an experiment.
  • Hermetic detector. A detector that surrounds the collision point as completely as possible. See One structure at two scales.
  • Luminosity (\mathcal{L}). The factor that turns a cross section into an interaction rate; developed in Week 2. See Accelerators, detectors and analysis.
  • Cross section (\sigma). An effective area for a process. See Accelerators, detectors and analysis.
  • Resolution. How finely a detector measures a quantity; for momentum, \sigma/p. See Accelerators, detectors and analysis.
  • Signal and background. The kind of event we look for, and the events that look similar enough to be mistaken for it. See Accelerators, detectors and analysis.
  • Pseudorapidity (\eta). A label for the angle from the beam axis, \eta=-\ln\tan(\theta/2). See CDF at the Tevatron.
  • Dark matter direct detection. Searching for a dark matter particle scattering in a detector. See An energy ladder.
  • WIMP. A weakly interacting massive particle, the candidate in such searches. See An energy ladder.
  • Neutrinoless double beta decay. A hypothetical decay searched for as a sharp line at the end of the double beta decay spectrum. See An energy ladder.
  • CEvNS. Coherent elastic neutrino-nucleus scattering, in which a neutrino scatters from a nucleus as a whole. See An energy ladder.
  • Phonon. A quantum of vibration in a crystal, read out by some low-energy detectors. See An energy ladder.

Week 2

  • Dipole. A magnet with a uniform field that bends the beam onto a circular arc. See Bending with a dipole.
  • Bending radius (\rho). The radius of the arc a beam of momentum p follows in a field B, \rho = p/0.3B in GeV/c, tesla and metres. See Bending with a dipole.
  • Magnetic rigidity (B\rho). The beam’s momentum expressed as a magnetic quantity. See Bending with a dipole.
  • Quadrupole. A four-pole magnet whose field grows linearly with distance from the axis, focusing in one plane and defocusing in the other. See Focusing with a quadrupole.
  • Field gradient (g). The rate \partial B_y/\partial x at which a quadrupole’s field grows with distance from the axis. See Focusing with a quadrupole.
  • Weak and strong focusing. Weak focusing uses one magnet to bend and focus; strong focusing alternates focusing and defocusing quadrupoles. See Weak and strong focusing.
  • FODO lattice. A repeating sequence of focusing (F), drift (O) and defocusing (D) elements that gives net focusing. See Weak and strong focusing.
  • Focal length (F). The distance at which a thin lens brings a parallel ray to the axis. See The quadrupole as a thin lens.
  • Reference orbit. The ideal closed path of a particle of the design momentum. See Describing motion about the reference orbit.
  • Focusing strength (K). The field gradient divided by the momentum, K=(e/p)\,\partial B_y/\partial x. See The equations of motion.
  • Transfer matrix. A matrix that maps a particle’s (x, x') at one position to its value further downstream. See Transfer matrices.
  • Hill’s equation. u''+K(s)u=0 with K periodic around the ring. See Beam envelope and emittance.
  • Betatron oscillation. The transverse oscillation of a particle about the reference orbit. See Beam envelope and emittance.
  • Betatron function (\beta(s)). The machine function that sets the beam envelope, \sigma=\sqrt{\epsilon\beta}. See Beam envelope and emittance.
  • Courant-Snyder parameters (\alpha,\beta,\gamma). The three functions of s that describe the shape and orientation of the phase-space ellipse. See Beam envelope and emittance.
  • Emittance (\epsilon). The size of a beam in transverse phase space, the value of the Courant-Snyder invariant. See Beam envelope and emittance.
  • Tune (\nu). The number of betatron oscillations per revolution. See Tune and resonances.
  • Resonance. A tune condition p\nu_x+q\nu_y= integer at which small errors add up turn after turn. See Tune and resonances.
  • Luminosity (\mathcal{L}). The factor that converts a cross section into an interaction rate. See Luminosity.
  • \beta^*. The value of the betatron function at the collision point. See Luminosity in terms of emittance.
  • Tune shift (\Delta\nu). The change in tune caused by extra focusing, in colliders mainly from the other beam. See The beam-beam force and the tune shift.
  • Centre-of-mass energy (\sqrt{s}). The energy available for making new particles. See Why colliders?.
  • RF (radio-frequency) cavity. A resonant cavity whose alternating longitudinal field accelerates the beam. See The basics of acceleration.
  • Harmonic number (h). The number of places around the ring where a particle can arrive in time with the RF field. See Orbit length and RF frequency.
  • Synchronous phase (\phi_s). The RF phase at which the reference particle arrives. See Phase stability and transition.
  • Transition energy (\gamma_T). The energy at which the sign of the arrival-time change with momentum flips. See Phase stability and transition.
  • RF bucket and separatrix. The region of stable longitudinal motion and the curve that bounds it. See Phase space of stable oscillations.
  • Synchrotron oscillation. The oscillation of a particle in energy and phase about the reference particle. See Synchrotron oscillations.

Week 3

  • Synchrotron radiation. The radiation a relativistic charged particle emits when its path is bent, as in a dipole. See Synchrotron radiation.
  • Larmor formula. The radiated power of an accelerated charge; for a bent relativistic particle P_\gamma \propto \beta^4\gamma^4/\rho^2. See Synchrotron radiation.
  • Lorentz factor (\gamma). The ratio E/mc^2 of a particle’s energy to its rest energy. See Synchrotron radiation.
  • Energy loss per turn (U_0). The energy a particle radiates on one trip around a ring, which the RF must restore, U_0 \propto \gamma^4/\rho. See Energy lost per turn.
  • Parton. A quark or gluon inside a proton; a proton collision is really a collision of one parton from each proton. See Electrons, protons and muons.
  • Linear collider. A collider that accelerates two beams in straight linacs and collides each bunch once. See Linear colliders.
  • Accelerating gradient. The energy a particle gains per metre of accelerating structure, in MV/m. See Linear colliders.
  • Beamstrahlung. Radiation a particle emits in the field of the oncoming bunch, which spreads the collision energies below the nominal value. See The energy spread of the collisions.
  • Muon collider. A collider of muons, point-like like electrons but radiating about 10^9 times less; its muons decay with a rest lifetime of about 2.2 \mus. See Muon colliders.
  • Time dilation. The lengthening of a moving particle’s lifetime in the laboratory to \gamma\tau. See Muon colliders.
  • Muon beam halo. Background in a muon collider detector from the products of muons decaying near the interaction region. See Muon colliders.
  • Beam screen. A liner inside the beam pipe of a superconducting ring that intercepts synchrotron radiation at a temperature where cooling is cheaper. See Synchrotron radiation in a hadron ring.
  • Critical photon energy. A characteristic energy of the synchrotron photons a beam emits. See Synchrotron radiation in a hadron ring.
  • Pileup (\mu). The extra collisions in the same bunch crossing as the one of interest, with mean \mu = \mathcal{L}\sigma_{\text{inel}}/f_{\text{BC}}. See Pileup.

Week 4

  • Rare event search. An experiment looking for a process expected only a few times per year in tens or hundreds of kilograms of detector, such as WIMP scattering, neutrinoless double beta decay or CEvNS. See Rare event searches.
  • Exposure. Detector mass times live time, for example in kg·day; the number of events of a rare process grows in proportion to it. See Background and the DRU.
  • Region of interest (ROI). The window of deposited energy in which the signal is expected. See Background and the DRU.
  • DRU. The differential rate unit, one event per keV per kg per day, used to compare background levels between experiments. See Background and the DRU.
  • Noise (versus background). Continuous fluctuations, usually not from particle interactions; background, by contrast, gives pulses from particle interactions. See Noise and background.
  • Reduced mass (m_r). m_\chi m_N/(m_\chi + m_N), the mass that sets the kinematics of a WIMP-nucleus collision. See The signal rate.
  • Form factor (F). The factor by which scattering from a nucleus falls below coherent scattering once the momentum transfer resolves the nucleus’s size. See The nuclear structure factor.
  • Spin-independent scattering. WIMP-nucleus scattering in which the amplitudes from all nucleons add coherently, so the cross section grows as A^2. See The particle physics factor.
  • Energy threshold. The smallest deposited energy a detector can reliably record. See Exposure and threshold.
  • Electron recoil (ER) and nuclear recoil (NR). A scatter from an atomic electron (gammas, betas) or from a nucleus (WIMPs, neutrons, CEvNS). See Signal against background.
  • Cosmogenic background. Background induced by cosmic rays, such as fast neutrons from muon spallation. See Signal against background.
  • Material assay. Measuring the radioactive contamination of a material, often at parts per billion, before it is used in a detector. See Step 1: radiopure materials.
  • Metres water equivalent (m.w.e.). Depth underground expressed as the depth of water that would stop muons equally well. See Step 2: going underground.
  • Passive and active shielding. Material that absorbs radiation (lead, polyethylene, water), and detectors that tag it so events can be vetoed (muon and neutron vetoes). See Step 3: shielding.
  • Ionization yield (Y). The ionization energy of an event divided by its phonon energy, near 1 for electron recoils and lower for nuclear recoils. See Particle identification from the detector response.
  • Surface events and fiducialization. Events near a detector surface with incomplete charge collection, removed by keeping only an inner (fiducial) volume. See Particle identification from the detector response.
  • Annual modulation. The yearly variation in a WIMP signal caused by the Earth’s orbit through the dark matter halo. See Astrophysical modulation.
  • Noise spectral density. Noise per \sqrt{\text{Hz}} of bandwidth, in V/\sqrt{\text{Hz}} or A/\sqrt{\text{Hz}}. See Reading a noise spectrum.
  • Johnson–Nyquist noise. Thermal noise in a resistor, v_n = \sqrt{4k_BTR\Delta f}, reduced by cooling. See Types of noise.
  • Shot noise. Noise from the discreteness of the charges in a current, i_n = \sqrt{2Iq\Delta f}. See Types of noise.
  • Heat capacity (C). The energy needed to raise an object’s temperature by one kelvin; at low temperature \propto T for electrons and \propto T^3 for phonons. See Specific heat.
  • Thermal conductivity (\kappa). The constant in Fourier’s law \dot q = -\kappa\nabla T; the heat flow along a rod is (A/L)\int\kappa\,dT. See Thermal conductivity.
  • Kapitza resistance. The thermal resistance of a boundary between two materials, from an acoustic mismatch for phonons. See Thermal conductivity.
  • Wiedemann–Franz law. For a metal, \kappa/\sigma = L_0T, linking thermal and electrical conductivity. See The Wiedemann–Franz law.
  • Residual-resistance ratio (RRR). R_{300\,\text{K}}/R_{4\,\text{K}}, a measure of a metal’s purity. See The Wiedemann–Franz law.
  • Thermal conductance (G) and time constant (\tau). G is the heat flow per kelvin through a link; an object of heat capacity C relaxes to its bath with \tau = C/G. See The cooling time constant.
  • Heat sinking. Thermally anchoring a wire or part to an intermediate cryostat stage so its heat is removed there. See Thermal conductivity and Keeping heat out: wiring.
  • Wet and dry cooling. Cooling with cryogenic liquids (nitrogen, helium) versus closed-cycle cryocoolers driven by electricity. See How to cool things down.
  • Pulse tube cryocooler. A closed-cycle refrigerator with no moving cold parts, typically in two stages near 50 K and 3 K. See The pulse tube cryocooler.
  • Dilution refrigerator. A continuous refrigerator that cools by moving helium-3 across the phase boundary in a helium-3/helium-4 mixture, reaching about 10 mK. See The dilution refrigerator.
  • Mixing chamber and still. The coldest stage of a dilution refrigerator, where the cooling happens, and the stage near 0.7 K where helium-3 is evaporated and pumped away. See How dilution cooling works.
  • Adiabatic demagnetization refrigerator (ADR). A single-shot refrigerator that cools a paramagnetic salt by ramping down a magnetic field. See The adiabatic demagnetization refrigerator.

Week 5

  • Mean free path (\lambda). The mean distance a particle travels between interactions, \lambda = 1/\sigma\rho with \rho the number density of targets; the survival probability is e^{-x/\lambda}. See Interaction probability and mean free path.
  • Areal density (mass thickness). Mass density times thickness, in g/cm², the amount of material a particle crosses. See Thickness in g/cm².
  • Mass stopping power. Energy loss per unit areal density, \frac{1}{\rho}\frac{dE}{dx} in MeV cm²/g. See Thickness in g/cm².
  • Ionization. Removing an electron from an atom, leaving a free electron and a positive ion. See Ionization by heavy charged particles.
  • Delta ray (δ-electron). An electron knocked out with enough energy to ionize further atoms, leaving a blob of extra ionization by the track. See Ionization by heavy charged particles.
  • Stopping power (-dE/dx). The average energy a particle loses per unit path length. See The classical derivation.
  • Impact parameter (b). The closest distance between a target electron and the incident particle’s straight-line path. See The classical derivation.
  • Primary ionization (n_{\text{prim}}). The number of ionizing collisions the incident particle itself makes per unit length, a few to a few tens per cm in gases. See Primary ionization in a gas.
  • Bethe-Bloch formula. The mean energy loss of a heavy charged particle by ionization, \propto (Z/A)(z^2/\beta^2)[\ln(2m_ec^2\beta^2\gamma^2/I) - \beta^2 - \delta/2]. See The Bethe-Bloch formula.
  • Mean excitation potential (I). The material constant inside the Bethe-Bloch logarithm, an average of the atomic excitation energies. See The Bethe-Bloch formula.
  • Relativistic rise. The slow logarithmic increase of dE/dx above its minimum near \beta\gamma \approx 3–4. See Reading the energy-loss curve.
  • Density effect (\delta). The reduction of energy loss at high \gamma because the polarized medium screens the particle’s field, giving a plateau near \gamma \approx I/\hbar\omega_p. See The density effect.
  • Plasma frequency (\omega_p). The natural frequency of collective electron oscillation in a medium, (n_0e^2/\epsilon_0 m_e)^{1/2}. See The density effect.
  • Minimum ionizing particle (MIP). A particle whose mean energy loss is near the Bethe-Bloch minimum, about 2 MeV cm²/g, such as a cosmic-ray muon. See Minimum ionizing particles and mixtures.
  • Landau distribution. The skewed distribution of energy loss in a thin layer, with a long tail from δ-rays; its most probable value lies below the mean. See Fluctuations: the Landau distribution.
  • Multiple scattering. The random change of a charged particle’s direction from many small Coulomb deflections by nuclei, with projected width \theta_0 \approx (13.6\ \text{MeV}/\beta cp)\,z\sqrt{x/X_0}. See Multiple scattering.
  • Bremsstrahlung. Photon emission by a charged particle deflected in the field of a nucleus; the main energy loss of high-energy electrons. See Electrons: bremsstrahlung and the radiation length.
  • Critical energy (E_c). The electron energy at which ionization and bremsstrahlung losses are equal (about 20 MeV in copper). See Electrons: bremsstrahlung and the radiation length.
  • Radiation length (X_0). The thickness, usually in g/cm², over which a high-energy electron keeps 1/e of its energy; roughly \propto A/Z^2. See Radiation length.
  • Photoelectric effect, Compton scattering, pair production. The main photon interactions, dominant at low energy, near 1 MeV, and above a few MeV respectively. See Photon interactions.
  • W value. The mean energy deposited per electron-ion pair, about 30 eV in gases; \langle N\rangle = E/W. See How many charges? The W value.
  • Band gap (E_g). The energy between the valence and conduction bands, about 1 eV in a semiconductor. See Band structure in brief.
  • Electron-hole pair. A conduction electron plus the vacancy (hole) it leaves in the valence band, the unit of ionization in a semiconductor. See Band structure in brief.
  • Pair-creation energy (\epsilon). The semiconductor W value: 3.62 eV in Si at 300 K, 2.96 eV in Ge at 77 K. See The pair-creation energy in silicon and germanium.
  • Fano factor (F). The ratio of the variance of the number of pairs to its Poisson value, \sigma^2 = F\langle N\rangle; about 0.13 in silicon. See The Fano factor.
  • Quenching factor (Q). The ionization (or light) energy of a recoil divided by its true recoil energy, Q = E_I/E_R; 1 for electron recoils and below 1 for nuclear recoils. See Nuclear recoils and quenching.
  • Ionization yield, stricter sense (Y(E_R)). The quenching factor for the ionization channel as a function of true recoil energy; the same as the Week 4 yield Y whenever the phonon channel measures the true recoil energy, and also used loosely for the number of pairs. See Nuclear recoils and quenching.
  • Neganov-Trofimov-Luke (NTL) phonons. Extra phonons produced as charges drift across a biased crystal, adding n_{eh}eV to the phonon energy. See Why the yield matters.
  • Birks’ law. Light yield per unit length dL/dx = S\,(dE/dx)/(1 + kB\,dE/dx), which saturates for densely ionizing particles. See Scintillation yield: Birks’ law.
  • Lindhard model. The standard analytical model of nuclear-recoil ionization yield, built on five simplifying approximations. See The Lindhard model and its measurement.
  • Migdal effect. Rare emission of an atomic electron when a nucleus is struck suddenly, giving unquenched ionization. See Beyond the averages.

Week 6

  • Wire chamber. A gas detector in which charged tracks ionize the gas, the electrons drift to thin anode wires, and an avalanche near each wire amplifies the signal. See The wire chamber and its three stages.
  • Diffusion coefficient (D). The constant setting how fast a cloud of charges spreads by random collisions; the width grows as \sigma_x^2 = 2Dt. See Diffusion without a field.
  • Drift velocity (w). The average velocity of a charge along the electric field, much smaller than its thermal speed; for ions w = \mu E/P. See Drift in an electric field.
  • Mobility (\mu). The constant relating drift velocity to field (and pressure) for a given charge carrier and gas. See Drift in an electric field.
  • Saturated drift velocity. A drift velocity nearly independent of the field over a range of fields, giving a stable distance-time relation. See Drift in an electric field.
  • \sigma(1\,\text{cm}). The diffusion width after 1 cm of drift, \sqrt{2D\cdot1\,\text{cm}/w}, the figure of merit for a drift gas; \sigma_x = \sigma(1\,\text{cm})\sqrt{x}. See The diffusion limit.
  • First Townsend coefficient (\alpha). The number of ionizing collisions a drifting electron makes per unit length; the gain is M = \exp\int\alpha\,dx. See Avalanche multiplication.
  • Gas gain (M). The multiplication factor of an avalanche, the number of electrons reaching the wire per primary electron. See Avalanche multiplication.
  • Proportional counter. A gas detector run at a gain where the pulse is proportional to the primary ionization. See The proportional tube.
  • Induced signal. The voltage on an electrode caused by charges moving in its field; in a wire chamber about 99% comes from the ions drifting away from the wire. See How the signal forms.
  • Total ion drift time (T) and t_{1/2}. The time for avalanche ions to reach the cathode (hundreds of μs), and the much shorter time, T\,a/(a+b), in which half of the signal arrives. See The time development of the pulse.
  • Pulse shaping. Terminating or filtering the readout so that only the fast early part of the signal is kept, giving a short pulse. See Shaping the pulse.
  • Time slewing and constant-fraction discrimination. The earlier threshold crossing of larger pulses, and the timing method that removes it by triggering at a fixed fraction of each pulse’s peak. See Shaping the pulse.
  • Quencher. A polyatomic gas added to a noble gas to absorb avalanche photons and allow stable high gain. See Operating a gas detector.
  • Multiwire proportional chamber (MWPC). A plane of anode wires in a shared gas volume between cathodes, recording which wire was hit. See Multiwire proportional chambers.
  • Pitch and s/\sqrt{12}. The spacing s between readout wires or strips, and the resulting rms resolution of a binary readout. See Multiwire proportional chambers.
  • Ghost hits. False points from wrongly pairing coordinates of several tracks measured by crossed planes. See Chamber systems and cathode readout.
  • Drift chamber. A wire chamber that also measures the electrons’ arrival time, giving the distance from the wire as x = wt. See Drift chambers.
  • Left-right ambiguity. A single drift wire measures the distance to the track but not the side it passed on. See The left-right ambiguity.
  • Stereo layers. Wire or strip layers tilted by a small angle, giving the coordinate along the wire with \sigma_z \approx \sigma_{r\phi}/\theta_{\text{stereo}}. See The third coordinate and the TPC.
  • Time projection chamber (TPC). A large gas volume in which charges drift to instrumented endplates; drift time gives z, endplate pads give r and \phi. See The third coordinate and the TPC.
  • Transverse momentum and dip angle. p_T = p\cos\lambda, the momentum component perpendicular to the solenoid field, with \lambda the angle of the track out of the transverse plane; p_T\,[\text{GeV}/c] = 0.3\,B\,[\text{T}]\,R\,[\text{m}]. See Measuring momentum.
  • Sagitta (s). The distance between the midpoints of an arc and its chord, s \approx L^2/8R, the quantity a tracker measures to find curvature. See Measuring momentum.
  • Momentum resolution (\delta p/p). The fractional momentum uncertainty: a curvature term growing as p_T/(BL^2), a dip-angle term, and a momentum-independent multiple-scattering term. See Momentum resolution.
  • Lorentz angle (\alpha_L). The angle between the electron drift and the electric field when \mathbf E \perp \mathbf B, with \tan\alpha_L = \omega\tau. See Electron drift in electric and magnetic fields.
  • Impact parameter, tracking sense. The distance of closest approach of a reconstructed track to the beam line or primary vertex; distinct from the Week 5 collision impact parameter. See Impact-parameter resolution.
