Week 1

  • Computational imaging. Optics, sensing, and computation co-designed together, rather than a fixed lens and passive sensor with processing bolted on afterward. See Week 1, §1.1.
  • Aperture. The opening that controls how much light enters an optical system; the pupil in the eye, an adjustable blade opening in a camera lens. See Week 1, §1.2.
  • Image sensor. The electronic chip in a digital camera that converts incoming light into an electrical signal; the retina’s biological counterpart. See Week 1, §1.2.
  • Accommodation. The eye’s ability to change focus by physically changing the lens’s shape via the ciliary muscle. See Week 1, §1.2 and §1.6.
  • Fovea. The small, densely cone-packed pit in the retina responsible for sharp, colour vision; everything outside it is lower-resolution peripheral vision. See Week 1, §1.3.
  • Metamerism. Two physically different light spectra producing the same three cone responses, so they look identical; the reason RGB displays and cameras do not need to reproduce a true physical spectrum. See Week 1, §1.4.
  • Bayer colour filter array. A repeating 2×2 red/green/green/blue grid of filters placed over a sensor so each pixel measures only one colour channel; full-colour images are reconstructed (demosaicked) from this mosaic. See Week 1, §1.5.
  • Refractive error (myopia / hyperopia / astigmatism). Conditions where light rays converge in front of, behind, or never at a single point on the retina, corrected with a diverging, converging, or cylindrical lens respectively. See Week 1, §1.6.
  • Visual angle. The angular size an object subtends at the eye, depending on both its physical size and its distance; the basis for acuity, dpi, and spatial-frequency units throughout this week. See Week 1, §1.8.
  • dpi / ppi. Dots (or pixels) per inch: a density measure, the reciprocal of the physical size of one resolvable pixel, p = 2d\tan(\alpha/2). See Week 1, §1.9.
  • Dynamic range. The ratio between the brightest and darkest signal a system can represent, in f-stops or orders of magnitude. See Week 1, §1.10.
  • Weber contrast / Michelson contrast. Two contrast definitions: Weber for a single feature on a uniform background, Michelson for a periodic pattern with no single background. See Week 1, §1.11.
  • Spatial frequency. How rapidly brightness oscillates across space, measured in cycles per pixel (image frequency, fixed to the file), cycles per physical distance (fixed once printed), or cycles per degree of visual angle (cpd, viewer-dependent). See Week 1, §1.12.
  • Contrast sensitivity function (CSF). Contrast sensitivity, the reciprocal of the minimum detectable contrast, plotted against spatial frequency in cpd; band-pass, peaking around 4-6 cpd and cutting off around 60 cpd. See Week 1, §1.12.
  • 2D Fourier transform (fft2). Decomposes an image into a sum of 2D sinusoidal gratings, one per spatial frequency (u,v); reversible via ifft2, losing nothing. See Week 1, §1.12.1.
  • fftshift / ifftshift. Convention that recenters an FFT’s zero-frequency (DC) component from the array corners to the array centre, and back. See Week 1, §1.12.1.
  • Convolution theorem. Multiplying two spectra in the frequency domain is equivalent to convolving the corresponding signals in the spatial domain; the basis for frequency-domain filtering. See Week 1, §1.12.1.
  • Low-pass / high-pass filter (frequency domain). A disc-shaped mask on an fftshift-ed spectrum that keeps frequencies below (low-pass) or above (high-pass) a cutoff radius. See Week 1, §1.12.1.
  • Hybrid image. An image formed by adding a low-pass-filtered photo to a high-pass-filtered photo, so its perceived content changes with viewing distance (Oliva, Torralba & Schyns, 2006). See Week 1, §1.13.
  • Binocular disparity / vergence. The difference between each eye’s view of a scene (disparity) and the inward eye rotation needed to fixate a near object (vergence); both are depth cues requiring two eyes. See Week 1, §1.14.
  • Vergence-accommodation conflict (VAC). The mismatch, on stereo displays, between the simulated depth the eyes verge to and the fixed physical screen distance they must accommodate to; the leading cause of stereo-3D/VR visual discomfort. See Week 1, §1.16.
  • Pinhole camera / camera obscura. A lensless imaging device: a light-tight box with a small aperture that projects an inverted image of the scene onto the opposite interior surface. See Week 1, §3.1.
  • Focal length (pinhole context). The distance from the pinhole to the screen; it sets image scale and field of view, with no lens involved. See Week 1, §3.1.
  • Diffraction. The spreading of a wave (light) as it passes through an aperture comparable in size to its wavelength; the reason a pinhole camera has an optimal, not minimal, diameter. See Week 1, §3.2.
  • Nyquist frequency. For a digital image, 0.5 cycles per pixel: the highest spatial frequency a pixel grid can represent without aliasing, from the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem. See Week 1, §3.3.
