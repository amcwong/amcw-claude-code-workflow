Week 1 practice: Visual Angle, Frequencies, and Pinholes

Overview. These problems drill Week 1. Try each stem before opening the worked solution. Numbers and setups are invented for practice; they are not homework answers. Notation matches the week chapter: \alpha is a visual angle, d is a viewing distance (or a pinhole diameter, stated each time), p is the physical size of one pixel, f is the pinhole-to-screen distance, and \lambda is a wavelength.

Problem 1: How big must a letter be?

An eye chart is hung on a corridor wall and read from 25 m away. On the Snellen chart convention, a row is just readable when the whole letter subtends 5 arcminutes and each stroke of the letter subtends 1 arcminute. How tall must a letter be, in millimetres? How thick is each stroke?

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 1, §1.2 (visual angle) and §1.11 (acuity).

The stem gives an angle and a distance and asks for a physical size, so we need the second form of the visual-angle relation, s = 2d\tan(\alpha/2).

For the whole letter, \alpha = 5' = 5/60\,° \approx 0.0833°, so \alpha/2 \approx 0.0417° \approx 7.27\times 10^{-4} rad. For an angle this small, \tan of the angle is essentially the angle in radians.

s = 2 \times 25\ \text{m} \times 7.27\times 10^{-4} \approx 0.0364\ \text{m}.

For a stroke, \alpha = 1' is one fifth of the letter’s angle, so the stroke is one fifth of the height. This matches the size proportionality for small angles in §1.2.

\text{stroke} \approx \frac{36.4\ \text{mm}}{5} \approx 7.3\ \text{mm}.

Answer: the letter is about 36 mm tall and each stroke is about 7.3 mm thick.

Problem 2: A monitor and its viewing distance

A desktop monitor is viewed from 60 cm. Taking the eye’s resolving limit as 1 arcminute, what pixel density (in ppi) makes the monitor look sharp at that distance? A cheaper monitor has 110 ppi. From what distance would it just reach the same sharpness?

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 1, §1.12 (the retina-display calculation). The pixel density is the reciprocal of the smallest resolvable pixel size, so we find p first.

Step 1, the pixel size at 60 cm:

p = 2d\tan\!\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right) = 2 \times 0.60\ \text{m} \times \tan(0.5') \approx 1.75\times 10^{-4}\ \text{m} = 0.175\ \text{mm}.

Step 2, convert to density. One inch is 25.4 mm:

\text{ppi} = \frac{25.4\ \text{mm}}{0.175\ \text{mm}} \approx 146.

For the second part, run the relation backwards. A 110 ppi screen has pixels of size p = 25.4/110 \approx 0.231 mm. Solving p = 2d\tan(\alpha/2) for d:

d = \frac{p}{2\tan(0.5')} \approx \frac{0.231\times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}}{2.91\times 10^{-4}} \approx 0.79\ \text{m}.

Coarser pixels need a longer distance so that each one subtends 1 arcminute.

Answer: about 146 ppi at 60 cm. The 110 ppi monitor reaches the same sharpness at about 79 cm.

Problem 3: One pattern, three rulers

A digital image contains a stripe pattern with a period of 10 pixels. It is printed at 200 dpi and viewed from 0.5 m.

  1. State the pattern’s frequency in cycles/pixel, cycles/inch, and cycles per degree.

  2. The contrast sensitivity function peaks near 5 cpd and falls to zero near 60 cpd. Where does the pattern sit on that curve at 0.5 m? Repeat for a viewer at 4 m.

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 1, §1.16 (the three rulers) and §1.15 (cpd and the CSF). Each conversion needs exactly one extra fact: dpi to leave the file, then the viewing distance to reach the eye.

  1. Step 1, the file’s own frequency:

\frac{1}{10\ \text{pixels/cycle}} = 0.1\ \text{cycles/pixel}.

Step 2, multiply by the print density:

0.1\ \text{cycles/pixel} \times 200\ \text{pixels/inch} = 20\ \text{cycles/inch}.

Step 3, find how many pixels fit in one degree. One pixel is 25.4/200 = 0.127 mm. One degree at 0.5 m covers 2 \times 0.5 \times \tan(0.5°) \approx 8.73 mm:

\text{pixels/degree} = \frac{8.73}{0.127} \approx 68.7.

Step 4, multiply:

0.1 \times 68.7 \approx 6.9\ \text{cpd}.

  1. At 0.5 m the pattern is at about 6.9 cpd, just above the 4–6 cpd peak, so it is highly visible.

At 4 m the distance is 8 times larger, so one degree covers 8 times as many pixels (about 550), and the pattern’s frequency is 8 times higher:

0.1 \times 550 \approx 55\ \text{cpd}.

That is just under the ~60 cpd cutoff, so the pattern would be barely visible. The file and the print did not change; only the fact in step 3 did.

Answer: 0.1 cycles/pixel, 20 cycles/inch, about 6.9 cpd at 0.5 m (near the peak) and about 55 cpd at 4 m (close to the cutoff).

Problem 4: Designing a pattern for a target frequency

A viewing setup puts 40 pixels into each degree of the viewer’s field of view.

  1. A pattern should appear at 8 cpd. What period, in pixels, must it have in the file? For a 500-pixel-wide image, how many cycles fit across the width?

  2. A designer asks for a pattern at 25 cpd in the same setup. Is that possible?

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 1, §1.16 (crossing the bridge from cpd to cycles/pixel) and §1.18 (the Nyquist-Shannon ceiling of 0.5 cycles/pixel).

  1. Pixels per degree is the bridge, and the “per degree” cancels:

\frac{8\ \text{cycles/degree}}{40\ \text{pixels/degree}} = 0.2\ \text{cycles/pixel}.

The period is the reciprocal, 1/0.2 = 5 pixels. To count cycles across the image, multiply by the width W = 500:

0.2 \times 500 = 100\ \text{cycles/image}.

  1. Divide again:

\frac{25}{40} = 0.625\ \text{cycles/pixel}.

That exceeds 0.5, which would need a period shorter than 2 pixels. A pixel grid cannot draw it, and sampling such a pattern would alias it into a coarser one. In this setup the finest pattern the file can show is 0.5 \times 40 = 20 cpd.

Answer: (a) a 5-pixel period, or 100 cycles across the image. (b) No; the limit is 20 cpd.

Problem 5: Why a hybrid image changes with distance

A hybrid image is built from the high-pass content of a photo of Einstein and the low-pass content of a photo of Marilyn Monroe, and printed. A viewer studies the print from 30 cm, then walks back to 6 m. Which face do they report at each distance? Explain using only cpd, the contrast sensitivity function, and the two filters.

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 1, §1.19, which builds on the CSF of §1.15.

The two filters put each face in its own band of the printed image. Einstein’s content is high-pass: edges and fine texture, packed into many cycles per unit length on the print. Marilyn’s content is low-pass: broad shading and overall shape, spread over few cycles per unit length.

The print never changes, but the number of cycles that fit into one degree of the viewer’s field of view does. Walking back shrinks every feature’s visual angle, so every cycles-per-degree value rises.

  • At 30 cm, the fine Einstein content sits at a moderate cpd, inside the visible range of the CSF, and it dominates perception. The Marilyn content is still present but is not what stands out.
  • At 6 m, the fine Einstein content is pushed past the ~60 cpd cutoff and becomes invisible. The Marilyn content stays at a low cpd, near the CSF’s visible band, so it is what remains.

Answer: Einstein up close, Marilyn from far away, because the CSF admits a different band at each distance.

Problem 6: Why a pinhole has a best size

A student builds a pinhole camera and finds that a 0.1 mm hole gives a dim, soft image, and a 5 mm hole also gives a soft image. Without any numbers, explain each result. Then predict, in words, whether the best hole diameter should grow or shrink if (a) the box is made longer, and (b) the light is redder.

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 1, §1.7 (why a hole forms an image) and §1.8 (the two blurs).

Two effects set the blur, and they pull in opposite directions as the hole shrinks.

  • Large hole: each scene point is smeared into a spot about as wide as the hole. This is geometric blur, so the 5 mm hole is soft because of geometry.
  • Small hole: the wave nature of light matters. Diffraction fans the light out, and the fan-out grows as the hole narrows. So the 0.1 mm hole is soft because of diffraction, and it is also dim because it admits little light.

For the predictions, recall that the diffraction spot is about \lambda f / d wide, while the geometric spot is about d.

    1. A longer box has a larger f. The fan-out has more distance to spread, so diffraction gets worse, and a somewhat larger hole is needed to keep it in check.
    1. Redder light has a larger \lambda. Diffraction again gets worse, so the best hole is again larger.

Both predictions agree with the formula d = 2\sqrt{f\lambda}, which grows with f and with \lambda.

Answer: the large hole is limited by geometric blur and the small one by diffraction. The best diameter grows for a longer box and for redder light.

Problem 7: Reading a filtering snippet

Here img is a 256×256 grayscale array.

import numpy as np

F = np.fft.fftshift(np.fft.fft2(img))
H, W = img.shape
yy, xx = np.meshgrid(np.arange(H) - H // 2, np.arange(W) - W // 2, indexing="ij")
mask = (xx**2 + yy**2) > 20**2
out = np.fft.ifft2(np.fft.ifftshift(F * mask)).real
  1. Is out a blurred version of img or an edges-only version? (b) What is the mask’s cutoff in cycles/pixel? (c) What would go wrong if np.fft.ifftshift were removed?
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 1, §1.17 (fft2, fftshift, and disc masks) and §1.16 (cycles/image to cycles/pixel).

After fftshift, the zero frequency is at the centre, and xx, yy measure how far each spectrum entry is from that centre. The condition > 20**2 is true outside a disc of radius 20 around the centre, so the mask keeps everything outside the disc and zeroes everything inside.

  1. Keeping high frequencies and dropping low ones is a high-pass filter. The output shows edges and fine texture only. It also removes the centre entry, the DC component, so the image’s average brightness becomes about 0. Some output values are negative, and the picture looks like a faint line drawing on a mid-grey field once displayed.

  2. The radius 20 is in units of array index, so it counts cycles across the whole width. Dividing by W converts to cycles/pixel:

\frac{20}{256} \approx 0.078\ \text{cycles/pixel}.

That is a period of about 13 pixels. Anything changing more slowly than that is removed.

  1. ifft2 expects the zero frequency in the array corner, not the centre. Without ifftshift, every entry of the masked spectrum is read as belonging to a different frequency than the one it was computed for, so the result is a scrambled image rather than the filtered picture. The .real at the end is there because the imaginary part of the result is numerical noise for a real-valued image.

Answer: (a) edges only (high-pass), (b) about 0.078 cycles/pixel, (c) the frequencies are put back in the wrong places and the output is garbled.

Problem 8: Numbers for a different pinhole box

A pinhole box is 0.10 m long (pinhole to screen) and is used with red light of wavelength 600 nm. Use the crude estimates geometric blur \approx d and diffraction blur \approx \lambda f / d, with constants ignored.

  1. For d = 0.2 mm and d = 0.8 mm, estimate each blur and say which dominates.

  2. What diameter does d = 2\sqrt{f\lambda} give? Does it fall between the two sizes in (a)?

  3. The box is lengthened to 0.40 m. What is the new optimal diameter?

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 1, §1.8. Convert everything to metres first: f = 0.10 m, \lambda = 600\times 10^{-9} m, so \lambda f = 6.0\times 10^{-8} m².

  1. For d = 0.2 mm = 2\times 10^{-4} m:

\text{geometric} \approx 0.2\ \text{mm}

\text{diffraction} \approx \frac{6.0\times 10^{-8}}{2\times 10^{-4}} = 3.0\times 10^{-4}\ \text{m} = 0.30\ \text{mm}

Diffraction is larger, so it dominates.

For d = 0.8 mm = 8\times 10^{-4} m:

\text{geometric} \approx 0.8\ \text{mm}

\text{diffraction} \approx \frac{6.0\times 10^{-8}}{8\times 10^{-4}} = 7.5\times 10^{-5}\ \text{m} \approx 0.075\ \text{mm}

Geometry dominates here.

  1. Applying the formula:

d = 2\sqrt{6.0\times 10^{-8}} \approx 2 \times 2.45\times 10^{-4}\ \text{m} \approx 0.49\ \text{mm}.

This lies between 0.2 mm (diffraction-limited) and 0.8 mm (geometry-limited), as it should. The point where the two crude estimates are exactly equal is \sqrt{f\lambda} \approx 0.245 mm, about half of 0.49 mm. As §1.8 explains, the constant depends on how each blur is measured, and the formula’s factor of 2 is Rayleigh’s choice.

  1. Only f changes, and it is four times larger. The diameter scales as \sqrt{f}, so it doubles:

d = 2\sqrt{(0.40)(600\times 10^{-9})} \approx 0.98\ \text{mm}.

Answer: (a) diffraction dominates at 0.2 mm and geometry at 0.8 mm; (b) about 0.49 mm; (c) about 0.98 mm.
