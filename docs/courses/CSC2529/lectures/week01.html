Week 1: The Human Visual System, Pinhole Cameras, and Hybrid Images

Week 1: The Human Visual System, Pinhole Cameras, and Hybrid Images

Overview. Week 1’s lecture is about the human visual system: the eye as an optical and sensing instrument, and the perceptual facts (visual acuity, contrast sensitivity, the Fourier view of images) that computational imaging designs around. Homework 1 asks us to build a physical pinhole camera and write code that merges two photographs into a hybrid image, whose apparent content changes with viewing distance. This chapter has three sections, labelled by source throughout. Section 1, Lecture Content, is the human visual system material as taught. Section 2, Homework Explanation, stays close to the homework brief, the problem-session slides, and the completed starter code, cross-referencing back to Section 1 wherever the lecture already supplies the needed concept. Section 3, Prerequisite Knowledge, covers the remaining gaps: material the homework needs but that neither the lecture nor the problem session teaches (chiefly the optics of the pinhole itself, and the sampling theorem the lecture explicitly defers to Week 5).

Section 1: Lecture Content

This section is the Week 1 lecture (D. Lindell, CSC2529), on the human visual system, together with the Oliva/Torralba/Schyns (2006) hybrid-images paper the lecture cites. It is course-taught material, not background the notes are supplying.

1.1 Why start a computational imaging course with the human eye?

Computational imaging designs a whole pipeline: optics, then sensing, then computation, with the goal of producing an image or a piece of information that a human or a downstream algorithm ultimately consumes. Before designing the optics, sensor, or algorithm, it helps to have a model of what the image is really for: human vision. The lecture frames the field with a simple triangle.

Definition. Computational imaging is optics, sensing, and computation, co-designed together, rather than a fixed lens and a passive sensor with image processing added afterward. A coded aperture (a specially shaped lens opening) paired with a matching deconvolution algorithm, designed as one system, is the lecture’s example.

The eye is also a physical instrument built from optics (cornea, lens) and sensing (retina), which makes it the fastest way to build intuition for both halves of that triangle at once.

1.2 Anatomy of the eye

Cross-section, front to back:

Structure Role
Cornea The clear, curved front surface. Provides most of the eye’s fixed focusing power; it is a strong, non-adjustable lens.
Aqueous humour Clear fluid between cornea and lens; maintains eye pressure and shape.
Iris / pupil The iris is the coloured muscle ring; the pupil is the hole at its centre. The iris widens or narrows the pupil to control how much light enters, the eye’s aperture.
Lens A flexible, adjustable lens behind the iris that fine-tunes focus by changing shape (see accommodation, §1.5).
Ciliary muscle / zonular fibers Muscle and fibers attached to the lens that squeeze or relax it to change its focal power.
Vitreous humour Clear gel filling the main eyeball cavity, behind the lens.
Retina The light-sensitive layer at the back of the eye; the eye’s sensor (§1.3).
Choroid A blood-vessel-rich layer behind the retina that supplies oxygen and nutrients and absorbs stray light, the same role the black interior paint plays in a pinhole camera (§2.2): stopping internal reflections from washing out contrast.
Sclera The white, tough outer shell of the eyeball; structural support, like a camera body.
Fovea A small pit in the retina, directly behind the pupil, densely packed with cone photoreceptors; where sharp, colour vision happens (§1.3).
Optic disc Where the optic nerve and retinal blood vessels exit the eye. It has no photoreceptors, which makes it the blind spot.
Optic nerve Carries the electrical signal from retina to brain.

An image sensor, the electronic chip in a digital camera that sits where photographic film used to go, converts incoming light into an electrical signal. The retina does the same job biologically.

Definition. Accommodation is the eye’s ability to change focus by physically changing the lens’s shape: the ciliary muscle contracts, the lens becomes rounder and more powerful, and near objects come into focus.

An emmetropic eye is a normal-sighted eye (§1.6, refractive errors).

1.3 The retina: rods and cones

The retina is a layered neural circuit, not a passive light-sensitive film. Light enters from the inner side of the retina first: it passes through ganglion cells and bipolar cells (plus horizontal and amacrine cells doing lateral processing) before reaching the photoreceptors, rods and cones, which sit at the very back, pointed away from the incoming light. The photoreceptor signal then travels forward again through the bipolar and ganglion cells, whose axons bundle into the optic nerve.

There are two types of photoreceptor:

Rods Cones
Count ~120 million ~6 million
Sensitivity Very high; used in low-light and night vision Lower; need brighter light
Colour Colourblind, one type only Three types (colour vision, §1.4)
Location Spread across the retina’s periphery Densely packed in the fovea (centre)
Acuity Low spatial resolution High spatial resolution

Because sharp, colourful vision exists only in the small central fovea, and we constantly move our eyes (“saccades”) to sample different parts of a scene with it, we do not perceive the world at uniform high resolution the way a camera sensor captures a frame. This non-uniform, foveated sampling recurs later in the course, in light-field and foveated displays and in compressive or adaptive sensing.

Note

A measured number. Roorda and Williams (1999, Nature) imaged the living human cone mosaic in the fovea and found individual cones spaced about 0.5 arcminutes of visual angle apart (an arcminute is 1/60 of a degree; see §1.8). This spacing is consistent with two other numbers later in this chapter: by the sampling logic in §3.3, a ~0.5 arcminute sampling pitch predicts a resolution limit around 1 arcminute, matching §1.8’s 20/20 figure, and a spatial-frequency cutoff around 60 cycles per degree, matching §1.12’s contrast-sensitivity cutoff.

1.4 Colour perception

Visible light is a narrow band, roughly 400-700 nanometers, of the full electromagnetic spectrum, between ultraviolet and infrared. Colour vision comes from three types of cones, each with a different, overlapping sensitivity curve over wavelength: S (short) cones peak near 440 nm (bluish), M (medium) cones peak near 545 nm (greenish), and L (long) cones peak near 565 nm (yellowish-red).

The M and L curves overlap heavily, which is why “green” and “red” cone signals are strongly correlated. Colour is represented as a three-number (tristimulus) space rather than by measuring wavelength directly because there are exactly three cone classes: the eye does not measure wavelength, it measures three overlapping weighted sums of the incoming spectrum.

Definition. Metamerism is the phenomenon where two physically different light spectra produce the same three cone responses and so look identical. It is the reason RGB displays and cameras work at all: they only need to match the eye’s three cone responses, not reproduce the true physical spectrum.

1.5 Eye versus camera

The lecture draws a direct structural analogy.

Eye Camera
Cornea and lens Lens
Iris and pupil Aperture
Retina Image sensor
Fovea (non-uniform density, denser at centre) Uniform pixel grid
Three cone types, irregularly interleaved Bayer colour filter array (regular RGGB mosaic)

A digital camera sensor is colourblind on its own: an individual light-sensing pixel measures brightness only, with no way to tell what wavelength it absorbed. To get colour, manufacturers place a Bayer colour filter array directly on the sensor: a physical grid of tiny red, green, and blue filters, one filter per pixel. Every pixel is single-colour, not multi-colour: a pixel under a green filter measures only the green component of the light hitting that spot, with no red or blue information at all, because the filter physically blocked those wavelengths before they arrived. What is multi-colour is the grid as a whole, arranged in a repeating 2\times 2 tile, one red, two green, one blue (“RGGB”, green doubled because human vision is most sensitive to green, per §1.4’s cone curves). RGGB describes the mosaic pattern of the pixel grid, not any property of a single pixel.

Because each pixel records only one channel, a full-colour photograph is a reconstruction: for each pixel, the two channels it did not measure are estimated from neighbouring pixels, which measured different colours thanks to the repeating tile. This estimation step is called demosaicking (Week 3); two-thirds of every pixel’s RGB value in a final photo is computed, not measured.

Two disanalogies are worth keeping in mind: the retina’s cone mosaic is irregular, not a neat repeating grid like a camera’s Bayer pattern, and sampling density on the retina is non-uniform (dense at the fovea, sparse at the periphery), unlike a camera sensor’s uniform sampling. Both differences matter again when the course covers demosaicking (Week 3) and non-uniform or adaptive sampling schemes.

1.6 Accommodation and refractive errors

Accommodation range, the span of distances the eye can bring into sharp focus, shrinks with age: at roughly 16 years old it spans about 8 cm to infinity, and by roughly 50 years old it typically narrows to about 50 cm to infinity, with the near range largely lost. This age-related loss is presbyopia, why people need reading glasses as they age: the lens stiffens and the ciliary muscle can no longer deform it enough for close focus.

Refractive errors are defined by where light rays converge relative to the retina.

Condition What happens Correction
Emmetropia Rays focus exactly on the retina None needed
Myopia (nearsightedness) Rays focus in front of the retina (eyeball too long, or cornea too curved) Concave (diverging) lens
Hyperopia (farsightedness) Rays focus behind the retina (eyeball too short) Convex (converging) lens
Astigmatism Cornea is irregularly curved, so rays never converge to a single point Cylindrical lens (corrects only the irregular axis)

This is a concrete instance of a pattern that recurs throughout computational imaging: optics can be broken in a specific, geometrically describable way, and correction is a matter of adding a compensating optical element or a compensating computation.

1.7 Field of view

The horizontal field of view across both eyes together spans roughly 190°; the binocular region where the two eyes overlap spans roughly 120°; the vertical field spans roughly 135°. One eye alone covers roughly 160° horizontally (about 100° temporal plus 60° nasal), so the 190° figure is the two-eye total, not a one-eye figure. This bears directly on display design (a wide-field-of-view VR headset is trying to fill as much of this natural visual field as possible) and on the pinhole camera in Homework 1: the field of view of a pinhole box is a geometric function of pinhole-to-screen distance and screen size, the same angular field-of-view idea, applied to a human-built “eye” (§2.2, §3.1).

1.8 Visual acuity

Definition. Visual angle is the angular size an object subtends at the eye. It depends on both the object’s physical size and its distance from the viewer, not on size alone, and is measured in degrees or in the smaller units of arcminutes (1° = 60 arcmin) and arcseconds (1 arcmin = 60 arcsec).

20/20 vision, the reference for “normal” acuity, corresponds to being able to resolve detail at about 1 arcminute of visual angle. The Snellen chart, the familiar eye-test letter chart, is built around this: each letter’s individual strokes are designed to subtend 1 arcminute at the standard test distance when a reader can just barely read that row, with the whole letter subtending 5 arcminutes.

Acuity is fundamentally a statement about the smallest angle the visual system can resolve, not a raw distance or pixel count. This angular framing is what lets us compare a phone screen 30 cm from the face to a movie screen 10 m away on equal footing (§1.9).

1.9 Retina displays: a worked example

This is the lecture’s worked example of turning the acuity concept (§1.8) into a concrete engineering number, using basic trigonometry: if the eye can just resolve a visual angle \alpha (about 1 arcminute, from §1.8), and a viewer sits at distance d from a screen, what is the physical size p of the smallest resolvable feature (pixel) on that screen?

p = 2 d \tan\!\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)

The resolvable feature subtends a small angle \alpha at the eye. Drawing the right triangle from the eye to the two edges of that feature at distance d, half the feature’s width is d\tan(\alpha/2), so the full width is 2d\tan(\alpha/2).

Note

Worked example (Steve Jobs’ “Retina Display” claim). For a tablet viewed at 12 inches, with \alpha = 1 arcminute, p = 2 \times 12" \times \tan(0.5\ \text{arcmin}) \approx 0.0035".

dpi is the same fact about pixel size, turned upside down. p is a length, how physically big one resolvable pixel is; dpi (“dots per inch”) is a density, how many of those pixel-widths fit into one inch. Since p is already “inches per pixel,” dpi is its reciprocal: \text{dpi} = 1\ \text{inch} / p. Plugging in, 1 / 0.0035 \approx 286 dpi.

(A pixel and a printing “dot” are treated as the same unit here. dpi is historically a printing term, since a printer physically deposits ink dots on paper; the more precise screen term is ppi, pixels per inch, but the lecture and this course treat the two as interchangeable, the same way Apple’s own marketing did.)

Apple’s marketing claim was 300 dpi, slightly above the computed 286 dpi threshold: the marketing number is a checkable physical claim, and it holds up with a small safety margin.

The same formula shows that how many pixels fall within the fovea is a moving target that depends entirely on viewing distance: a screen designed to be “retina” at 12 inches would look pixelated at 3 inches and be wastefully over-resolved at 3 meters. This exact idea, the same physical image carrying different perceived spatial-frequency content depending on distance, is the entire mechanism behind hybrid images (§1.13) and is exactly what Homework 1 Task 3 asks us to compute for a printed photo at two viewing distances (§2.4).

1.10 Dynamic range

Definition. Dynamic range is the ratio between the brightest and darkest signal a system can represent, usually expressed in f-stops (each stop is a factor of 2\times) or orders of magnitude (factors of 10\times).

The lecture gives three figures: the full human luminance vision range, across all adaptation states from starlight to sunlight, is about 14 orders of magnitude (10^{14}\times); the instantaneous human range, without the eye re-adapting (pupil dilation, photoreceptor bleaching), is about 5 orders of magnitude; and typical digital displays, at the time of the cited slide, managed only about 3 orders of magnitude. This large gap between what the eye can perceive and what a standard display can reproduce is the motivation for HDR (high dynamic range) imaging and displays (Week 4), such as “Sunnybrook”-style displays that add a secondary low-resolution backlight array behind an LCD panel to multiply the achievable contrast ratio.

1.11 Contrast

Before defining a contrast sensitivity function, we need a numeric definition of contrast itself, and there is more than one.

Definition. Weber contrast, for a single small feature on a large uniform background, is

C_{\text{Weber}} = \frac{I_{\text{feature}} - I_{\text{background}}}{I_{\text{background}}}.

Michelson contrast, for a periodic or repeating pattern (such as the sinusoidal gratings used in contrast-sensitivity experiments, §1.12) where no single “background” exists, is

C_{\text{Michelson}} = \frac{I_{\max} - I_{\min}}{I_{\max} + I_{\min}},

normalized by the sum of the pattern’s brightest and darkest parts. Equivalently, it is half that difference (the pattern’s amplitude) divided by the average of I_{\max} and I_{\min}.

Which formula applies depends on whether we are describing a single feature (Weber) or a repeating pattern (Michelson). Contrast-sensitivity experiments use grating stimuli, so Michelson contrast is the natural definition there.

1.12 The contrast sensitivity function

This is the most important technical concept of Week 1, and the direct theoretical basis for Homework 1 Task 3. The word “frequency” is overloaded in this course, so we pin it down before touching the function itself.

Three kinds of frequency. Light frequency (equivalently, wavelength or colour) is how fast the electromagnetic wave oscillates, measured in Hz or, more commonly, its wavelength in nm (§1.4’s ~400-700 nm visible range). Temporal frequency is how fast a signal changes over time, such as a flickering light or a video frame rate, measured in Hz (§1.17’s ~60 Hz). Spatial frequency is how fast brightness changes across space, as the eye or a sensor scans sideways across an image, measured in cycles per degree (cpd) or cycles per unit distance. From here through §1.13, “frequency” always means spatial frequency: it has nothing to do with colour or with time, and a “high-frequency” region of an image can be any colour and appear in a still photo with no time dimension at all.

Definition. Picture a grating: a test pattern of alternating light and dark stripes, like a barcode (the stimulus Campbell and Robson, 1968, used). One cycle is one full light-stripe-then-dark-stripe pair. Spatial frequency counts how many cycles are packed into some distance: fine, tightly packed stripes are high spatial frequency (the same idea as a sharp edge or fine detail in an ordinary photo), and wide, slowly varying stripes or a smooth gradient are low spatial frequency (a photo’s overall shape, silhouette, and coarse shading).

Why cycles per degree, not cycles per centimeter. A grating printed on paper has a fixed “cycles per centimeter,” a physical property of the ink that never changes regardless of who looks at it or from where. But the visual system experiences visual angle (§1.8), not physical centimeters directly, and visual angle shrinks as we back away and grows as we step closer (the same p = 2d\tan(\alpha/2) relationship from §1.9, used in the other direction: a fixed physical size subtends a smaller angle as distance grows). So vision science asks the perceptual version: how many light and dark cycles fit into one degree of the visual field, from wherever the viewer happens to be standing. That count is cycles per degree (cpd).

Note

Worked example. Suppose a grating’s cycles are 1 cm wide, and we stand 1 meter away. Stepping back to 2 meters, the same physical 1 cm cycle subtends roughly half its previous visual angle, so more whole cycles fit into any fixed one-degree slice of the field of view: cpd goes up. Stepping closer to 50 cm, the same cycle subtends roughly double its previous angle, so fewer cycles fit into one degree: cpd goes down. The physical ink on the page never changes; only how many of its cycles fit inside a one-degree window changes, and that depends entirely on viewing distance.

Image frequency versus spatial frequency. A third ruler sits upstream of both: image frequency is how fast brightness changes across the pixel grid of a digital image file, in cycles per pixel. It is a property of the array of pixel values alone, indifferent to print size, display, or viewing distance; two copies of the same image file, one on a phone and one on a cinema screen, have identical image frequency, because that number never leaves the file’s own pixel-index coordinate system. This is precisely the frequency axis produced by a digital image’s Fourier transform (§1.12.1). Converting image frequency to physical or perceptual spatial frequency is a two-step chain. First, image frequency converts to physical spatial frequency via dpi (§1.9): \text{physical spatial frequency (cycles/inch)} = \text{image frequency (cycles/pixel)} \times \text{dpi (pixels/inch)}, which depends only on the chosen print or display density, never on the viewer. Second, physical spatial frequency converts to cpd via viewing distance, the same visual-angle logic already applied to the 1 cm grating above; this step is the only one that depends on the viewer at all.

Note

Worked example. Suppose a digital image contains a fine repeating texture with a period of 6 pixels, one full light-dark cycle every 6 pixels, so its image frequency is 1/6 \approx 0.167 cycles/pixel, a fixed fact about the file. Printed at 300 dpi, the physical spatial frequency is 0.167 \times 300 = 50 cycles/inch (\approx 19.7 cycles/cm), fixed once printed. Viewed from 40 cm, applying the visual-angle machinery above gives roughly 13.7 cpd (past the 4-6 cpd peak, described below, but nowhere near the ~60 cpd cutoff). Changing viewing distance moves the cpd figure, but the image frequency and the physical spatial frequency never change.

Image frequency is the innermost, most fixed quantity: a property of the pixel array alone. Spatial frequency is the umbrella term for how fast brightness varies across space, splitting into a fixed-once-printed flavour (cycles per physical distance) and a viewer-dependent flavour (cpd, what the contrast sensitivity function is plotted against). A texture with fixed image frequency can sit anywhere on the sensitivity curve depending on print size and viewing distance, which is exactly the reasoning Homework 1 Task 3 asks us to work through (§2.4).

Definition. Campbell and Robson (1968) showed subjects sinusoidal gratings at different spatial frequencies and different contrasts (§1.11), and found the minimum contrast at which a subject could just barely detect the stripes. The reciprocal of that minimum detectable contrast is the subject’s contrast sensitivity at that spatial frequency; a low minimum-detectable-contrast means high sensitivity. The contrast sensitivity function (CSF) plots this sensitivity against spatial frequency in cpd.

The CSF curve is band-pass, not low-pass or flat: sensitivity is reduced at very low spatial frequencies (large, slowly varying patterns, genuinely hard to notice) and at very high spatial frequencies (very fine stripes), and peaks around 4-6 cpd, the density of light-dark cycles the eye is best at detecting. At the high-frequency end, sensitivity drops to zero around ~60 cpd, set by cone packing density: stripes finer than the photoreceptor mosaic can sample simply cannot be perceived, the same physical sampling-grid limit as the ~0.5 arcminute cone spacing in §1.3. Because of the conversion chain above, the same physical pattern’s position on this curve shifts as viewing distance changes, since its cpd value shifts with distance: moving closer lowers its cpd, potentially toward the 4-6 cpd peak (more visible); moving farther raises its cpd, potentially past ~60 cpd (less visible, eventually invisible).

Concretely: a photo with genuinely fine detail, hairline brushstrokes, has a fixed physical size, but not a fixed cpd. Viewed from far away, that small physical size subtends a tiny visual angle, so many strokes cram into one degree, pushing their cpd past the ~60 cpd cutoff and making them invisible. Viewed up close, the same strokes subtend a larger visual angle, so fewer fit into one degree, dropping their cpd into the visible, even peak-sensitivity, range. A single physical image can therefore contain content that is only visible up close (high spatial frequency) stacked with content that stays visible from far away (low spatial frequency), which is precisely how a hybrid image works (§1.13).

1.12.1 The Fourier transform: images as sums of waves

Everything above establishes that an image has spatial-frequency content, and how the eye perceives it. This subsection is about how a computer actually splits an image into its low-frequency and high-frequency pieces. It is deliberately a partial, homework-driven preview: the exact discrete Fourier transform formula, the sampling theorem, Nyquist rate and aliasing, and why the FFT algorithm is fast are left for Week 5 (“Sampling, Linear Systems, Deconvolution”); the point spread function and deconvolution, which reuse the convolution theorem introduced below, are left for Week 6. The “frequency” this subsection computes with is image frequency (cycles per pixel), not yet cpd; converting to cpd still needs the dpi and viewing-distance chain from §1.12.

Homework 1’s hybrid-images task is implemented by calling np.fft.fft2, np.fft.fftshift, np.fft.ifftshift, and np.fft.ifft2 (§2.4), so a mental model of what those functions do is worth building even at this partial level.

Most computer-vision code treats an image as an array of brightness values indexed by pixel location: what colour is at row y, column x. This is the spatial domain view. Computational imaging routinely switches to a different, equally complete representation of the same image: not “what colour is here,” but “how much of each possible wave pattern is present, across the whole image at once.” That is the frequency domain. The Fourier transform converts between the two, losing nothing; the inverse Fourier transform converts back.

The 1D idea. A musical chord sounds like one complex sound, but is really several pure tones (sine waves) sounding at once, each with its own frequency (pitch) and amplitude (loudness). More generally, essentially any signal, a sound wave over time, brightness along a single scanline of a photo, or any single value varying along one axis, can be written as a sum of sine waves of different frequencies, each with its own amplitude (how strong that wave is) and phase (how far it is shifted relative to a reference point). The Fourier transform takes a signal’s original description (a value at each moment or position) and returns the recipe of which frequencies are present, and how strong and shifted each one is.

Note

Worked example. Let s(t) = \sin(2\pi \cdot 3t) + 0.5\sin(2\pi \cdot 7t), a signal built from two pure tones: one oscillating 3 times per unit of t with amplitude 1, another oscillating 7 times per unit of t with amplitude 0.5. Its Fourier transform is zero almost everywhere except two sharp spikes, one at frequency 3 (amplitude 1) and one at frequency 7 (amplitude 0.5). A signal built from only a few frequencies produces a spectrum that is mostly zero with a few spikes.

From 1D to 2D. An image has two axes to vary along, horizontal and vertical, independently, so its “pure tone” building block cannot be an ordinary 1D sine wave; it must be a pattern that oscillates horizontally, vertically, or diagonally at once. That building block is a 2D sinusoidal grating, a repeating stripe pattern like the gratings in §1.12, now allowed to run in any direction:

I(x, y) = A \cos\!\big(2\pi(ux + vy) + \varphi\big)

where x, y are pixel coordinates, A is the grating’s amplitude, \varphi is its phase, u is the horizontal image frequency (cycles per pixel scanning left to right), and v is the vertical image frequency (cycles per pixel scanning up and down). If v = 0, u > 0, brightness only changes with x: vertical stripes. If u = 0, v > 0, brightness only changes with y: horizontal stripes. If both are positive, the stripes run diagonally, oriented perpendicular to the direction (u, v) points, with tightness set by the combined image frequency \sqrt{u^2 + v^2} cycles/pixel. Any real image can be treated as a sum of these 2D gratings, one for every possible (u, v) pair, each with its own amplitude and phase; the 2D Fourier transform reports, for every (u,v), how much of that tilted stripe pattern is present.

What fft2’s output represents. Calling np.fft.fft2 on an H \times W image returns another H \times W array, but comparing the two arrays entry by entry is meaningless, because they represent different things. In the input array, the entry at row y, column x is the brightness at pixel (x, y): index equals location. In the output array, the entry at row k_v, column k_u is a single complex number describing one particular 2D grating: its magnitude says how strong that grating is in the image, and its phase says how it is shifted (the same amplitude/phase pair from the 1D case, packaged as one complex number). Here k_u and k_v are plain array indices, not yet the u, v of the grating formula: k_u counts how many complete stripe-cycles that grating fits across the image’s entire width, cycles across the whole image, not cycles per pixel. Converting to image frequency in cycles per pixel divides by the dimension: u = k_u / W, v = k_v / H. Because W and H can differ, the same array position can give different cycles-per-pixel values for u and v.

Note

Worked example. A 256-pixel-wide image’s fft2 output has a nonzero entry at column index k_u = 8. That grating completes exactly 8 full stripe-cycles across the whole 256-pixel width, so its image frequency is u = 8/256 = 0.03125 cycles/pixel, one full cycle every 32 pixels.

The array of these magnitudes is the image’s spectrum. Low (u,v), near the origin, is slow brightness variation: coarse shapes and broad shading. High (u,v), far from the origin, is rapid variation: fine edges and texture. A colour image is really three stacked H \times W arrays, one per channel; fft2 only accepts a single 2D array, so each channel is transformed separately, giving three independent spectra with no interaction between channels (§2.4).

fftshift. Displaying a raw fft2 output’s magnitude as an image looks wrong at first: the brightest spot, the DC component (zero image frequency, u = v = 0, the image’s average brightness), sits in a corner rather than the centre. This is because the discrete Fourier transform’s indexing treats the far end of the array as a small negative frequency (the transform is periodic, so index N-1 is treated as frequency -1), so the second half of each axis holds negative frequencies, wrapped to the far end instead of sitting in front of frequency 0. Since rows index v and columns index u, the raw array’s four quadrants each hold one fixed combination of signs, with the DC corner in the top-left. np.fft.fftshift relocates these four blocks to meet at a shared centre, so frequency 0 lands in the middle and frequency magnitude increases outward in every direction, matching the natural picture of a spectrum and matching how the low-pass and high-pass masks below are naturally described (a disc around the centre). ifftshift undoes exactly this reordering and must run before ifft2, which expects the original DC-in-the-corner layout.

Filtering and the convolution theorem. Once a spectrum is fftshift-ed, building a filter is a masking operation: to keep only low image frequencies, zero out everything except a disc around the centre, a low-pass filter; to keep only high image frequencies, zero out that disc and keep everything outside it, a high-pass filter, the complement of the low-pass mask. Multiplying a spectrum by such a mask, then inverse-transforming back (ifftshift, then ifft2) produces a filtered image, and this is the exact same operation as blurring by averaging neighbouring pixels (low-pass) or extracting edges by subtracting a blur from the original (high-pass). The equivalence is the convolution theorem: multiplying two spectra in the frequency domain is mathematically identical to convolving (a generalized sliding weighted average) the two corresponding signals in the spatial domain. The frequency-domain route is usually easier to control precisely, for example choosing an exact cutoff frequency as a mask radius, and it is the route Homework 1’s own code uses. This same theorem underlies the point spread function (Week 5) and deconvolution (Week 6): a blur is convolution in the spatial domain and multiplication in the frequency domain, and undoing a blur means dividing out its frequency-domain multiplier.

1.13 Hybrid images

Definition. A hybrid image (Oliva, Torralba & Schyns, 2006, SIGGRAPH) merges two images so that, viewed up close, the high-spatial-frequency content of one image (fine detail, sharp edges) dominates perception, and viewed from far away, the low-spatial-frequency content of the other (coarse, blurry, overall shape) dominates.

Any photo can be thought of as coarse structure (overall shape and shading, low spatial frequency) with fine structure layered on top (edges, texture, high spatial frequency). A low-pass filter blurs an image, for instance by averaging each pixel with its neighbours; averaging smooths out anything that changes quickly from pixel to pixel while leaving slow, broad variation mostly intact, producing an out-of-focus-looking result. A high-pass filter does the opposite: subtracting a blurred version of the image from the original cancels whatever was slow and broad (present in both), leaving only the fast-changing edges and fine texture, a faint line drawing on a mid-grey field.

In code this is done in the frequency domain, using the machinery of §1.12.1: take Image A, compute its fftshift-ed spectrum, and apply a high-pass mask to keep only its high-(u,v) content, H_A(u,v); take Image B, compute its spectrum, and apply a low-pass mask to keep only its low-(u,v) content, L_B(u,v); add the two masked spectra together per colour channel,

F_{\text{hybrid}}(u, v) = H_A(u, v) + L_B(u, v);

then ifftshift and ifft2 back to the spatial domain. By the convolution theorem, this is mathematically equivalent to the blur-and-subtract description above; the frequency-domain route just gives precise control over the cutoff. The result is a single image containing both spatial-frequency bands at once, stacked together. Which band we consciously perceive depends on viewing distance, because the contrast sensitivity function (§1.12) determines which spatial-frequency band is currently in the visible range at that distance.

Concretely: Image A’s high-frequency content is made of small, tightly packed features. Viewed up close, those features subtend a large-enough visual angle that relatively few fit into one degree, so their cpd sits in a visible, even peak-sensitivity, range, and Image A is seen clearly. Viewed from far away, the same tiny features subtend a much smaller visual angle, so many more cram into one degree, pushing their cpd past the ~60 cpd cutoff, and they become invisible. Image B’s low-frequency content is made of large-scale features to begin with, and stays at a low, visible cpd across a wide range of realistic viewing distances, including far away, where Image A’s fine detail has already dropped out. So from a distance, Image B’s coarse structure is essentially all that remains visible. The same printed composite gives Image A up close and Image B from across the room, purely because the CSF admits a different spatial-frequency band at each distance, no trick beyond the ordinary physics of visual angle already covered above.

1.14 Depth perception

Human depth perception combines many independent cues, in two families.

Monocular cues (work with one eye): perspective (parallel lines converging toward a vanishing point), relative size (a familiar object appears smaller when farther away), absolute size (known real-world size of familiar objects), occlusion (an object blocking another is in front of it), accommodation (the eye’s focus state, §1.6, is itself a weak depth cue), retinal blur (out-of-focus objects are perceived as being at a different depth), motion parallax (nearer objects appear to move faster across the view than farther objects, as the viewer moves), texture gradients (texture appears finer and denser with distance), and shading (how light and shadow fall on a surface implies its 3D shape).

Binocular cues (require two eyes): vergence, the inward rotation angle of both eyes needed to fixate a near object (more rotation means a closer object), and binocular disparity, the difference between each eye’s retinal image of the same scene, which the brain decodes into depth, the basis of stereoscopic 3D.

Visual illusions (M.C. Escher drawings, or the Held et al. 2006 SIGGRAPH illusion demonstrations) show that these cues can be individually tricked or isolated: a picture that violates one cue (impossible occlusion, say) while satisfying others produces a perceptual paradox, evidence that these are genuinely separable computational cues rather than one monolithic “depth sense.”

1.15 Stereoscopic displays

Stereoscopic comes from Greek stereos (“firm, solid”) plus skopeo (“to look, to see”): literally, solid viewing, meaning viewing that conveys solid three-dimensional form. It achieves that using two eyes. Ordinary binocular vision already works this way: the two eyes sit a few centimeters apart, so each sees the same scene from a slightly different horizontal viewpoint, and the difference between those views (binocular disparity, §1.14) is what the brain decodes into depth. A stereoscopic device exploits this deliberately: instead of showing both eyes the same flat image, it feeds each eye a slightly offset image, mimicking binocular disparity so the brain fuses them into a perceived depth that is not really there on a flat screen or print.

1.16 Vergence-accommodation conflict

In the real world, vergence (§1.14, how much the eyes rotate inward to fixate an object) and accommodation (§1.6, how the lens focuses) both point to the same distance; they naturally match. On a stereo display, the two eyes are shown offset images that make them verge to a simulated depth (an object appears 2 m away, say), while accommodation must still focus at the actual physical screen distance (a headset lens at a fixed distance), usually different from the simulated depth.

Definition. The vergence-accommodation conflict (VAC) is this mismatch: verging to one distance while accommodating to another. It is the leading cause of visual discomfort, fatigue, eyestrain, and nausea in stereo 3D and VR displays.

This conflict is the direct engineering motivation for light field displays and future holographic displays, which can in principle present a physically correct focus depth per pixel instead of a fixed screen distance; this connects forward to the light-field imaging week (Week 9).

1.17 Lecture summary, reproduced

The lecture ends with a compact summary of every number introduced.

  • Visual acuity: 20/20 is about 1 arcminute (§1.8).
  • Field of view: ~190° horizontal across both eyes, ~120° binocular overlap, ~135° vertical, ~160° horizontal for one eye alone (§1.7).
  • Temporal resolution: ~60 Hz (varies with contrast and luminance), how fast a flickering or changing stimulus must be before it appears smooth to us; relevant later for flutter-shutter and coded-exposure photography (Week 4) and for displays generally.
  • Dynamic range: ~6.5 f-stops instantaneous, adapting up to ~46.5 total. The total figure is a unit conversion of §1.10’s ~14 orders of magnitude (1 order of magnitude is about 3.32 f-stops, so 14 \times 3.32 \approx 46.5). The instantaneous figure is a separately cited number, not a matching conversion of §1.10’s ~5 orders of magnitude (5 orders is about 16.6 f-stops): 6.5 f-stops is the eye’s truly momentary, no-adaptation contrast range, while ~5 orders of magnitude is a looser, more commonly quoted figure that already includes some fast local adaptation.
  • Colour: describable by the CIE xy chromaticity diagram; perceptual distances between colours are approximately linear (uniform) in CIE Lab space, a colour space designed so equal numeric distances correspond to roughly equal perceived colour differences.
  • Depth cues in 3D displays: vergence, focus (accommodation), their potential conflict, and resulting (dis)comfort (§1.14-§1.16).
  • Accommodation range: ~8 cm to infinity when young, degrading with age (§1.6).

2.4 Reading hw1_starter.py

The starter file computes three things in order, matching Task 3’s three sub-parts. We describe what each part computes and why; the file’s actual printed numbers are the assignment’s answers and are not reproduced here.

Part (a): physical print size and visual angle. The code starts from the image resolution (W \times W pixels), a print resolution in dots per inch (dpi), and two viewing distances (d, in meters). It converts dpi to a physical pixel size on the printout (inches to meters, divided by dpi), multiplies by W to get the printed image’s physical width, and then converts each viewing distance into a pixels-per-degree figure using exactly the visual-angle geometry §1.9 already derives for retina displays: \delta = 2D\tan(\theta/2) with \theta = 1° gives the physical extent of one degree of visual angle at distance D, and dividing by the physical pixel size converts that into pixels per degree.

Part (b): converting the CSF peak into an image frequency. The problem session states, as in §1.12, that the peak of the contrast sensitivity function is at 5 cpd. The code converts this angular frequency into an image frequency (cycles per pixel, then cycles across the whole image) using the pixels-per-degree figure from Part (a), following exactly the two-step conversion chain §1.12 already builds:

\text{cycles/pixel} = \frac{\text{cpd}}{\text{pixels/degree}}, \qquad \text{cycles/image} = \text{cycles/pixel} \times W.

Because the two viewing distances (near and far) give two different pixels-per-degree values, this produces two different peak-CSF image frequencies. The code averages them into a single “halfway” frequency, which Part (c) uses as a filter cutoff.

Part (c): building the filters and combining the images. The code builds a circular low-pass filter as a boolean mask over a 2D pixel grid (np.meshgrid), where every pixel within r0 of the grid centre is kept and everything outside is zeroed; the high-pass filter is 1 minus the low-pass mask, exactly the disc-shaped masking scheme §1.12.1 describes. For each colour channel separately, it takes the 2D FFT of both images (fft2), fftshifts each spectrum, multiplies the first image’s shifted spectrum by the low-pass mask and the second image’s shifted spectrum by the high-pass mask, adds the two masked spectra together, then ifftshifts and ifft2s back to a spatial-domain image, keeping only the real part (the imaginary part is numerical noise for a real-valued result) and clipping to a valid pixel range. This is exactly the hybrid-image procedure of §1.13, once per colour channel.

3.1 Pinhole image formation

How does a hole with no lens form a recognizable image at all?

Definition. A pinhole camera is an imaging device with no lens: a light-tight enclosure with a single small aperture on one side and a light-sensitive surface (film, sensor, or, in the homework’s case, a screen photographed by a separate camera) on the opposite side.

Every point on the scene being imaged radiates light in many directions at once. Without a lens, most of those directions of light land all over the screen, washing everything into a uniform grey. A pinhole works because the hole is small enough that, from any one scene point, only a narrow bundle of rays makes it through; the geometry of a narrow bundle through a fixed small hole is what keeps light from one scene point mostly landing in one place on the screen, rather than everywhere. Because rays travel in straight lines and the hole is a single point, a ray from the top of the scene that passes through the pinhole continues downward to the bottom of the screen, and a ray from the bottom of the scene continues upward: the image forms upside down, which is exactly what §2.2’s silo photo and diagram show.

The focal length here is not a lens property (a pinhole has no lens to focus); it is simply the distance f from the pinhole to the screen, and it sets the image’s scale. A scene object twice as tall as another, at the same distance from the camera, still projects twice as tall on the screen regardless of f; but for a fixed scene, a longer f magnifies the projected image (the same scene fills more of a screen placed farther from the pinhole) at the cost of a narrower field of view (a screen placed farther away captures a narrower cone of the surrounding scene, because the rays reaching its edges must come from a narrower angular slice of the scene to reach that far given the fixed pinhole).

3.2 Diffraction and the optimal-pinhole trade-off

If a smaller hole always gave a sharper image, the homework would simply ask for the smallest pinhole we can physically make. It does not, because two separate blurring effects pull in opposite directions as the hole shrinks.

The first is purely geometric: a pinhole of nonzero diameter d lets through not a single ray per scene point but a small cone of rays, so each scene point projects not to a point on the screen but to a small disk of diameter roughly proportional to d. A larger pinhole makes this disk larger, blurring the image; shrinking d shrinks the disk and sharpens the image. Purely on this geometric argument, an arbitrarily small pinhole gives an arbitrarily sharp image.

The second effect works the other way. Light is a wave, and when a wave passes through an aperture comparable in size to its wavelength, it spreads out on the far side rather than continuing in a straight line; this spreading is diffraction. As the pinhole shrinks toward the wavelength of visible light (roughly 400-700 nanometers, per §1.4), diffraction spreads each scene point’s ray bundle into a blur disk on the screen whose size grows as the aperture shrinks, the opposite dependence from the geometric blur.

The pinhole camera’s total blur is roughly the geometric blur plus the diffraction blur. Geometric blur grows with d; diffraction blur shrinks with d. Somewhere in between is a diameter that minimizes the sum, and solving for that minimum (treating diffraction blur as proportional to \lambda f / d and geometric blur as proportional to d, then setting the derivative of their sum with respect to d to zero) gives d \propto \sqrt{f\lambda}. The leading constant depends on exactly how the two blurs are measured, so this argument fixes the shape of the formula but not its constant: Petzval’s 1857 version of it gives d = \sqrt{2f\lambda} \approx 1.41\sqrt{f\lambda}, while the constant of 2 in the formula below is Lord Rayleigh’s (1891), reached from wave-optics assumptions:

d = 2\sqrt{f\lambda}

The formula grows with the focal length f (a longer box needs a larger pinhole to keep diffraction blur in check) and with the wavelength \lambda (redder light diffracts more than bluer light, so it wants a slightly larger hole too).

Note

Worked example. For illustration only, take a box with f = 0.20 m and green light at \lambda = 550 nm = 550 \times 10^{-9} m (not any particular student’s measured box):

d = 2\sqrt{(0.20)(550 \times 10^{-9})} = 2\sqrt{1.1 \times 10^{-7}} \approx 2 \times 3.3 \times 10^{-4} \approx 6.6 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{m} \approx 0.66\ \text{mm}.

3.3 The Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem

§1.12.1 notes, in the lecturer’s own words, that the sampling theorem and Nyquist rate are left for Week 5. Homework 1’s code implicitly relies on the underlying idea (a digital image’s spatial-frequency content is bounded), so a short preview is worth having now rather than treating the relevant numbers as unexplained.

Definition. The Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem says that a signal sampled at a fixed rate can only unambiguously represent frequencies up to half that sampling rate. A higher-frequency component, once sampled, becomes indistinguishable from (is confused with, or aliased to) a lower-frequency one.

A digital image samples brightness once per pixel, a sampling rate of exactly 1 sample per pixel. By the theorem, the highest spatial frequency the image can represent without ambiguity is half that rate: 0.5 cycles per pixel, the image’s Nyquist frequency. A pattern that oscillates faster than this (alternating black and white pixels every half-pixel, say, which is not representable on a discrete grid) cannot be drawn on the pixel grid at all; sampling such a pattern produces aliasing, where the too-fine pattern is misrepresented as a coarser, wrong one. This is why any filter cutoff chosen in image-frequency units, such as §2.4’s “halfway frequency,” must land below 0.5 cycles per pixel to be a meaningful choice on the pixel grid, and why the fft2 frequency axis (§1.12.1) naturally spans from 0 up to this Nyquist limit and back.

Note

Key takeaways from Week 1:

  • The eye is an optical and sensing instrument (cornea and lens for optics, retina for sensing), and computational imaging is designed with its properties, acuity, dynamic range, contrast sensitivity, in mind.
  • Visual angle, not physical size, is what the eye actually perceives, and dpi, cycles per degree, and pixels-per-degree are all built from the same p = 2d\tan(\alpha/2) geometry.
  • The contrast sensitivity function is band-pass, peaking around 4-6 cpd and cutting off around 60 cpd, and because cpd depends on viewing distance, the same physical pattern can sit anywhere on this curve depending on how far away we stand.
  • Any image decomposes into spatial-frequency components via the 2D Fourier Transform; keeping only low or only high frequencies (a disc-shaped mask after fftshift) produces a blurred or an edge-only version of the same image, reversibly.
  • Hybrid images exploit the CSF by adding a low-pass-filtered image to a high-pass-filtered image, so perceived content changes with viewing distance.
  • Pinhole cameras form images with no lens, at the cost of a trade-off between geometric blur (large pinhole) and diffraction blur (very small pinhole), balanced by d = 2\sqrt{f\lambda}, a topic outside this week’s lecture.
