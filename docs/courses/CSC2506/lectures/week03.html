Week 3: Markov Random Fields and Exact Inference

Week 3: Markov Random Fields and Exact Inference

Overview. Week 2 built directed graphical models: pick an ordering of the variables, draw an arrow from each parent to each child, and read conditional independencies off the arrows. That works well when there is a natural causal or generative ordering, but many domains (a social network, a protein interaction network, the pixels of an image) have no natural direction to assign to an edge. This lecture introduces Markov random fields (MRFs), undirected graphical models where an edge means “these two variables interact” rather than “one causes the other.” We show what MRFs can express that DAGs cannot, and vice versa, then specialize to two concrete families: the Ising model (for discrete/binary variables) and Gaussian graphical models (for continuous ones), the latter directly useful for this course’s first assignment. The second half turns to exact inference: given a graphical model, how do we actually compute a marginal or conditional probability without enumerating every possible assignment? Variable elimination answers this for both DAGs and MRFs at once, and we finish with a worked example and a modern application, the restricted Boltzmann machine.

From DAGs to undirected graphs

Week 2 represented a joint distribution with a DAG, where every edge has a direction. Some problems have no natural direction: neighbouring pixels in an image, for example, influence each other symmetrically. To see what directed edges cost us in such a problem, we look at how much of the graph a single variable depends on, which is measured by its Markov blanket.

Recall the Markov blanket of a variable X_i: the smallest set of other variables that makes X_i conditionally independent of everything else. In a DAG, the Markov blanket of X_i is its parents, its children, and its children’s other parents (the co-parents), because of the explaining-away effect from Week 2 (Conditional independence: three canonical structures in Week 2): observing a child can create dependence between that child’s parents.

This has an awkward consequence. Take a grid of variables X_1,\dots,X_{20} arranged in rows, where intuitively each variable should depend only on its four grid neighbors (up, down, left, right). If we try to encode this as a DAG by choosing some ordering of the variables (say, left to right, top to bottom) and drawing arrows accordingly, the Markov blanket of an interior node like X_8 can end up including nodes that have nothing to do with X_8’s grid neighbors. Figure 1 shows the lecture’s version: every horizontal edge points right and every vertical edge points down.

Reading X_8’s Markov blanket off Figure 1 with the parents, children, co-parents rule:

  • Parents: X_3 (above) and X_7 (left).
  • Children: X_9 (right) and X_{13} (below).
  • Co-parents: the other parents of those children. X_9 has parents X_4 and X_8, so X_4 is a co-parent. X_{13} has parents X_8 and X_{12}, so X_{12} is a co-parent.

The first two groups are exactly X_8’s four grid neighbors, as we would expect. X_4 and X_{12} are the surprise: they sit diagonally from X_8 and share no edge with it. They are in the blanket because of the co-parent rule, which comes from the collider case of Week 2’s d-separation rule (The Bayes Ball algorithm in Week 2). The path X_8\to X_9\leftarrow X_4 meets head-to-head at X_9, so X_9 is a collider on it. A Markov blanket must contain X_8’s children, so X_9 is conditioned on. A collider blocks a path only when neither it nor any of its descendants is observed, so an observed collider leaves the path active. This is explaining away: given X_9, learning X_4 changes what we believe about X_8, its other cause. The only way to cut that path is to condition on X_4 too. The same argument through X_{13} puts X_{12} in the blanket.

The asymmetry gives the orientation away. The other two diagonal cells, X_2 and X_{14}, are not in the blanket: X_2’s children are X_3 and X_7, which are parents of X_8 rather than children, and X_{14} is a child of X_9 and X_{13}, not a parent of either. Which diagonals join the blanket depends only on which way the arrows were drawn. The DAG is not wrong: it does encode a valid factorization. But its dependency structure does not match the “each cell depends on its neighbors” structure we actually wanted to express, and no choice of edge directions fixes this in general.

Figure 1. The lecture’s grid as a DAG, with every edge pointing right or down. X_8’s Markov blanket is its parents X_3, X_7 (blue), its children X_9, X_{13} (green), and its co-parents X_4, X_{12} (orange). The highlighted edges X_4\to X_9 and X_{12}\to X_{13} are what make X_4 and X_{12} co-parents, even though neither is a grid neighbor of X_8.

Markov random fields, cliques, and the factorization theorem

The grid shows the problem with arrows: they force us to choose directions the problem does not have, and that choice drags non-neighbours like X_4 and X_{12} into X_8’s blanket through colliders. The fix is to drop the arrows. In an undirected graph an edge only says “these two variables interact”, there are no colliders, and (as The Hammersley-Clifford theorem will show) a node’s Markov blanket is just its neighbours. An undirected graph then needs its own rule for which distributions it represents, which is what this section sets up.

Definition. An undirected graphical model, or Markov random field (MRF), represents a set of random variables as nodes of an undirected graph G, where an edge between two nodes means those variables interact directly, rather than one causing the other.

To write down what distributions an MRF represents, we need the notion of a clique.

Definition. A clique is a subset of nodes in which every pair is connected by an edge. A maximal clique is a clique that cannot be extended by adding one more adjacent node without breaking the all-pairs-connected property.

Definition. Let \boldsymbol{x} = (x_1,\dots,x_m) be all the variables in graph G, and let \mathcal{C} be the set of all maximal cliques of G. A distribution p factorizes with respect to G if p(\boldsymbol{x}) \propto \prod_{C \in \mathcal{C}} \psi_C(x_C) for some nonnegative potential functions \psi_C, where x_C = (x_i)_{i \in C}. The MRF on G represents exactly the distributions that factorize with respect to G.

TipQuestion: What does the C in psi_C mean, and are we free to define these functions however we like?

What C stands for. C is a clique: a set of node indices, such as C=\{1,2,3\}. The product \prod_{C\in\mathcal{C}} runs over every maximal clique of the graph, and x_C is the list of values of just the variables in that clique. So \psi_C(x_C) is “the potential attached to clique C, evaluated at that clique’s variables.” In practice we write the clique’s members as the subscript: for C=\{1,2,3\}, \psi_C(x_C) is written \psi_{1,2,3}(x_1,x_2,x_3). The letter \psi (psi) has no meaning of its own; it is the usual name for a potential.

What we may choose. Almost anything, within three rules:

  • Nonnegative. \psi_C(x_C)\ge 0 for every setting. Hammersley-Clifford (The Hammersley-Clifford theorem) needs the resulting p to be strictly positive, which is why potentials are often written as \psi_C = e^{(\text{something})}, which can never be zero.
  • Only the clique’s own variables. \psi_{1,2,3} may look at x_1,x_2,x_3 and nothing else. This rule is the entire content of the graph: it is what stops a factor from linking two variables that share no edge.
  • No normalization. A potential need not sum to 1 and is not a probability. Multiplying one potential by a constant changes nothing once we divide by Z.

Within those rules, choosing the potentials is the modelling step. They are usually given a parametric form and learned from data, such as the Ising model’s \psi_{st}=e^{J_{st}x_sx_t} (The Ising model) or the log-linear form of MRFs as exponential families.

Potentials on smaller cliques. We may also attach potentials to single nodes or to edges inside a larger clique, as the Ising model does with its per-node field. This does not enlarge the family of distributions: any such factor can be multiplied into the potential of a maximal clique that contains it, which recovers the one-potential-per-maximal-clique form above.

TipQuestion: What does it mean for a distribution to factorize with respect to a graph, concretely?

What “factorizes” means. A function of many variables factorizes if it can be written as a product of smaller functions, each of which looks at only some of the variables. Week 2 already did this for DAGs: p(x_1,\dots,x_N)=\prod_ip(x_i\mid\text{parents}(x_i)), where each factor involves one node and its parents (Directed graphical models in Week 2). Here the factors are potentials instead of conditional probabilities, and the graph decides which groups of variables are allowed to share a factor: one factor per maximal clique. “With respect to G” means exactly that restriction. Any distribution can be written as a single factor over all its variables, but that only factorizes with respect to the complete graph.

A concrete example. Take the chain X_1-X_2-X_3 with each X_i\in\{0,1\}. Its maximal cliques are the edges \{1,2\} and \{2,3\}, so a distribution factorizes with respect to this graph if p(x_1,x_2,x_3)=\frac1Z\,\psi_{1,2}(x_1,x_2)\,\psi_{2,3}(x_2,x_3). Choose both potentials to reward agreement, as in the Ising model of The Ising model: \psi(a,b)=2 if a=b and 1 if a\neq b. Multiplying the two potentials for each of the 8 settings gives the unnormalized table:

(x_1,x_2,x_3) 000 001 010 011 100 101 110 111
\psi_{1,2}\,\psi_{2,3} 4 2 1 2 2 1 2 4

The entries sum to Z=18, so for example p(0,0,0)=4/18=2/9 and p(0,1,0)=1/18. Eight probabilities were specified by two 2\times2 tables: that saving is the point of factorizing.

What the factorization buys. Because no factor contains both x_1 and x_3, they can only influence each other through x_2. Check it with the definition of conditional probability (Bayes’ rule), dividing the joint by the marginal of what we condition on: p(x_3\mid x_1,x_2)=\frac{p(x_1,x_2,x_3)}{p(x_1,x_2)}=\frac{p(x_1,x_2,x_3)}{\sum_{x_3'}p(x_1,x_2,x_3')}. The \frac1Z in every joint probability cancels between numerator and denominator, so the table entries can be used directly. With x_2=0 and x_1=0: p(x_3=0\mid x_1=0,x_2=0)=\frac{p(0,0,0)}{p(0,0,0)+p(0,0,1)}=\frac{4/18}{4/18+2/18}=\frac{4}{4+2}=\frac23. With x_2=0 and x_1=1: p(x_3=0\mid x_1=1,x_2=0)=\frac{p(1,0,0)}{p(1,0,0)+p(1,0,1)}=\frac{2/18}{2/18+1/18}=\frac{2}{2+1}=\frac23. Changing x_1 does not change the answer once x_2 is known, so X_1\perp X_3\mid X_2. This is the independence that The Hammersley-Clifford theorem reads directly off the graph.

A distribution that does not factorize. Let X_2 be a fair coin, independent of everything, and let X_1=X_3 be a second fair coin, so p(x_1,x_2,x_3)=\frac14 when x_1=x_3 and 0 otherwise. Given x_2, knowing x_1 still tells us x_3 exactly, so X_1\not\perp X_3\mid X_2. Every distribution of the form \frac1Z\psi_{1,2}\psi_{2,3} has X_1\perp X_3\mid X_2 (the general proof is the chain argument in The Hammersley-Clifford theorem; the check above is one instance), so no choice of potentials can produce this one. It factorizes only with respect to a graph with an edge between X_1 and X_3.

Unlike a DAG’s conditional probability tables, a potential \psi_C need not sum or integrate to 1, and need not have any probabilistic interpretation on its own; it is simply a nonnegative score attached to each joint setting of the clique’s variables. The product of all the potentials is only proportional to p(\boldsymbol{x}), so an explicit normalizing constant Z = \sum_{\boldsymbol{x}} \prod_{C} \psi_C(x_C) (the partition function) is needed to turn it into an actual probability. This factored structure is what makes sums and integrals over p(\boldsymbol{x}) tractable: instead of manipulating one giant table over all variables jointly, we manipulate several small tables, one per clique, exactly the same motivation that drove the chain-rule factorization of Week 2 (Why factor the joint in Week 2).

As a small concrete instance, a 4-cycle of binary variables x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4 (edges 1\text{-}2, 2\text{-}3, 3\text{-}4, 4\text{-}1) has four maximal cliques, one per edge, so

p(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4) = \frac{1}{Z}\,\psi_{1,2}(x_1,x_2)\,\psi_{2,3}(x_2,x_3)\,\psi_{3,4}(x_3,x_4)\,\psi_{1,4}(x_1,x_4).

Figure 2. A 4-cycle: each edge is its own maximal clique, since no three nodes here are all pairwise connected. Each \psi can be stored as a small table (for binary variables, 4 numbers per edge); these numbers are potentials, not probabilities, since the normalizer Z has not been applied.

The Hammersley-Clifford theorem

We now have two ways to read an undirected graph. Markov random fields, cliques, and the factorization theorem reads it as a factorization: a product of clique potentials. The motivation in From DAGs to undirected graphs reads it as a set of independence statements: a variable should depend only on its neighbours. Nothing so far says these two readings describe the same distributions. The Hammersley-Clifford theorem says they do, for distributions that are strictly positive.

Hammersley-Clifford theorem. If p(\boldsymbol{x}) > 0 for all \boldsymbol{x}, the following are equivalent:

  • p factorizes with respect to G, i.e. p(\boldsymbol{x}) \propto \prod_{C \in \mathcal{C}} \psi_C(x_C) for nonnegative potentials \psi_C.
  • Global Markov property: X_A \perp X_B \mid X_S whenever S separates A from B in G (every path from A to B passes through S).

Two special cases of the global Markov property are worth naming directly, since they recur constantly: if i and j are not joined by an edge, then X_i \perp X_j \mid X_{\text{rest}} (conditioning on every other variable separates any two non-adjacent nodes); and the Markov blanket of X_i in an MRF is simply its graph neighbors, no co-parent correction needed, in contrast to the DAG case of From DAGs to undirected graphs.

Why the two directions match: a chain. The core mechanism is easiest to see on a three-node chain X-Y-Z, whose only maximal cliques are the edges \{X,Y\} and \{Y,Z\}, so the class of distributions that factorizes with respect to this graph is exactly those of the form f(x,y,z) = \alpha(x,y)\,\beta(y,z) for positive functions \alpha,\beta. The claim is that this is equivalent to X \perp Z \mid Y.

Independent \Rightarrow factorized. Using the product rule, f(x,y,z) = f(y,z)\,f(x\mid y,z), and separately f(x,y,z) = f(x\mid y)\,f(y,z) whenever X\perp Z\mid Y already holds, so a factorization of the stated form is available with \alpha(x,y) = f(x\mid y) and \beta(y,z) = f(y,z). This direction just restates the definition of conditional independence as a factorization.

Factorized \Rightarrow independent, without assuming it first. Start only from f(x,y,z)=\alpha(x,y)\beta(y,z) and derive the independence. Summing out x, f(y,z) = \Big(\sum_x \alpha(x,y)\Big)\beta(y,z). Dividing the joint by this marginal gives the conditional: f(x\mid y,z) = \frac{\alpha(x,y)\,\beta(y,z)}{\left(\sum_x \alpha(x,y)\right)\beta(y,z)} = \frac{\alpha(x,y)}{\sum_x \alpha(x,y)}. The right-hand side has no z in it at all, so f(x\mid y,z) does not depend on z, which is exactly X\perp Z\mid Y. The mechanism is the same cancellation as Week 2’s causal-chain check (Conditional independence: three canonical structures in Week 2): a shared factor in numerator and denominator drops out, leaving no trace of the conditioned-out variable.

Worked example: cliques, factorization, and independence

With both readings in hand, we apply them to one graph: find its maximal cliques, write the factorization they give, then read independencies off the graph by separation, which Hammersley-Clifford guarantees will agree with the factorization.

Figure 3. A seven-node MRF with edges 1\text{-}2, 1\text{-}3, 2\text{-}3, 2\text{-}4, 2\text{-}5, 3\text{-}5, 3\text{-}6, 4\text{-}5, 4\text{-}6, 4\text{-}7, 5\text{-}6, 5\text{-}7, 6\text{-}7.

How many maximal cliques are there? Checking every pairwise connection within each candidate set gives five maximal cliques: \{1,2,3\}, \{2,3,5\}, \{2,4,5\}, \{3,5,6\}, and \{4,5,6,7\}. Node 5 belongs to four of the five, since it connects to nearly every other node; that is exactly why it appears in so many of the clique’s variable lists below.

What is the factorization? p(\boldsymbol{x}) \propto \psi_{1,2,3}(x_1,x_2,x_3)\,\psi_{2,3,5}(x_2,x_3,x_5)\,\psi_{2,4,5}(x_2,x_4,x_5) {}\times \psi_{3,5,6}(x_3,x_5,x_6)\,\psi_{4,5,6,7}(x_4,x_5,x_6,x_7).

What independencies does the graph imply? Reading the global Markov property off the picture: removing \{3,4,5\} disconnects \{1,2\} from \{6,7\} entirely (every path from 1 or 2 to 6 or 7 passes through 3, 4, or 5), so (X_1,X_2) \perp (X_6,X_7) \mid (X_3,X_4,X_5). Similarly, every path from 1 to 5 passes through 2 or 3, so X_1 \perp X_5 \mid (X_2,X_3).

Note

Practice question. In Figure 3, is X_1 \perp X_4 \mid X_2? Check by finding every path from node 1 to node 4 and seeing whether \{2\} blocks all of them.

Solution. Node 1’s only edges go to nodes 2 and 3, so every path out of node 1 starts by visiting 2 or 3. A path starting 1\to 2 \to 4 is blocked once we condition on \{2\}, since 2 is on that path and is now fixed. But a path starting 1\to 3 \to 5 \to 4 never touches node 2 at all, so conditioning on \{2\} does nothing to it: it stays open. Since at least one path from 1 to 4 survives, \{2\} does not separate them, so X_1 \not\perp X_4 \mid X_2. Separating 1 from 4 would require conditioning on a set that intersects every route out of node 1’s second neighbor, node 3, as well, for example \{2,3\} or \{2,5,6\}.

What MRFs can express that DAGs cannot, and vice versa

The grid made MRFs look like a strict improvement on DAGs. They are not: the two representations are not nested, and each can express independence patterns the other cannot.

An MRF with no equivalent DAG. Take the 4-cycle A-D-C-B-A shown below (edges A\text{-}D, A\text{-}B, D\text{-}C, B\text{-}C, and no diagonal). Its two non-adjacent pairs give two independence statements simultaneously: A \perp C \mid \{D,B\} and B \perp D \mid \{A,C\}. Any attempt to encode this as a DAG by orienting the four edges necessarily creates a v-structure (a collider) at one of the four nodes, and by the explaining-away logic of Week 2, that collider blocks one of the two independencies while leaving the other intact, or introduces an independence (such as making B and D marginally independent) that was never true in the undirected model. No orientation of the diamond’s edges reproduces both independencies at once.

Figure 4. A diamond MRF. Both A\perp C\mid\{D,B\} and B\perp D\mid\{A,C\} hold simultaneously; no directed version of this graph reproduces both.

A DAG with no equivalent MRF. Conversely, take the explaining-away structure from Week 2, X \to Z \leftarrow Y. There, X and Y are marginally independent (X\perp Y) but become dependent once Z is observed (X \not\perp Y \mid Z). An undirected model cannot express this pattern: the global Markov property says that if X \perp Y holds unconditionally, no set of nodes we condition on can ever create a dependency between them, since observing more variables in an MRF can only add to the separating set, never remove an edge. Collider-created dependence is a genuinely directed phenomenon.

Figure 5. X\perp Y marginally, but X \not\perp Y \mid Z: no undirected graph reproduces both facts at once.

MRFs as exponential families

So far a potential has been any nonnegative table, and the graph has told us only which variables share a potential. To learn potentials from data we need a parameterized family. Week 1 already gave us one with a complete MLE theory, the exponential family, and this section shows that MRFs fit into it.

Note

Homework-relevant. This section’s log-linear form and sufficient statistics are the same exponential-family machinery from Week 1 (Maximum likelihood estimation in Week 1), applied to potentials instead of a single density. If a current or upcoming assignment asks you to write an MRF or a Gaussian graphical model in exponential-family form, this derivation pattern is the one to reuse.

Consider the parametric family p(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\theta) = \frac{1}{Z(\theta)}\prod_{C\in\mathcal{C}} \psi_C(x_C\mid\theta_C), \qquad \theta = (\theta_C)_{C\in\mathcal{C}}. Taking the log of the product turns it into a sum, exactly as in Week 1’s move from likelihood to log-likelihood: p(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\theta) = \exp\Big\{\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}} \log\psi_C(x_C\mid\theta_C) - \underbrace{\log Z(\theta)}_{=A(\theta)}\Big\}. If each potential has a log-linear form, \log\psi_C(x_C\mid\theta_C) = \theta_C^\top \phi_C(x_C), this becomes exactly Week 1’s exponential family, with natural parameter \theta, sufficient statistic \phi = (\phi_C)_{C\in\mathcal{C}}, and log-partition function A(\theta) = \log Z(\theta): p(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\theta) = \exp\Big\{\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}} \theta_C^\top \phi_C(x_C) - A(\theta)\Big\}.

TipQuestion: Where does the log-linear form come from, and why do we want an exponential family at all?

The core idea. We choose the potentials so that the log of each one is a weighted sum of features of its clique’s variables: \log\psi_C(x_C\mid\theta_C) = \theta_C^\top\phi_C(x_C) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \psi_C(x_C\mid\theta_C) = \exp\{\theta_C^\top\phi_C(x_C)\}. So \psi_C is not \theta times a function of x. Its log is, and \psi_C is the exponential of that (which also keeps it positive). This is a modelling choice, not something derived from the MRF definition. We make it because it is exactly what Week 1’s template needs.

What each object is. For a clique C with |C| variables and d_C chosen features:

Object Input Output
x_C (none) the values of the clique’s \lvert C\rvert variables, e.g. (x_1,x_2)\in\{0,1\}^2
\phi_C (feature map) x_C a vector in \mathbb{R}^{d_C}
\theta_C (weights) (none) a vector in \mathbb{R}^{d_C}, one weight per feature
\psi_C (potential) x_C a positive number
\log\psi_C x_C a real number

Why the transpose. Both \theta_C and \phi_C(x_C) are column vectors of length d_C. \log\psi_C must be a single number, and \theta_C^\top\phi_C(x_C), a (1\times d_C) row times a (d_C\times 1) column, is the dot product \sum_{k=1}^{d_C}\theta_{C,k}\,\phi_{C,k}(x_C): each feature times its own weight, summed. Without the transpose, \theta_C\phi_C would be a column times a column, which is undefined.

Why this makes an exponential family. Run Week 1’s recipe (Exponential families):

  • \log Z(\theta) sums over all \boldsymbol x, so it depends only on \theta. It plays the role of A(\theta).
  • Each \log\psi_C mixes data and parameters, so it must factor as (parameters)^\top(function of data). The log-linear form is precisely that requirement, clique by clique. A potential like \psi_C = 1+\theta_C x_C fails, since \log(1+\theta_C x_C) does not split.
  • A sum of dot products is one dot product of stacked vectors. With \theta\in\mathbb{R}^d and \phi(\boldsymbol x)\in\mathbb{R}^d the concatenations over cliques, d=\sum_C d_C: \sum_C\theta_C^\top\phi_C(x_C) = \theta^\top\phi(\boldsymbol x).

This gives natural parameter \theta, sufficient statistic \phi, h=1, and A(\theta)=\log Z(\theta), a map from \mathbb{R}^d to \mathbb{R}.

Examples. The Ising model (The Ising model) has d_{st}=1: \phi_{st}=x_sx_t and \theta_{st}=J_{st}. For a discrete table, indicator features always work. A binary edge with invented values \psi(0,0)=2, \psi(0,1)=1, \psi(1,0)=1, \psi(1,1)=4 has d_C=4, \theta_C=(\log 2,0,0,\log 4), and \phi_C(1,1)=(0,0,0,1), so \theta_C^\top\phi_C(1,1)=\log 4.

Why we want this. From Week 1:

  • the data enter only through \sum_n\phi(\boldsymbol x^{(n)}) (clique counts, for indicator features);
  • \nabla A(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_\theta[\phi(\boldsymbol X)] (Mean of sufficient statistics), so the MLE matches the model’s clique marginals to the data’s clique frequencies;
  • the second derivative of A is a covariance, so A is convex and the log-likelihood is concave, with no spurious local maxima.

The catch is that Z and its gradient sum over every joint configuration, which is why inference keeps returning in later weeks.

When does the log-linear form hold? For finite discrete x_C, \log\psi_C takes only finitely many values, one per setting of x_C. Let \theta_C list all those values, and let \phi_C(x_C) be the indicator vector with a 1 in the entry matching x_C’s current setting and 0 elsewhere: then \theta_C^\top \phi_C(x_C) just selects out the one matching entry, which is \log\psi_C(x_C\mid\theta_C) by construction. As in Week 1’s exponential-family facts, the log-partition function’s gradient recovers an expectation: \partial \log Z(\theta) / \partial \theta_C = \mathbb{E}[\phi_C(X_C)].

Worked example: minimal vs. non-minimal. Take the chain X_1 - X_2 - X_3 with binary variables, so p(x_1,x_2,x_3\mid\theta) = \frac{1}{Z(\theta)}\psi_{1,2}(x_1,x_2\mid\theta_{1,2})\,\psi_{2,3}(x_2,x_3\mid\theta_{2,3}). Following the finite-discrete construction, index \theta_{1,2} by the four settings of (x_1,x_2): \theta_{1,2} := \begin{bmatrix}\log\psi_{1,2}(0,0)\\ \log\psi_{1,2}(0,1)\\ \log\psi_{1,2}(1,0)\\ \log\psi_{1,2}(1,1)\end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^4, with \phi_{1,2}(x_1,x_2) the corresponding indicator vector, e.g. \phi_{1,2}(0,1) = (0,1,0,0)^\top. This reproduces \log\psi_{1,2}(x_1,x_2\mid\theta_{1,2}) = \theta_{1,2}^\top \phi_{1,2}(x_1,x_2) exactly. If the potentials are taken to be normalized already (as the tutorial does), Z(\theta)=1; in general Z(\theta)=\sum_{\boldsymbol x}\exp\{\theta^\top\phi(\boldsymbol x)\}.

But this representation is not minimal: every setting of \phi_{1,2}(x_1,x_2) has entries summing to exactly 1 (only one indicator is ever “on”), so all four possible values of \phi_{1,2} lie on the hyperplane \{v : v^\top(1,1,1,1)^\top = 1\} rather than spanning \mathbb{R}^4. The tutorial says a non-minimal family fails the gradient identity \nabla A(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_\theta[\phi(X)]. More precisely, the identity still holds for the true log-partition function A(\theta)=\log Z(\theta); what fails is identifiability (different \theta give the same distribution, and A is not strictly convex). The identity only appears to fail if \theta is restricted to the set where Z(\theta)=1, because then A=0 there and the coordinates of \theta cannot vary independently. The fix is to drop the redundant first coordinate and re-express the other three relative to it: \bar\theta_{1,2} = \big(\theta^{(1)},\,\theta^{(2)},\,\theta^{(3)}\big), \quad \text{where} \theta^{(1)} = \log\psi_{1,2}(0,1)-\log\psi_{1,2}(0,0), \theta^{(2)} = \log\psi_{1,2}(1,0)-\log\psi_{1,2}(0,0), \theta^{(3)} = \log\psi_{1,2}(1,1)-\log\psi_{1,2}(0,0), \bar\phi_{1,2}(x_1,x_2) = \begin{bmatrix}(1-x_1)x_2\\ x_1(1-x_2)\\ x_1 x_2\end{bmatrix}, and analogously for \bar\theta_{2,3}, \bar\phi_{2,3}. This three-dimensional-per-edge parameterization is minimal, and its log-partition function, A(\bar\theta) = \log Z - \log\big(\psi_{1,2}(0,0)\,\psi_{2,3}(0,0)\big), which is -\log\big(\psi_{1,2}(0,0)\,\psi_{2,3}(0,0)\big) under the Z=1 convention, is no longer identically zero. The general lesson: writing every table entry as its own parameter is always possible but wastes one degree of freedom per clique to the normalization constraint; a minimal representation fixes a reference setting (here, (0,0)) and parameterizes only the contrast with it.

The Ising model

The log-linear form of MRFs as exponential families is general. Its simplest concrete instance is the Ising model: binary variables with one parameter per edge. On a grid it is the standard MRF for the kind of problem From DAGs to undirected graphs opened with, where each variable interacts symmetrically with its neighbours.

Definition. The Ising model is an MRF over spin variables x_s \in \{-1,+1\}, originally used to model magnets, with pairwise clique potentials \psi_{st}(x_s,x_t) = e^{J_{st}\,x_s x_t}, where J_{st} is the coupling strength between neighboring nodes s and t (J_{st}=0 if s,t are not connected).

Since x_s x_t = +1 when the two spins agree and -1 when they disagree, \psi_{st}(-1,-1)=\psi_{st}(1,1)=e^{J_{st}} while \psi_{st}(-1,1)=\psi_{st}(1,-1)=e^{-J_{st}}: a positive coupling J_{st}>0 makes the potential larger when the two spins agree, so the model favors, but does not force, matching neighbors. Adding a per-node potential \psi_s(x_s) = e^{b_s x_s} (an external field biasing each spin individually) gives the overall distribution p(\boldsymbol{x}) \propto \prod_{s\sim t} \psi_{st}(x_s,x_t) \prod_s \psi_s(x_s) = \exp\Big\{\sum_{s\sim t} J_{st}x_sx_t + \sum_s b_s x_s\Big\}, where s\sim t ranges over edges. This is exactly the log-linear exponential family of MRFs as exponential families, with sufficient statistics x_sx_t per edge and x_s per node. By Hammersley-Clifford, setting J_{st}=0 for a non-edge pair is precisely what makes X_s \perp X_t \mid X_{\text{rest}}.

Note

Practice question. In an Ising model with three spins X_1,X_2,X_3 arranged in the chain X_1-X_2-X_3 (so J_{13}=0), is X_1 \perp X_3 marginally (with X_2 unobserved), or only X_1 \perp X_3 \mid X_2?

Solution. J_{13}=0 says only that X_1 and X_3 are not directly coupled, which by Hammersley-Clifford gives the conditional statement X_1 \perp X_3 \mid X_2 (they are the two endpoints of a chain, and X_2 is the sole separator between them, exactly the causal-chain structure of Week 2’s Conditional independence: three canonical structures). It says nothing about the marginal relationship: both spins are still coupled to the same neighbor X_2, so marginally (summing X_2 out) X_1 and X_3 are typically dependent, the common-cause pattern from the same Week 2 section. This is the Ising-model instance of the general chain fact proved in The Hammersley-Clifford theorem.

Gaussian graphical models

The Ising model is the standard pairwise MRF for discrete variables. MRFs are not limited to discrete variables, though. For continuous variables, the multivariate Gaussian gives a pairwise MRF whose edges correspond directly to nonzero entries of a matrix, and it is again an exponential family.

Recall the multivariate normal: for X=(X_1,\dots,X_m), mean vector \mu\in\mathbb{R}^m, and a symmetric positive definite covariance matrix \Sigma (meaning u^\top \Sigma u > 0 for every nonzero u), we write X\sim N_m(\mu,\Sigma) if f(\boldsymbol{x};\mu,\Sigma) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}(\det\Sigma)^{1/2}} \times\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{x}-\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\mu)\right), with \mathbb{E}[X]=\mu and \mathrm{var}(X)=\Sigma. Splitting X into two blocks X=(X_A,X_B) with \mu=(\mu_A,\mu_B) and \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{AA} & \Sigma_{AB}\\ \Sigma_{BA} & \Sigma_{BB}\end{bmatrix}, the marginal is again Gaussian, X_A \sim N(\mu_A,\Sigma_{AA}), and the conditional is X_A \mid X_B=x_B \sim N\big(\mu_A + \Sigma_{AB}\Sigma_{BB}^{-1}(x_B-\mu_B),\; \Sigma_{AA}-\Sigma_{AB}\Sigma_{BB}^{-1}\Sigma_{BA}\big), where the conditional covariance does not depend on the observed value x_B, only on which variables were observed.

Reading independence off \Sigma. Marginal independence is simple: X_i \perp X_j if and only if \Sigma_{ij}=0. Conditioning on everything else is more useful for graphical models, and it is governed not by \Sigma but by its inverse. Let K = \Sigma^{-1}, the precision matrix. Writing R = V\setminus\{i,j\} for every other variable, the following are equivalent: X_i \perp X_j \mid X_R; \Sigma_{ij} - \Sigma_{i,R}\Sigma_{R,R}^{-1}\Sigma_{R,j} = 0; and (\Sigma^{-1})_{ij} = K_{ij} = 0. The last equivalence is the useful one: a single entry of the precision matrix tells us whether two variables are conditionally independent given everything else, with no matrix inversion needed per pair.

Definition. A Gaussian graphical model represents X\sim N(\mu,\Sigma) with precision matrix K=\Sigma^{-1} as an undirected graph on the coordinates of X, with an edge between i and j exactly when K_{ij}\neq 0. Writing out the density in terms of K, p(\boldsymbol{x}\mid\mu,\Sigma) \propto \prod_s e^{-\frac{1}{2}K_{ss}(x_s-\mu_s)^2} \prod_{s<t} e^{-K_{st}(x_s-\mu_s)(x_t-\mu_t)}, so K_{ij}=0 if and only if X_i \perp X_j \mid X_{\text{rest}}, matching the Hammersley-Clifford statement of The Hammersley-Clifford theorem.

Practice question. For the precision matrix in Figure 6, is X_1 \perp X_3? Is X_1 \perp X_3 \mid X_2?

Solution. Marginal independence is governed by \Sigma, not K, so the zero pattern of K alone does not answer the first question; in general \Sigma_{13}\neq 0 even though K_{13}=0, since matrix inversion mixes entries. For the conditional question, K_{13}=0 directly gives X_1 \perp X_3 \mid X_{\text{rest}} = X_1 \perp X_3 \mid \{X_2,X_4,X_5\}, conditioning on every other variable, not just X_2. Whether X_1\perp X_3\mid X_2 alone holds is a separate, graph-separation question: in the 5-cycle, removing only node 2 leaves a path 1-5-4-3 still connecting 1 and 3, so \{2\} does not separate them and X_1\not\perp X_3\mid X_2 in general.

Note

Homework-relevant. Fitting a Gaussian graphical model by maximum likelihood is a direct continuous-variable analogue of Week 1’s Bernoulli MLE (Maximum likelihood estimation in Week 1): same “write the log-likelihood, then optimize” pattern, different sufficient statistic.

Suppose we observe i.i.d. data \boldsymbol{x}_{1:n} = \{\boldsymbol{x}_1,\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_n\} from an m-variate Gaussian with (for simplicity, after centering the data) mean zero, and write K=\Sigma^{-1}. The log-density is \log f(\boldsymbol{x}; K) = -\tfrac{m}{2}\log(2\pi) + \tfrac{1}{2}\log\det K - \tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}^\top K \boldsymbol{x}. Dropping the constant that does not involve K, the log-likelihood over n points is \ell_n(K;\boldsymbol{x}_{1:n}) = \frac{n}{2}\log\det(K) - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n \boldsymbol{x}_i^\top K \boldsymbol{x}_i. The sum simplifies using the trace identity u^\top K u = \mathrm{tr}(Kuu^\top) and linearity of trace: \sum_{i=1}^n \boldsymbol{x}_i^\top K \boldsymbol{x}_i = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{tr}(K\boldsymbol{x}_i\boldsymbol{x}_i^\top) = n\,\mathrm{tr}(K S_n), S_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \boldsymbol{x}_i \boldsymbol{x}_i^\top, where S_n is the sample covariance matrix, playing the same role here that the sample mean played in Week 1’s Bernoulli MLE: it is the sufficient statistic that summarizes all n data points for the purpose of estimating K. Substituting back, \ell_n(K;\boldsymbol{x}_{1:n}) = \frac{n}{2}\big(\log\det(K) - \mathrm{tr}(KS_n)\big). Two facts about this objective, useful for actually solving the maximization: \log\det(K) is a strictly concave function of K, so the objective has at most one maximizer, and exactly one when S_n is invertible, and \mathrm{tr}(KS_n) is linear in K. Their gradients are \nabla \log\det(K) = K^{-1} and \nabla\, \mathrm{tr}(KS_n) = S_n, so setting the gradient of \ell_n to zero gives K^{-1} = S_n, i.e. the maximum likelihood estimate of the covariance matrix is simply the sample covariance matrix, and \hat K_{\text{MLE}} = S_n^{-1}: the same “set the derivative to zero, read off an empirical average” pattern as every MLE derivation so far this course, just with a matrix-valued statistic in place of a scalar one.

Exact inference as a conditional distribution

Everything up to here has been about representation: which distributions a graph describes, and how to parameterize them. Having built up two families of graphical models (DAGs in Week 2, MRFs in this lecture), we now turn to computation, with a question common to both: given the model, how do we compute an actual number?

Definition. In a graphical model over \boldsymbol{x}, split the variables into x_E (the observed evidence), x_F (the unobserved variable(s) we want to infer), and x_R = \boldsymbol{x} - \{x_F,x_E\} (remaining variables, summed out). Inference is computing the conditional distribution p(x_F\mid x_E) = \frac{p(x_F,x_E)}{p(x_E)} = \frac{p(x_F,x_E)}{\sum_{x_F} p(x_F,x_E)}, where the joint over the subject of inference and the evidence is obtained by marginalizing out the remaining variables, p(x_F,x_E) = \sum_{x_R} p(x_F,x_E,x_R).

Both the numerator and the normalizing sum over x_F require summing over x_R (and, for the denominator, over x_F too), so the real computational question is how to perform these sums efficiently, which is exactly what variable elimination addresses.

Variable elimination

The definition above reduces inference to sums over x_R. Done naively, a sum over n variables with k states each enumerates all k^n joint settings. The factorizations of Weeks 2 and 3 are what let us do better.

The order in which we marginalize out variables changes the computational cost dramatically, even though the final answer is the same regardless of order (summation commutes). Variable elimination is a simple, general exact-inference algorithm that applies to any graphical model, DAG or MRF, whose computational complexity depends on the graph structure and the chosen elimination order; it is a form of dynamic programming that avoids enumerating every joint assignment.

Worked example: a simple chain. Consider A\to B\to C\to D, and suppose we want the marginal p(D) with no evidence, so x_F=\{D\}, x_E=\{\}, x_R=\{A,B,C\}. Week 2’s chain rule gives p(A,B,C,D) = p(A)p(B\mid A)p(C\mid B)p(D\mid C).

Figure 7. A chain of four variables, used to compare naive marginalization against variable elimination.

Assume each variable takes k possible values. Marginalizing naively, p(D) = \sum_{A,B,C} p(A,B,C,D) = \sum_C \sum_B \sum_A p(A)p(B\mid A)p(C\mid B)p(D\mid C), costs O(k^4): every joint assignment of the four variables must be visited. Instead, choose an elimination order and push each sum as far inward as it will go, past every factor that does not mention the variable being summed: p(D) = \sum_C p(D\mid C) \left(\sum_B p(C\mid B) \sum_A p(A)p(B\mid A)\right). Working from the inside out, the innermost sum depends only on B once A is gone: \sum_A p(A)p(B\mid A) = p(B). Substituting back and repeating the same move for B: \sum_B p(C\mid B)\,p(B) = p(C). So the whole expression collapses to p(D) = \sum_C p(D\mid C)\,p(C). Every intermediate sum here touches at most two variables at once (k^2 terms), and there are n such sums, so the total cost is O(nk^2), compared to O(k^n) for constructing and marginalizing the full joint table directly: an exponential-to-polynomial improvement from exactly the same reordering trick that made Week 2’s chain-rule factorization efficient (Why factor the joint in Week 2).

Elimination ordering matters, and finding the best one is hard. The complexity of variable elimination depends on the chosen order, since different orders leave different numbers of variables inside each intermediate sum. Unfortunately, finding the elimination ordering that minimizes the worst intermediate sum is NP-hard in general, so in practice orderings are chosen by heuristics (or, in this course, simply given).

Sum-product inference: unifying DAGMs and MRFs

The chain example eliminated variables from a DAG’s conditional probabilities. Since the point of this lecture is MRFs, we need to check that the same algorithm works when the factors are clique potentials that need not sum to 1.

Variable elimination on a DAG is really the same algorithm as on an MRF, once we notice that a DAG’s conditional probability tables are themselves just a special kind of nonnegative potential. Introduce a nonnegative factor \phi for each conditional probability table (or each MRF potential): for a DAG on X,A,B,C with p(A,B,C) = \sum_X p(X)p(A\mid X)p(B\mid A)p(C\mid B,X), write \phi_1(X)=p(X), \phi_2(A,X)=p(A\mid X), \phi_3(A,B)=p(B\mid A), \phi_4(X,B,C)=p(C\mid B,X), so p(A,B,C) = \sum_X \phi_1(X)\phi_2(A,X)\phi_3(A,B)\phi_4(X,B,C). The factor \phi_3(A,B) does not involve X, so it can be pulled outside the sum over X: p(A,B,C) = \phi_3(A,B) \sum_X \phi_1(X)\phi_2(A,X)\phi_4(X,B,C) = \phi_3(A,B)\,\tau(A,B,C). Marginalizing out X produces a brand new factor \tau(A,B,C), an intermediate factor that generally is not any original conditional probability table; it is simply whatever function of the remaining variables the sum happened to produce. This is the mechanical step that repeats at every stage of variable elimination.

Abstractly, both DAG and MRF inference reduce to the same sum-product computation:

p(x_F\mid x_E) \propto \tau(x_F,x_E) = \sum_{x_R} \prod_{C\in\mathcal{F}} \psi_C(x_C),

where \mathcal{F} is the set of factors: for a DAG, \mathcal{F} consists of the sets \{i\}\cup\text{parents}(i) for every node i (one factor per conditional probability table); for an MRF, \mathcal{F} is the set of maximal cliques.

Worked example: variable elimination on a Bayes net

On the chain of Variable elimination, every intermediate sum involved at most two variables. On a graph with more branching, an intermediate factor can involve several variables, and the elimination order decides how many. The lecture’s student network shows this, using the sum-product form just stated.

Figure 8. A Bayes net over Coherence C, Difficulty D, Intelligence I, Grade G, SAT S, Letter L, Job J, and Happiness H.

This graph gives the factorization p(C,D,I,G,S,L,H,J) = p(C)p(D\mid C)p(I)\,p(G\mid D,I)p(L\mid G)p(S\mid I)p(J\mid S,L)p(H\mid J,G). Reading off one factor per node (its own conditional probability table), \mathcal{F} = \big\{\{C\},\{C,D\},\{I\},\{G,D,I\},\{L,G\},\{S,I\},\{J,S,L\},\{H,J,G\}\big\}, so m=|\mathcal{F}|=8.

Suppose we want p(J), the probability of getting a job, with the elimination order C,D,I,H,G,S,L (an ordering the instructor supplies; finding an optimal one is the NP-hard problem from Variable elimination). Eliminate C first: it appears only in \psi(C) and \psi(C,D), so \sum_C \psi(C)\,\psi(C,D) = \tau(D). Eliminate D next, in \psi(G,D,I) and the new \tau(D): \sum_D \psi(G,D,I)\,\tau(D) = \tau(G,I). Eliminate I, in \psi(S,I), \psi(I), and \tau(G,I): \sum_I \psi(S,I)\,\psi(I)\,\tau(G,I) = \tau(S,G). Eliminate H, which appears only in \psi(H,G,J): \sum_H \psi(H,G,J) = \tau(G,J). Eliminate G, now present in \psi(L,G), \tau(S,G), and \tau(G,J): \sum_G \psi(L,G)\,\tau(S,G)\,\tau(G,J) = \tau(J,L,S). Eliminate S, in \psi(J,L,S) and \tau(J,L,S): \sum_S \psi(J,L,S)\,\tau(J,L,S) = \tau(J,L). Finally eliminate L: \sum_L \tau(J,L) = \tau(J). The result, \tau(J), is proportional to p(J); since Z=1 for a DAG’s own conditional probability tables (they are already properly normalized), no further rescaling is needed here, though for a general MRF the final \tau would still require dividing by \sum_{x_F}\tau(x_F) to become an actual probability.

Reading off the complexity. Each sum above is labeled by how many variables appear inside it: \sum_C has N_C=2 (namely C,D), \sum_D has N_D=3 (G,D,I), \sum_I has N_I=3 (S,I,G), \sum_H has N_H=3 (H,G,J), \sum_G has N_G=4 (L,G,S,J, the largest), \sum_S has N_S=3, and \sum_L has N_L=2. The complexity of variable elimination under a given ordering is O\big(m\,k^{N_{\max}}\big), \qquad N_{\max}=\max_i N_i, where m is the number of initial factors and k is the (assumed common) number of states per variable. Here N_{\max}=4 (achieved at the G-elimination step), so the cost of this particular ordering is O(8k^4): substantially better than enumerating the full 8-variable joint, but not as good as the chain example of Variable elimination, because this graph has a genuinely more complex dependency structure than a simple chain.

Variable elimination with evidence

Inference as defined in Exact inference as a conditional distribution usually conditions on observed evidence x_E, but the student-network example computed a marginal with nothing observed. Reintroducing evidence changes only which variable is fixed rather than summed.

Worked example. Take the DAG X_1 \to X_2 \to X_4, X_1 \to X_3 \to X_5 \to X_6, X_2 \to X_6 (so X_6 has two parents, X_2 and X_5), giving p(x_1,\dots,x_6) = p(x_1)p(x_2\mid x_1)p(x_3\mid x_1)p(x_4\mid x_2)p(x_5\mid x_3)p(x_6\mid x_2,x_5). Suppose X_6=\bar x_6 is observed and we want p(X_1\mid \bar x_6), so x_F=\{x_1\}, x_E=\{x_6\}, x_R=\{x_2,x_3,x_4,x_5\}. By the definition in Exact inference as a conditional distribution, p(x_1\mid \bar x_6) = \frac{p(x_1,\bar x_6)}{\sum_{x_1} p(x_1,\bar x_6)}. Computing the numerator by variable elimination in the order 5,4,3,2 (the nested sums below run from x_2 outermost to x_5 innermost) (the observed value \bar x_6 is simply substituted into p(x_6\mid x_2,x_5) wherever it appears, rather than summed over): p(x_1,\bar x_6) = p(x_1)\sum_{x_2} p(x_2\mid x_1)\sum_{x_3} p(x_3\mid x_1)\sum_{x_4} p(x_4\mid x_2)\sum_{x_5} p(x_5\mid x_3)\,p(\bar x_6\mid x_2,x_5). The innermost sum over x_5 produces a new factor depending on x_2,x_3: \sum_{x_5} p(x_5\mid x_3)\,p(\bar x_6\mid x_2,x_5) = p(\bar x_6\mid x_2,x_3). The next sum, over x_4, involves only p(x_4\mid x_2), none of whose terms depend on x_4 after it is summed out (it sums to 1), so it drops out entirely: \sum_{x_4} p(x_4\mid x_2) = 1. This leaves p(x_1,\bar x_6) = p(x_1)\sum_{x_2} p(x_2\mid x_1)\sum_{x_3} p(x_3\mid x_1)\,p(\bar x_6\mid x_2,x_3). Summing x_3 next produces p(\bar x_6\mid x_1,x_2) (using \sum_{x_3}p(x_3\mid x_1)\,p(\bar x_6\mid x_2,x_3) = p(\bar x_6\mid x_1,x_2) by the product and sum rules), and then x_2: p(x_1,\bar x_6) = p(x_1)\sum_{x_2} p(x_2\mid x_1)\,p(\bar x_6\mid x_1,x_2) = p(x_1)\,p(\bar x_6\mid x_1). So the whole computation collapses to the two-factor product one would expect from a two-node chain X_1 \to X_6, even though the actual graph has six nodes; every other variable was marginalized away without ever touching a table larger than three variables at once. Normalizing gives the final answer: p(x_1\mid \bar x_6) = \frac{p(x_1)\,p(\bar x_6\mid x_1)}{\sum_{x_1} p(x_1)\,p(\bar x_6\mid x_1)}. This is Bayes’ rule from Week 1 (Worked example: a diagnostic test in Week 1), rederived as a special case of variable elimination: computing a posterior for a two-variable relationship, after algebraically marginalizing away every variable that connects them only indirectly.

Restricted Boltzmann machines

Variable elimination is exact but can be expensive, and every MRF query seems to need the partition function Z, itself a sum over all variables. The lecture closes with a model whose graph structure makes some queries cheap without ever computing Z. As a modern application tying together the Ising model, exponential families, and Hammersley-Clifford, consider a restricted Boltzmann machine (RBM): a special Ising model over visible variables X_1,\dots,X_k and hidden variables H_1,\dots,H_l, all in \{-1,+1\}, whose graph is bipartite: every H_i connects to every X_j, but no H_i-H_{i'} or X_j-X_{j'} edges exist at all.

Figure 9. An RBM’s graph: every hidden node connects to every visible node, and there are no hidden-hidden or visible-visible edges.

Writing the Ising-style distribution with per-edge potentials \psi_{X_i,H_j}(x_i,h_j) = \exp\{\frac{1}{l}\alpha_i x_i + \frac{1}{k}\beta_j h_j + J_{ij}x_ih_j\} (splitting the node biases evenly across the edges touching each node so that the product of edge potentials reproduces the full node bias), the joint is p(x_1,\dots,x_k,h_1,\dots,h_l) = \frac{1}{Z}\prod_{i=1}^k\prod_{j=1}^l \psi_{X_i,H_j}(x_i,h_j). Computing Z exactly can be expensive (it is a sum over all 2^{k+l} joint settings), but the bipartite structure lets us compute the two conditional distributions p(x\mid h) and p(h\mid x) efficiently, without ever needing Z. This is exactly what the Hammersley-Clifford theorem predicts: since there are no edges between different H_i’s, they are all mutually independent given X (every H_i-H_{i'} pair is separated by the fully-observed X, the same non-adjacent-pair rule from The Hammersley-Clifford theorem), so the joint conditional factors into a per-node product, and each factor can be computed on its own: p(h_j\mid x) = \frac{\prod_{i=1}^k \psi_{ij}(x_i,h_j)}{\prod_{i=1}^k\psi_{ij}(x_i,-1) + \prod_{i=1}^k\psi_{ij}(x_i,1)} = \sigma\Big(\beta_j + \sum_i J_{ij}x_i\Big), where \sigma(y) = \frac{e^y}{e^{-y}+e^y} = \frac{1}{1+e^{-2y}} is the sigmoid function. This gives a probabilistic reading of the sigmoid activation used in neural networks: it is exactly the conditional-probability formula that falls out of treating a layer of binary units as conditionally independent given the layer below, under an Ising-style pairwise potential.

Summary

Note

Key takeaways from Week 3:

  • Markov random fields (MRFs) model undirected interactions: p(\boldsymbol{x}) \propto \prod_{C\in\mathcal{C}} \psi_C(x_C) over maximal cliques \mathcal{C}. The Hammersley-Clifford theorem says this factorization is equivalent to the global Markov property, X_A\perp X_B\mid X_S whenever S separates A from B in the graph.
  • MRFs and DAGs are not nested: a 4-cycle’s independencies cannot be captured by any DAG, and explaining-away cannot be captured by any MRF.
  • MRFs are exponential families when potentials are log-linear; a minimal representation drops one redundant coordinate per clique. The Ising model and Gaussian graphical models (where K_{ij}=0 \iff X_i\perp X_j\mid X_{\text{rest}} for the precision matrix K) are the two running examples, and Gaussian MLE reduces to \hat K^{-1}=S_n, the sample covariance.
  • Inference is computing p(x_F\mid x_E) by marginalizing out x_R. Variable elimination does this efficiently by choosing an order and pushing sums inward; the same sum-product algorithm handles DAGs and MRFs via a shared notion of factors, and the resulting intermediate factors (\tau) are what make the computation dynamic-programming-style rather than exponential.
  • Restricted Boltzmann machines are a bipartite Ising model where Hammersley-Clifford guarantees the hidden units are conditionally independent given the visible ones (and vice versa), giving the sigmoid activation as a direct consequence rather than an ad hoc choice.
