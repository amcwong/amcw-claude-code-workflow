Week 1: Introduction and Probabilistic Models

Week 1: Introduction and Probabilistic Models

Overview. This lecture asks what probabilistic machine learning is, then builds the first technical objects the rest of the course uses: families of distributions indexed by a parameter, and a rule for fitting that parameter to data. We start from the four standard ML problem settings, recast them as estimating a distribution, review joint, marginal, and conditional probability on a diagnostic-test example, and then introduce parametric models, maximum likelihood, sufficient statistics, and exponential families. Directed graphical models, exact inference, sampling, and variational inference appear only as a course roadmap and are deferred.

Logistics slides (grading, Piazza, policies, calendar) are skipped.

What is machine learning?

How can we design programs for problems that are too complex to specify as a fixed set of rules (recognizing a face, transcribing speech)?

Definition. Machine learning (ML) is the design of algorithms whose behavior is learned from data or experience rather than written as hand-coded rules.

The lecture names four standard settings, distinguished by what is observed and what signal drives learning:

  • Supervised learning: given input–output pairs (x^{(i)}, y^{(i)}), learn a mapping f from inputs x to outputs y.
  • Unsupervised learning: given only unlabelled inputs x^{(i)}, find structure or relations among them (the objective varies by task).
  • Semi-supervised learning: a small amount of labelled data plus a large amount of unlabelled x^{(i)}.
  • Reinforcement learning: a learning system receives a reward signal and learns to act so as to maximize it.

The reason this is a probabilistic ML course is that all four settings can be cast as estimating a probability distribution from data: p(y \mid x) for supervised learning, p(x) for unsupervised learning, p(x, y) for the joint case. The through-line of the course is how we specify, fit, and use such distributions.

ML and statistics. Statistics is more concerned with helping people draw sound conclusions from data (interpretability, mathematical rigour). ML is more concerned with building systems with a desired behavior (predictive performance, scalability, autonomy). The two fields share almost all their mathematical machinery. This course will look a lot like a statistics course in its formulas. The purpose to which that machinery is put differs.

Discriminative vs generative models

In supervised classification we observe pairs (x^{(i)}, c^{(i)}), where c denotes a discrete class label (to distinguish it from a continuous output y).

Definition. A discriminative model specifies only the conditional p(c \mid x). A generative model specifies the joint p(c, x) (or p(x,y)). The joint is more work to write down, but it also lets us generate new synthetic x’s from a given class via p(x \mid c).

Joint, marginal, and conditional distributions

The rest of this week’s material is about discrete distributions over a small number of variables. We pin down three objects using the diagnostic-test example that the tutorial works in full (infected or not; test positive or not). That example is also the running numeric example below.

For two discrete random variables H and Y:

Definition. The joint distribution p(H, Y) is the probability of every combination of outcomes (e.g. p(H=1, Y=0)). All entries are non-negative and sum to 1 over every combination.

The marginal distribution p(H) is obtained by summing the joint over the other variable: p(H=h) = \sum_y p(H=h, Y=y).

The conditional distribution p(H \mid Y) is the distribution of H once Y’s value is known: p(H=h \mid Y=y) = \dfrac{p(H=h, Y=y)}{p(Y=y)}.

The last identity (conditional equals joint divided by marginal) is Bayes’ theorem once you also expand the joint using the other factorization.

TipRecap: Joint, marginal, and conditional distributions

What the symbols stand for:

  • H and Y: two discrete random variables (health state and test result in the running example).
  • h and y: particular values those variables can take, such as h=1 or y=0.
  • p(H=h, Y=y): the joint, one number per combination of values.
  • p(H=h): the marginal, the probability of H=h when we ignore Y.
  • p(H=h \mid Y=y): the conditional, the probability of H=h once we know Y=y.

Taking the equations apart:

p(H=h) = \sum_y p(H=h, Y=y)

  • The left side asks about H alone.
  • The sum runs over every value Y could take, so we add up every joint cell in which H=h. Summing out Y is what “ignoring Y” means in symbols.

p(H=h \mid Y=y) = \frac{p(H=h, Y=y)}{p(Y=y)}

  • The numerator keeps only the cell where both events happen.
  • The denominator is the total probability of the thing we learned, Y=y. Dividing by it rescales the surviving cells so they sum to 1 again.

A teaching example. Take a different test with this joint table: p(H{=}1,Y{=}1)=0.06, p(H{=}1,Y{=}0)=0.04, p(H{=}0,Y{=}1)=0.09, p(H{=}0,Y{=}0)=0.81. The four cells sum to 1. Marginalizing gives p(H{=}1) = 0.06 + 0.04 = 0.10 and p(Y{=}1) = 0.06 + 0.09 = 0.15. Conditioning on a positive test keeps the Y{=}1 cells and rescales them: p(H{=}1 \mid Y{=}1) = 0.06/0.15 = 0.4 and p(H{=}0 \mid Y{=}1) = 0.09/0.15 = 0.6, which sum to 1 as a distribution over H should.

Worked example: a diagnostic test

Let H = 1 mean infected and H=0 mean not infected; let Y=1 mean the test is positive and Y=0 mean it is negative. We want p(H=h \mid Y=y): given the test result, the chance of actually being infected.

Three numbers describe the situation:

  • Sensitivity (true positive rate): p(Y=1 \mid H=1) = 0.875.
  • Specificity (true negative rate): p(Y=0 \mid H=0) = 0.975.
  • Prevalence (the prior): p(H=1) = 0.1 in the first scenario.

Step 1. Build the joint. Each cell is (conditional probability of the test result) times (prior probability of the health state):

p(Y{=}0,H{=}0) = 0.975 \cdot 0.9 = 0.8775 \qquad p(Y{=}0,H{=}1) = 0.125 \cdot 0.1 = 0.0125 p(Y{=}1,H{=}0) = 0.025 \cdot 0.9 = 0.0225 \qquad p(Y{=}1,H{=}1) = 0.875 \cdot 0.1 = 0.0875

These four numbers are non-negative and sum to 1.

Y \backslash H H=0 H=1 marginal over Y
Y=0 0.8775 0.0125 0.89
Y=1 0.0225 0.0875 0.11
marginal over H 0.9 0.1

Step 2. Condition. The marginal p(Y{=}1) = 0.11 sums the Y{=}1 row, so

p(H{=}1 \mid Y{=}1) = \frac{p(Y{=}1, H{=}1)}{p(Y{=}1)} = \frac{0.0875}{0.11} \approx 0.795.

A positive test means a 79.5% chance of infection. Similarly, p(H{=}1 \mid Y{=}0) = 0.0125 / 0.89 \approx 0.014: a negative test leaves a 1.4% chance of infection.

Figure 1. Building the joint of (H,Y) by multiplying prior times conditional along each branch. Conditioning on a positive test (the two Y=1 leaves) and renormalizing gives p(H=1\mid Y=1) = 0.0875 / 0.11 \approx 79.5\%.

If the prevalence is lower, say p(H{=}1)=0.01 instead of 0.1, the same arithmetic drops the positive-test posterior to about 26%, even though sensitivity and specificity have not changed. When the disease is rare, most positive tests come from the much larger pool of uninfected people who got a false positive. This is the base-rate effect: a direct consequence of Bayes’ rule.

This example is itself a parametric model: the joint of (Y,H) is completely determined by three numbers (prevalence, sensitivity, specificity).

TipRecap: Worked example: a diagnostic test

What the symbols stand for:

  • H=1 / H=0: infected / not infected. Y=1 / Y=0: test positive / negative.
  • Sensitivity p(Y{=}1 \mid H{=}1): how often the test catches a real infection.
  • Specificity p(Y{=}0 \mid H{=}0): how often the test correctly clears a healthy person. One minus specificity is the false-positive rate.
  • Prevalence p(H{=}1): the prior, how common the infection is before any test.

Taking the computation apart. Each joint cell is “how likely this test result is for this health state” times “how common this health state is”:

p(Y{=}y, H{=}h) = p(Y{=}y \mid H{=}h)\, p(H{=}h).

The posterior after a positive test then has a numerator of true positives and a denominator of all positives:

p(H{=}1 \mid Y{=}1) = \frac{p(Y{=}1 \mid H{=}1)\,p(H{=}1)}{p(Y{=}1 \mid H{=}1)\,p(H{=}1) + p(Y{=}1 \mid H{=}0)\,p(H{=}0)}.

  • The first term in the denominator is the same as the numerator: infected people who test positive.
  • The second term is healthy people who test positive anyway. When it is large, the posterior is small.

A teaching example. The section states that dropping prevalence to 0.01 gives about 26%. Here is the arithmetic. True positives: 0.875 \times 0.01 = 0.00875. False positives: 0.025 \times 0.99 = 0.02475. All positives: 0.00875 + 0.02475 = 0.0335. Posterior: 0.00875 / 0.0335 \approx 0.261. The false positives outnumber the true positives almost three to one because the healthy group is 99 times larger, which is the base-rate effect.

Parametric models and the likelihood

Rather than considering every possible distribution over the data, we restrict attention to a family indexed by a small number of parameters.

Definition. A parametric model is a set of distributions \mathcal{P} = \{p(x \mid \theta) : \theta \in \Theta\}, indexed by a parameter \theta ranging over a parameter space \Theta.

Example: \mathcal{P} = \{p(x\mid\theta) = \mathcal{N}(\theta, 1) : \theta \in \mathbb{R}\} is the family of Gaussians with variance fixed at 1 and mean \theta free. In the diagnostic-test example, \Theta was 3-dimensional (prevalence, sensitivity, specificity).

The course’s stated goal: given data believed to come from some true distribution p^*, and a family \mathcal{P}, find the member p \in \mathcal{P} that best approximates p^*, and do so efficiently.

Suppose the data \mathcal{D} = \{x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(N)}\} are drawn i.i.d. (independently and identically distributed: each point drawn independently from the same distribution) from p(x \mid \theta^*) for some unknown true \theta^*. Independence makes the joint probability of the dataset factor as a product, the likelihood function \mathcal{L}(\theta; \mathcal{D}):

p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta) = \prod_{i=1}^N p(x^{(i)} \mid \theta).

Products of many small numbers are numerically awkward, and calculus is easier on sums, so we almost always work with the log-likelihood:

\ell(\theta; \mathcal{D}) = \log \mathcal{L}(\theta;\mathcal{D}) = \sum_{i=1}^N \log p(x^{(i)} \mid \theta).

TipRecap: Parametric models and the likelihood

What the symbols stand for:

  • \theta: the parameter, the knob that picks one distribution out of the family.
  • \Theta: the set of allowed values of \theta (for a coin, the interval [0,1]).
  • \mathcal{P}: the family itself, one distribution p(x \mid \theta) for each \theta \in \Theta.
  • \theta^*: the unknown true parameter that generated the data.
  • \mathcal{D} = \{x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(N)}\}: the dataset of N observations. The superscript (i) indexes data points, not powers.
  • \mathcal{L}(\theta;\mathcal{D}) and \ell(\theta;\mathcal{D}): the likelihood and the log-likelihood. Both are functions of \theta with the data held fixed.

Taking the equations apart:

  • p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta) = \prod_{i} p(x^{(i)} \mid \theta): the probability of the whole dataset is a product of one factor per data point. The product is allowed only because the points are independent.
  • \ell = \sum_i \log p(x^{(i)} \mid \theta): taking the log turns that product into a sum, because \log(ab) = \log a + \log b. Each data point contributes one additive term.

A teaching example. Model a coin as p(x \mid \theta) = \theta^x(1-\theta)^{1-x} and observe \mathcal{D} = \{1, 0, 1\}. At \theta = 0.6 the three factors are 0.6, 0.4, 0.6, so \mathcal{L} = 0.144 and \ell = \log 0.6 + \log 0.4 + \log 0.6 \approx -0.511 - 0.916 - 0.511 = -1.938, which matches \log 0.144. At \theta = 0.5 the likelihood is 0.5^3 = 0.125. The data are more probable under \theta = 0.6, so the likelihood prefers it. The next section turns “prefers” into a rule.

Maximum likelihood estimation

Which member of the family should we pick? A natural rule is: the parameter value under which the data we actually observed would have been most probable.

Definition. Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) chooses \hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = \arg\max_\theta \, \ell(\theta; \mathcal{D}) = \arg\max_\theta \, \mathcal{L}(\theta;\mathcal{D}).

Maximizing \ell and maximizing \mathcal{L} give the same \hat\theta because \log is a strictly increasing function: it never reorders which \theta is best; it just makes the optimization easier.

Note

Worked example: MLE for a Bernoulli. Let x^{(i)} = 1 with probability \theta (heads) and x^{(i)}=0 with probability 1-\theta (tails), for i = 1,\dots,N i.i.d. trials. The log-likelihood is \begin{aligned} \ell(\theta;\mathcal{D}) &= \sum_{i=1}^N \Big[x^{(i)}\log\theta + (1-x^{(i)})\log(1-\theta)\Big] \\ &= \Big(\sum_i x^{(i)}\Big)\log\theta + \Big(N - \sum_i x^{(i)}\Big)\log(1-\theta). \end{aligned} Setting the derivative with respect to \theta to zero, \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta} &= \frac{\sum_i x^{(i)}}{\theta} - \frac{N - \sum_i x^{(i)}}{1-\theta} = 0 \\ \implies \hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N x^{(i)}. \end{aligned} The MLE of a Bernoulli parameter is the observed fraction of ones. (A homework that asks you to derive an MLE for a different model is left to the assignment; the same derivative-of-the-log-likelihood steps apply.)

TipRecap: Maximum likelihood estimation

What the symbols stand for:

  • \hat\theta_{\text{MLE}}: the estimate, the single \theta we pick. The hat marks “computed from data”.
  • \arg\max_\theta: “the value of \theta that makes the following expression largest”. It returns the location of the peak, not the height.
  • \sum_i x^{(i)}: the number of ones (heads) in the data. N - \sum_i x^{(i)} is the number of zeros.

Taking the Bernoulli derivation apart:

  • \ell(\theta) = (\#\text{ones})\log\theta + (\#\text{zeros})\log(1-\theta). Each head contributes \log\theta and each tail contributes \log(1-\theta).
  • \partial\ell/\partial\theta = \#\text{ones}/\theta - \#\text{zeros}/(1-\theta). The first term pulls \theta up, the second pulls it down.
  • Setting the derivative to zero finds where the two pulls balance, which is \theta = \#\text{ones}/N.

A teaching example. Ten flips give 7 heads and 3 tails. At \theta = 0.5 the derivative is 7/0.5 - 3/0.5 = 8 > 0, so the log-likelihood is still rising and we should increase \theta. At \theta = 0.7 it is 7/0.7 - 3/0.3 = 10 - 10 = 0, the balance point. So \hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = 0.7, the fraction of heads.

Sufficient statistics

In the Bernoulli example, the log-likelihood only ever referred to the data through one number: S = \sum_i x^{(i)}, the total count of ones. It did not matter which trials were heads, or in what order, only how many.

Definition. T(x) is a sufficient statistic for \theta in the model p(x\mid\theta) if and only if the density factors as p(x\mid\theta) = h(x)\, g_\theta\big(T(x)\big) for some function h that does not depend on \theta, and some function g_\theta. This is the Fisher–Neyman factorization theorem. All dependence on \theta is routed through T(x), so once you know T(x), the rest of x tells you nothing more about \theta.

Checking the factorization on the Bernoulli likelihood from above: p(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^N \theta^{x^{(i)}}(1-\theta)^{1-x^{(i)}} = \theta^{\,S}(1-\theta)^{\,N-S}, \qquad S := \sum_{i=1}^N x^{(i)}. The individual data values never appear on the right except through S. That is the factorization with h(\mathcal{D})=1 and T(\mathcal{D})=S, which is why the MLE derivation only needed S.

TipRecap: Sufficient statistics

What the symbols stand for:

  • T(x): the sufficient statistic, a summary computed from the data (here, a count).
  • h(x): a factor that may depend on the data but never on \theta.
  • g_\theta(T(x)): a factor that depends on \theta, and on the data only through T(x).
  • S = \sum_i x^{(i)}: the Bernoulli sufficient statistic, the number of ones.

Taking the factorization apart:

p(x \mid \theta) = h(x) \; g_\theta\big(T(x)\big)

  • When we compare two values of \theta, the h(x) factor is the same for both, so it cancels out of the comparison.
  • Everything that does change with \theta looks at the data only through T(x). Two datasets with the same T therefore give the same verdict about \theta.

A teaching example. Take N = 4 flips in two different orders: \{1,1,0,0\} and \{0,1,0,1\}. Both have S = 2. At \theta = 0.3 the first dataset has likelihood 0.3 \cdot 0.3 \cdot 0.7 \cdot 0.7 = 0.0441, and the second has 0.7 \cdot 0.3 \cdot 0.7 \cdot 0.3 = 0.0441. Both equal \theta^S(1-\theta)^{N-S} = 0.3^2 \cdot 0.7^2. The order of the flips changed nothing, which is what “S is sufficient” means.

Exponential families

Which “family”?

Word as used here What varies Where else it shows up
Parametric model / family \mathcal{P} which member distribution is chosen any parametric model, not just exponential ones
Exponential family a specific algebraic form that many parametric models share Bernoulli, Multinomial, Gaussian (this week); GLMs and undirected graphical models (later)
Natural parameter \eta the coordinate system exponential families are written in related to \theta in p(x\mid\theta), but not always the same coordinates

Every exponential family is a parametric model. Not every parametric model belongs to the exponential family.

The general form

Bernoulli, Gaussian, Multinomial, and many named distributions can be written in one template. Once a distribution is in that template, its sufficient statistic, a general MLE recipe, and its mean come for free.

Definition. A distribution belongs to the exponential family if its density (or mass function) can be written p(x\mid\eta) = h(x)\exp\{\eta^\top T(x) - A(\eta)\} with four named pieces: sufficient statistic T(x), natural parameter \eta, log-partition function A(\eta) (the normalizer), and carrying measure h(x) (the \eta-independent factor).

Writing the density this way isolates all parameter-dependence into the inner product \eta^\top T(x), which is the structure Fisher–Neyman asks for. Every exponential-family member therefore has T(x) as a sufficient statistic.

Named examples in this family (per the lecture): Bernoulli, (multivariate) Gaussian, gamma, exponential, chi-squared, beta, Dirichlet, Poisson, geometric, and, as derived next, the Multinomial and the univariate Gaussian.

Figure 2. The four pieces of an exponential family. All parameter-dependence routes through \eta and T(x) via a single inner product, which is what Fisher–Neyman requires for T(x) to be sufficient.

Recipe: putting a distribution into exponential-family form

How do we take a familiar density, written in terms of its usual parameter \theta, and recognise the four pieces? The target is

p(x\mid\theta) = h(x)\exp\{\eta^\top T(x) - A(\eta)\},

and the work is sorting every piece of \log p(x\mid\theta) by what it depends on: the data x, the parameter \theta, or both.

Recipe.

  1. Get an exponential. If the density has no obvious \exp\{\cdot\}, write it as p = \exp\{\log p\}. Then expand \log p with the log rules (\log ab = \log a + \log b, \log a^x = x\log a) until it is a sum of simple terms.
  2. Sort each term into one of three bins.
    • Mixed: depends on both x and \theta.
    • Parameter-only: depends on \theta but not on x.
    • Data-only: depends on x but not on \theta (pure constants can go here too).
  3. Mixed bin gives \eta and T(x). Factor each mixed term as (a function of \theta) times (a function of x). Group terms that share the same function of x. Each distinct function of x is one entry of T(x), and the function of \theta multiplying it is the matching entry of \eta.
  4. Parameter-only bin gives A(\eta). The sum of these terms equals -A (note the minus sign in the template). It is first written in terms of \theta, so invert the map from step 3 (solve \eta(\theta) for \theta) and substitute, to get A as a function of \eta.
  5. Data-only bin gives h(x). h(x) is \exp of the data-only terms, times any factor sitting outside the exponential that depends only on x.
  6. Check A. Because the density must sum (or integrate) to 1, A(\eta) = \log\sum_x h(x)\exp\{\eta^\top T(x)\}. Computing this sum directly should reproduce the A from step 4.
TipQuestion: Where does the log-sum formula for the log-partition function come from?

Start from the requirement that the density sums to 1 over every possible outcome x:

\sum_x p(x\mid\eta) = \sum_x h(x)\exp\{\eta^\top T(x) - A(\eta)\} = 1.

A(\eta) does not depend on x, so it comes out of the sum as a constant factor. Using \exp\{a - b\} = \exp\{a\}\exp\{-b\}:

\exp\{-A(\eta)\}\sum_x h(x)\exp\{\eta^\top T(x)\} = 1.

Multiply both sides by \exp\{A(\eta)\}:

\sum_x h(x)\exp\{\eta^\top T(x)\} = \exp\{A(\eta)\}.

Taking the log of both sides gives A(\eta) = \log\sum_x h(x)\exp\{\eta^\top T(x)\}.

So A is not a free choice. Whatever h and T are, A must be the log of the sum that makes the probabilities add to 1. That sum is the normalizing constant, and A is its logarithm. For a continuous x, replace the sum with an integral; the steps do not change. The sum must be finite for this to work, which limits \eta to the values where it is.

Step 6 is therefore a check, not an extra step. If the sum and the step 4 result disagree, the sort in step 2 or the inversion in step 4 contains a mistake.

For the Bernoulli with h(x)=1 and T(x)=x on x\in\{0,1\}, the sum is \sum_x e^{\eta x} = 1+e^\eta, so A(\eta)=\log(1+e^\eta). This matches the parameter-only bin computed above.

Two points refine the short version “the thing multiplying the data is \eta”.

  • “The data” means T(x), which need not be x itself. It can be x^2, \log x, an indicator \mathbb{1}[x=i], or a count. The Gaussian below has two entries, x and x^2, so \eta is a 2-vector.
  • Not every term in the exponent belongs to \eta. A term that does not multiply any function of x (the parameter-only bin) carries no information about which x was observed. Its only job is to make the probabilities sum to 1, which is exactly the role of the normalizer A.

If a term cannot be split into (function of \theta) times (function of x), for example \log(x+\theta), or if the set of possible x values itself depends on \theta (a uniform on [0,\theta]), the distribution is not in the exponential family.

With N i.i.d. draws the same recipe applies to the joint density \prod_n p(x^{(n)}\mid\theta): the logs add, so T becomes a sum over draws (a count of ones, a sum of x’s) and A is multiplied by N. The Multinomial section below works in that form.

Bernoulli as an exponential family

We now run the recipe on the single-trial Bernoulli mass function

p(x\mid\theta) = \theta^x(1-\theta)^{1-x}, \qquad x\in\{0,1\}.

Step 1: get an exponential. There is no \exp yet, so take the log and exponentiate it back:

p(x\mid\theta) = \exp\big\{\log\big[\theta^x(1-\theta)^{1-x}\big]\big\} = \exp\big\{x\log\theta + (1-x)\log(1-\theta)\big\}.

Step 2: sort the terms. The second term still mixes a pure-parameter part with a data part, so distribute (1-x) first:

x\log\theta + (1-x)\log(1-\theta) = x\log\theta - x\log(1-\theta) + \log(1-\theta).

The first two terms are mixed (each contains both x and \theta). The last term, \log(1-\theta), is parameter-only. There are no data-only terms.

Step 3: read off \eta and T(x). Both mixed terms multiply the same function of the data, x, so factor it out:

x\log\theta - x\log(1-\theta) = x\,\big[\log\theta - \log(1-\theta)\big] = x\log\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}.

Matching to \eta\,T(x):

T(x) = x, \qquad \eta = \log\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}.

The quantity \log\frac{\theta}{1-\theta} is the log-odds of heads versus tails.

Why is \log(1-\theta) not part of \eta? It does not multiply x. Whether we observe x=0 or x=1, this term contributes the same amount \log(1-\theta) to the exponent, so it does not distinguish one outcome from the other. In the template, \eta is defined only as the coefficient of T(x); anything that is left over and depends only on \theta is the normalizer.

Step 4: get A(\eta). The parameter-only term is -A:

-A = \log(1-\theta) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A = -\log(1-\theta).

This is still in terms of \theta. To write it in terms of \eta, invert \eta = \log\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}:

e^\eta = \frac{\theta}{1-\theta} \;\Longrightarrow\; e^\eta(1-\theta) = \theta \;\Longrightarrow\; \theta = \frac{e^\eta}{1+e^\eta}.

This inverse is the logistic sigmoid. It follows that

1-\theta = 1 - \frac{e^\eta}{1+e^\eta} = \frac{1}{1+e^\eta},

and substituting into A = -\log(1-\theta):

A(\eta) = -\log\frac{1}{1+e^\eta} = \log(1+e^\eta).

Step 5: get h(x). No data-only terms and no factor outside the exponential, so h(x) = 1.

Step 6: check A by normalizing. Summing h(x)\exp\{\eta x\} over the two outcomes:

\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}} e^{\eta x} = e^{0} + e^{\eta} = 1+e^\eta, \qquad \log(1+e^\eta) = A(\eta). \checkmark

Collecting the pieces:

T(x) = x \qquad \eta = \log\frac{\theta}{1-\theta} \qquad A(\eta) = \log(1+e^{\eta}) \qquad h(x) = 1.

T(x) x the function of the data in the mixed terms
\eta \log\frac{\theta}{1-\theta} the coefficient of T(x)
A(\eta) \log(1+e^\eta) minus the parameter-only term, rewritten via \theta = e^\eta/(1+e^\eta)
h(x) 1 no data-only terms

The K-class Multinomial below uses the same shape, \eta_i = \log(q_i/q_K), with Bernoulli as the K=2 case.

A check against the general fact \mathbb{E}[T(X)] = A'(\eta) proved later: A(\eta) = \log(1+e^\eta) has A'(\eta) = e^\eta/(1+e^\eta) = \theta = \mathbb{E}[X], and A''(\eta) = \theta(1-\theta), the Bernoulli variance.

TipRecap: Exponential families

What the symbols stand for:

  • \eta: the natural parameter, the coefficient that multiplies the data inside the exponent.
  • T(x): the sufficient statistic, the function of the data that \eta multiplies.
  • \eta^\top T(x): their inner product. When both are vectors this is \sum_j \eta_j T_j(x); when both are scalars it is just \eta\,T(x).
  • A(\eta): the log-partition function, the normalizer that makes the probabilities sum to 1.
  • h(x): the carrying measure, everything that depends on the data alone.
  • \theta: the familiar parameter (for a Bernoulli, the probability of heads). \eta is the same information in different coordinates.

Taking the template apart:

p(x \mid \eta) = h(x)\exp\{\eta^\top T(x) - A(\eta)\}

  • \eta^\top T(x) is the only place where data and parameter interact. Different outcomes x get different scores through this term.
  • -A(\eta) is the same for every outcome. Its job is to shift the scores so the probabilities add to 1.
  • h(x) adjusts outcomes in a way that does not depend on the parameter.

For the Bernoulli, \eta = \log\frac{\theta}{1-\theta} (log-odds), T(x) = x, A(\eta) = \log(1+e^\eta), h(x) = 1. The map back from \eta to \theta is the sigmoid \theta = e^\eta/(1+e^\eta).

A teaching example. Take a coin with \theta = 0.8. Its log-odds is \eta = \log(0.8/0.2) = \log 4 \approx 1.386, and A(\eta) = \log(1 + 4) = \log 5 \approx 1.609. Plug both outcomes into the template:

  • x = 1: \exp\{1.386 - 1.609\} = \exp\{-0.223\} \approx 0.8.
  • x = 0: \exp\{0 - 1.609\} = 1/5 = 0.2.

The template reproduces the coin. Going back through the sigmoid, e^\eta/(1+e^\eta) = 4/5 = 0.8 = \theta, and A''(\eta) = 0.8 \times 0.2 = 0.16 is the coin’s variance.

Multinomial as an exponential family

A Multinomial random variable X takes one of K discrete states i \in \{1,\dots,K\}, each with probability q_i, where q_i \ge 0 and \sum_i q_i = 1 (a K-class label, or an unfair K-sided die). We observe N i.i.d. draws \mathcal{D} = \{x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(N)}\}.

Step 1. Likelihood in terms of counts. Let N_i = \sum_{n=1}^N \mathbb{1}[x^{(n)} = i]. Then

p(\mathcal{D}; \mathbf{q}) = \exp\Big(\sum_{i=1}^K N_i \log q_i\Big).

The sufficient statistics are the counts N_1, \dots, N_K.

Step 2. The q_i are not free: \sum_i q_i = 1. Write q_K = 1-\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}q_i and peel out the i=K term:

\sum_{i=1}^K N_i\log q_i = \sum_{i=1}^{K-1} N_i \log\frac{q_i}{q_K} \;+\; N\log q_K.

That split is \eta^\top T(x) - A(\eta) for i=1,\dots,K-1:

T(x)_i = N_i \qquad \eta_i = \log\frac{q_i}{q_K} \qquad h(x)=1 \qquad A(\eta) = N\log\Big(1+\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}e^{\eta_i}\Big).

(Invert \eta_i=\log(q_i/q_K) to get q_i/q_K = e^{\eta_i}, sum to get 1/q_K = 1+\sum_i e^{\eta_i}, substitute into N\log(1/q_K).)

Step 3. MLE under \sum_i q_i = 1, via a Lagrange multiplier \lambda:

L(\mathbf{q},\lambda) = \sum_i N_i \log q_i + \lambda\Big(1-\sum_i q_i\Big).

Setting \partial L/\partial q_i = 0 gives q_i = N_i/\lambda; the constraint pins \lambda = N, so

\hat q_{i,\text{MLE}} = \frac{N_i}{N}.

Each class probability’s MLE is its observed relative frequency (Bernoulli is the K=2 case).

TipRecap: Multinomial as an exponential family

What the symbols stand for:

  • K: the number of possible states (faces of the die, classes).
  • q_i: the probability of state i. The vector \mathbf{q} = (q_1, \dots, q_K) must sum to 1.
  • N: the number of draws. N_i: how many draws landed in state i, so \sum_i N_i = N.
  • \mathbb{1}[x^{(n)} = i]: the indicator, 1 if draw n is state i and 0 otherwise. Summing it over draws counts them.
  • \eta_i = \log(q_i / q_K): the natural parameter, the log-odds of state i against the last state K.
  • \lambda: the Lagrange multiplier that enforces \sum_i q_i = 1 during the maximization.

Taking the equations apart:

  • \sum_i N_i \log q_i: each draw in state i contributes \log q_i, and there are N_i of them.
  • Peeling out state K rewrites this as \sum_{i<K} N_i \log(q_i/q_K) + N\log q_K. The first sum is the data-meets-parameter part \eta^\top T. The second term depends on the parameters only, so it is -A.
  • The Lagrangian L = \sum_i N_i\log q_i + \lambda(1 - \sum_i q_i) adds a penalty term that is zero exactly when the constraint holds. Its derivative N_i/q_i - \lambda = 0 says every q_i is proportional to its count.

A teaching example. Roll a three-sided die (K = 3) ten times and see counts N_1 = 5, N_2 = 3, N_3 = 2. Each q_i = N_i/\lambda, and the constraint 5/\lambda + 3/\lambda + 2/\lambda = 1 forces \lambda = 10 = N. So \hat{\mathbf{q}} = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2). In natural coordinates, \eta_1 = \log(0.5/0.2) \approx 0.916 and \eta_2 = \log(0.3/0.2) \approx 0.405. The normalizer is A = 10\log(1 + e^{\eta_1} + e^{\eta_2}) = 10\log(1 + 2.5 + 1.5) = 10\log 5, which matches N\log(1/q_3) = 10\log 5.

Univariate Gaussian as an exponential family

Assume N i.i.d. samples x^{(i)} \in \mathbb{R} from the univariate Gaussian \mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu, \sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\exp\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}(x-\mu)^2\}.

Step 1. Expand the square so the parameter and the data interact only through an inner product \eta^\top T(x):

-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}(x-\mu)^2 = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}x^2 + \frac{\mu}{\sigma^2}x - \frac{\mu^2}{2\sigma^2}.

The x-independent factor \exp\{-\mu^2/2\sigma^2\} becomes part of the log-partition function. The remaining terms that multiply x and x^2 make T(x) two-dimensional:

\eta = \begin{bmatrix}\mu/\sigma^2 \\ 1/\sigma^2\end{bmatrix} \qquad T(x) = \begin{bmatrix}x \\ -x^2/2\end{bmatrix}.

So \mu = \eta_1/\eta_2 and \sigma^2 = 1/\eta_2: the ordinary (\mu,\sigma^2) parameterization is recoverable from the natural one, but they are not the same coordinates (the disambiguation table above).

Step 2. MLE in the familiar (\mu, \sigma^2) coordinates:

\ell(\theta;\mathcal{D}) = -\frac{N}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2}\sum_n\frac{(x^{(n)}-\mu)^2}{\sigma^2}.

Setting \partial \ell/\partial \mu = 0 gives \hat\mu_{\text{MLE}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_n x^{(n)}, the sample mean. Setting \partial \ell /\partial \sigma^2 = 0 gives \hat\sigma^2_{\text{MLE}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_n\big((x^{(n)})^2 - \hat\mu_{\text{MLE}}^2\big), the sample variance (with divisor N, not N-1).

TipRecap: Univariate Gaussian as an exponential family

What the symbols stand for:

  • \mu: the mean, the centre of the bell curve. \sigma^2: the variance, its spread.
  • \eta = (\mu/\sigma^2,\; 1/\sigma^2): the natural parameter, a 2-vector. The second entry, one over the variance, is called the precision.
  • T(x) = (x,\; -x^2/2): the sufficient statistic, also a 2-vector, because the exponent contains both x and x^2.
  • \hat\mu_{\text{MLE}}, \hat\sigma^2_{\text{MLE}}: the fitted mean and variance.

Taking the expanded square apart:

-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} = -\frac{x^2}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{\mu}{\sigma^2}x - \frac{\mu^2}{2\sigma^2}

  • The x term gives \eta_1 T_1(x) = (\mu/\sigma^2)\cdot x.
  • The x^2 term gives \eta_2 T_2(x) = (1/\sigma^2)\cdot(-x^2/2).
  • The term with no x goes into A, together with the \log(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma) from the front factor.

In the MLE, \hat\mu is the average of the data and \hat\sigma^2 is (average of x^2) minus (average of x) squared.

A teaching example. Observe \{2, 4, 6\}. Then \hat\mu = 12/3 = 4. The average of the squares is (4 + 16 + 36)/3 = 56/3, so \hat\sigma^2 = 56/3 - 16 = 8/3 \approx 2.667, the same as the average squared distance from the mean, (4 + 0 + 4)/3. The natural parameter is \eta = (4 \cdot 3/8,\; 3/8) = (1.5,\; 0.375), and going back, \eta_1/\eta_2 = 4 = \mu and 1/\eta_2 = 8/3 = \sigma^2.

Mean of sufficient statistics

Result. For X \sim p(x\mid\eta) in an exponential family, \mathbb{E}_\eta[T(X)] = A'(\eta). Differentiating again gives \mathrm{Var}(T(X)) = A''(\eta).

Why. Every valid density integrates to 1 for every \eta. Start from \mathbb{E}[T(X)] - A'(\eta), rewrite the lone A'(\eta) as an integral using \int p = 1, and recognize the integrand as \frac{d}{d\eta}p(x\mid\eta). Swap derivative and integral (under mild regularity conditions the lecture does not dwell on):

\mathbb{E}[T(X)] - A'(\eta) = \frac{d}{d\eta}\int p(x\mid\eta)\,dx = \frac{d}{d\eta}\, 1 = 0.

The proof used only two facts: a density integrates to 1, and differentiating \exp\{\cdot\} brings down the contents of the exponent. Nothing about the specific distribution mattered, which is why the formula applies to Bernoulli, Multinomial, Gaussian, or any other exponential family.

MLE consequence. For N i.i.d. exponential-family samples, setting \partial \ell/\partial\eta = 0 gives

A'(\hat\eta_{\text{MLE}}) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N T(x^{(i)}).

The MLE sets the model’s expected sufficient statistic equal to the empirical average of the sufficient statistic. Whenever this equation has a closed form, it reproduces the Bernoulli, Multinomial, and Gaussian answers above.

TipRecap: Mean of sufficient statistics

What the symbols stand for:

  • \mathbb{E}_\eta[T(X)]: the average value of the sufficient statistic if we drew many samples from the model with parameter \eta.
  • A'(\eta), A''(\eta): the first and second derivatives of the log-partition function with respect to \eta.
  • \mathrm{Var}(T(X)): the variance of the sufficient statistic under the model.
  • \frac{1}{N}\sum_i T(x^{(i)}): the average of the sufficient statistic over the observed data.

Taking the equations apart:

  • \mathbb{E}_\eta[T(X)] = A'(\eta): the slope of the normalizer is the model’s predicted average of T. We get a mean by differentiating, with no sum or integral over outcomes.
  • A'(\hat\eta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i T(x^{(i)}): the left side is what the model predicts, the right side is what the data show. The MLE is the \eta that makes them agree.

A teaching example. Four flips give \{1, 1, 1, 0\}, so the data average of T(x) = x is 3/4. For the Bernoulli, A'(\eta) = e^\eta/(1+e^\eta). Setting this equal to 3/4 gives e^\eta = 3, so \hat\eta = \log 3 \approx 1.099. Converting back, \hat\theta = 3/4, the same answer as the fraction of heads from Maximum likelihood estimation. The variance of a flip under the fitted model is A''(\hat\eta) = 0.75 \times 0.25 = 0.1875.

High-level overview

This section retells each part of the week in plain language and without formulas, using the lecture’s own examples. Read it before the chapter for orientation, or after it as a review.

What is machine learning? (What is machine learning?). Some tasks are too complicated to write rules for, so we let a program learn its behaviour from examples instead. Nobody can write down the rules for recognizing a face, but we can show a program many labelled faces. The course’s stance is that nearly every learning task can be phrased as “estimate a probability distribution from data”. A discriminative model learns only how to go from input to label (whose face is this?). A generative model learns how the data itself arises, so it can also produce new examples.

Joint, marginal, and conditional distributions (Joint, marginal, and conditional distributions). These are three ways of reading the same table of chances. In the diagnostic-test example, the joint distribution lists the chance of every combination: infected and positive, infected and negative, and so on. The marginal ignores one variable: how common is infection overall, whatever the test says? The conditional fixes one variable and asks about the other: among people who tested positive, what fraction are infected?

Worked example: a diagnostic test (Worked example: a diagnostic test). Bayes’ rule turns “how good is this test?” into “what does my result mean for me?”, and these are different questions. The answer depends on two things at once: how accurate the test is, and how common infection was before testing (the prior). A positive result raises the chance of infection, but healthy people who test positive by mistake keep it from reaching certainty.

Parametric models and the likelihood (Parametric models and the likelihood). Rather than considering every possible distribution, we pick a family described by a few adjustable numbers, the parameters. For coin flips, the single parameter is the chance of heads. The likelihood scores each candidate value by asking how probable the data we actually saw would be if that value were true. It is a score for parameter values, not the probability that a value is correct.

Maximum likelihood estimation (Maximum likelihood estimation). Pick the parameter value with the highest likelihood score. If a coin lands heads 7 times in 10 flips, the chance of heads that makes this outcome most probable is 0.7, which matches intuition. MLE is one sensible fitting rule, not the only one.

Sufficient statistics (Sufficient statistics). A sufficient statistic is a summary of the data that loses nothing useful for learning the parameter. For coin flips, the number of heads is enough: which flips came up heads, and in what order, tells us nothing more about the coin. This lets us compress a large data set into a few numbers without losing information.

Exponential families (Exponential families). Many common distributions (coin flips, dice, bell curves, and more) fit one shared template. The template splits a distribution into pieces: the summary of the data that matters, the setting that controls the shape, and a correction term whose only job is to make all the probabilities add up to 1. Once a distribution is in the template, its sufficient statistic and its MLE recipe come out automatically instead of being worked out one distribution at a time. The recipe subsection shows how to sort the terms of a formula into those pieces, using the coin flip.

Multinomial and univariate Gaussian (Multinomial as an exponential family, Univariate Gaussian as an exponential family). The same recipe, applied to two more distributions. For a die, the summary is how many times each face came up. For a bell curve, the summary is the sum of the measurements and the sum of their squares, which is exactly what we need to recover the average and the spread.

Mean of sufficient statistics (Mean of sufficient statistics). The correction term in the template is useful in its own right: how fast it changes gives the average value of the summary. Combined with MLE, this gives a clean rule: the fitted model is the one whose predicted average summary equals the summary we actually observed. For coin flips, the model’s predicted fraction of heads is set equal to the observed fraction.
