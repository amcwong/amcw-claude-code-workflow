Problem 1: Two steps on a three-state chain

A stationary Markov chain on states \{1,2,3\} has transition matrix

A = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0.3 & 0.2\\ 0.2 & 0.6 & 0.2\\ 0.1 & 0.3 & 0.6\end{pmatrix}, \qquad A_{ij}=p(x_t=j\mid x_{t-1}=i).

  1. Check that A is a valid transition matrix. Do its columns need to sum to 1?
  2. Starting in state 1, what is the probability of being in state 3 two steps later? Compute it as a sum over the intermediate state.
  3. Compute the full two-step matrix A(2) and check one of its rows.
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 5, Transition matrices and state transition diagrams and n-step transitions and the Chapman-Kolmogorov equations.

Part 1. Entry A_{ij} is a conditional probability: given that the chain is in state i now, the probability that it is in state j next. For a fixed current state i, the next state must be exactly one of 1,2,3, so by the law of total probability \sum_{j=1}^3 A_{ij} = \sum_{j=1}^3 p(x_t=j\mid x_{t-1}=i) = 1. That is the row condition. Together with nonnegativity, it is all a transition matrix needs. Here every entry is nonnegative and the rows sum to 0.5+0.3+0.2=1, 0.2+0.6+0.2=1, 0.1+0.3+0.6=1, so A is valid.

A column collects the probabilities of arriving in one state j from each possible current state: A_{1j}=p(x_t=j\mid x_{t-1}=1), A_{2j}=p(x_t=j\mid x_{t-1}=2), and so on. Each term conditions on a different event, so they are not the probabilities of mutually exclusive outcomes of one experiment, and nothing forces them to add to 1. Here the columns sum to 0.8, 1.2, 1.0.

The column sums still carry information. If the chain were equally likely to be in each state, the probability of landing in j one step later would be \tfrac13 times column j’s sum, here (0.267,\ 0.400,\ 0.333). Column 2 has the largest sum, so state 2 is the most popular destination, which Problem 2 confirms for the long run.

Part 2. To go from state 1 to state 3 in two steps, the chain must be in some state k after the first step. Splitting on that intermediate state (the law of total probability again), p(x_{t+2}=3\mid x_t=1) = \sum_{k} p(x_{t+1}=k\mid x_t=1)\;p(x_{t+2}=3\mid x_{t+1}=k,\ x_t=1). By the Markov property, once we know x_{t+1}=k the earlier state x_t=1 adds nothing, so the second factor is A_{k3}. The first factor is A_{1k}. So A_{13}(2) = \sum_k A_{1k}A_{k3} = 0.5(0.2)+0.3(0.2)+0.2(0.6) = 0.10+0.06+0.12 = 0.28. The three terms are “stay at 1, then move to 3”, “move to 2, then to 3”, and “move to 3, then stay”. Each path’s probability is the product of its two steps, and the paths are mutually exclusive, so their probabilities add.

Part 3. By Chapman-Kolmogorov, A(2)=A^2. Entrywise, the two-step matrix is A(2)_{ij} = \sum_k A_{ik}A_{kj}, that is, the two-step probability from i to j sums over every intermediate state k. Each entry of A^2 is therefore row i of A dotted with column j of A. For example, A(2)_{13}=\sum_k A_{1k}A_{k3}, which is the calculation in part 2. Filling in all nine entries gives A^2 =\begin{pmatrix} 0.33 & 0.39 & 0.28\\ 0.24 & 0.48 & 0.28\\ 0.17 & 0.39 & 0.44\end{pmatrix}. Row 1 sums to 0.33+0.39+0.28=1. That is not a coincidence: every row of A^2 sums to 1, because \sum_j A(2)_{ij} = \sum_j\sum_k A_{ik}A_{kj} = \sum_k A_{ik}\Big(\sum_j A_{kj}\Big) = \sum_k A_{ik} = 1, using the row condition of Part 1 twice. So a row sum that is not 1 points to an arithmetic slip.

In general, the (i,j) entry of A^n is the probability of going from state i to state j in exactly n steps: A(n)_{ij}=P(x_{t+n}=j\mid x_t=i). Row i of A^n is the distribution of the state after n steps when the chain starts in state i, so it sums to 1.

Answer. (1) Valid; only rows must sum to 1. (2) 0.28. (3) A^2 as above.

Problem 2: The stationary distribution of the same chain

For the chain of Problem 1:

  1. Find the stationary distribution \pi by solving A^\top\pi=\pi with \pi_1+\pi_2+\pi_3=1.
  2. Check your answer by applying one step of the chain.
  3. Which state does the chain spend most time in, in the long run? Explain from the matrix why.
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 5, The stationary distribution.

Write \pi_t(j)=p(x_t=j) for the probability of being in state j at step t. Summing over where the chain was one step earlier, \pi_t(j) = \sum_i \pi_{t-1}(i)\,A_{ij}. The right side is the total probability flowing into j. From each state i, including j itself (the “stay” term A_{jj}), it takes the mass \pi_{t-1}(i) sitting there times the chance A_{ij} of moving to j. The sum runs down column j of A, so it is the dot product of the vector \pi_{t-1} with column j. Stacking all j gives \pi_t = A^\top\pi_{t-1}: the transpose turns the columns of A into rows, so that ordinary matrix-vector multiplication takes these column dot products.

A stationary distribution is a \pi that this update leaves unchanged, A^\top\pi=\pi. It is a fixed point of the update, not the starting distribution and not \pi_t at any particular time.

Part 1. The condition \pi_j=\sum_i\pi_iA_{ij} gives one equation per column. Column 1 and column 3: \pi_1 = 0.5\pi_1+0.2\pi_2+0.1\pi_3 \ \Rightarrow\ 5\pi_1 = 2\pi_2+\pi_3, \pi_3 = 0.2\pi_1+0.2\pi_2+0.6\pi_3 \ \Rightarrow\ 2\pi_3 = \pi_1+\pi_2. Why only two of the three column equations? Add all three. The left sides give \sum_j\pi_j, and the right sides give \sum_j\sum_i\pi_iA_{ij}=\sum_i\pi_i\sum_jA_{ij}=\sum_i\pi_i, because rows sum to 1. That sum holds for every vector, so the three equations carry only two independent constraints, and the column-2 equation follows from the other two. The normalization \pi_1+\pi_2+\pi_3=1 supplies the missing equation and fixes the scale.

Substituting \pi_3=5\pi_1-2\pi_2 into the second equation gives 10\pi_1-4\pi_2=\pi_1+\pi_2, so \pi_2=\tfrac95\pi_1 and \pi_3=\tfrac75\pi_1. Normalizing, \pi_1(1+\tfrac95+\tfrac75)=1 gives \pi_1=\tfrac5{21}, so \pi = \big(\tfrac{5}{21},\ \tfrac{9}{21},\ \tfrac{7}{21}\big) \approx (0.238,\ 0.429,\ 0.333).

Part 2. “Applying one step of the chain” means: suppose the chain’s current state is distributed according to our \pi, take one step, and compute the distribution of the next state, A^\top\pi. If \pi is stationary we must get \pi back. Entry j of A^\top\pi is \pi dotted with column j of A: (A^\top\pi)_1 = \frac{0.5(5)+0.2(9)+0.1(7)}{21} = \frac{2.5+1.8+0.7}{21} = \frac{5}{21}, (A^\top\pi)_2 = \frac{0.3(5)+0.6(9)+0.3(7)}{21} = \frac{1.5+5.4+2.1}{21} = \frac{9}{21}, (A^\top\pi)_3 = \frac{0.2(5)+0.2(9)+0.6(7)}{21} = \frac{1.0+1.8+4.2}{21} = \frac{7}{21}. Each dot product returns the matching entry of \pi, so one step leaves \pi unchanged. The dot products do not produce new quantities to interpret; their whole job is to reproduce \pi. The check also tests the column-2 equation, which Part 1 never used, so an arithmetic slip in Part 1 would show up here.

Part 3. Two things need explaining: what “time spent in the long run” means, and why state 2 gets the most of it.

\pi_j is the long-run probability of being in state j. If we run the chain for many steps, the distribution of the current state approaches \pi, and the fraction of steps spent in j approaches \pi_j. That reading needs the chain to converge to \pi wherever it starts, and here it does. Every entry of A is positive, so the chain is regular, and by the theorem of When is the stationary distribution unique? its stationary distribution is unique and \pi_t\to\pi from any start (Problem 3 works through these terms). Started in state 1, the distribution after t steps is row 1 of A^t: (0.5,\ 0.3,\ 0.2) after one step, (0.33,\ 0.39,\ 0.28) after two, (0.271,\ 0.417,\ 0.312) after three, and so on toward (0.238,\ 0.429,\ 0.333). The gap in the first entry shrinks by a factor of roughly three each step.

To see why state 2 wins, move the stay term of the stationary equation to the left side: \pi_j-\pi_jA_{jj}=\sum_{i\neq j}\pi_iA_{ij}, that is, \pi_j\,(1-A_{jj}) = \sum_{i\neq j}\pi_i A_{ij}. The left side is the mass leaving j in one step and the right side is the mass arriving from the other states, so in stationarity the two balance at every state. For states 2 and 1 this reads 0.4\,\pi_2 = 0.3\,\pi_1 + 0.3\,\pi_3, \qquad 0.5\,\pi_1 = 0.2\,\pi_2 + 0.1\,\pi_3. (Check, in units of \tfrac1{21}: 0.4(9)=3.6=0.3(5)+0.3(7) and 0.5(5)=2.5=0.2(9)+0.1(7).) State 2 loses only 40\% of its mass per step and receives 30\% of the mass of each other state, so \pi_2 must be large for its small outflow to match that inflow. State 1 loses half its mass per step and receives only 20\% and 10\% from the others, so a small \pi_1 already balances. States that are easy to enter and hard to leave collect long-run probability, as with the two-state chain in the week chapter.

Answer. \pi=(5,9,7)/21\approx(0.238,0.429,0.333); state 2 is visited most.

Problem 3: Unique? Convergent?

Three chains on states \{1,2,3\}:

C_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0&1&0\\ 0&0&1\\ 1&0&0\end{pmatrix}, C_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1&0&0\\ 0.3&0.4&0.3\\ 0&0&1\end{pmatrix}, C_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 0&1&0\\ 0&0.5&0.5\\ 0.5&0&0.5\end{pmatrix}.

For each chain: is it irreducible? Is it regular? Is its stationary distribution unique? Started in a single state, does the distribution of x_t converge?

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 5, When is the stationary distribution unique? and The stationary distribution.

Each C_k is the transition matrix of a separate chain on the same three states, with (C_k)_{ij}=p(x_t=j\mid x_{t-1}=i) as in Problem 1. The letter C only keeps them apart from Problem 1’s A. Before taking the chains one at a time, here is how each of the four questions is tested.

  • Irreducible. From any state, you can eventually get to any other state. Draw the state transition diagram, with an arrow i\to j whenever entry (i,j) is positive. The chain is irreducible if, from every state, following arrows reaches every other state.
  • Regular. There is one number of steps n such that, after n steps, you have a positive chance of being in any state, whichever state you started in. Compute the powers C, C^2, C^3,\dots and look for one with every entry strictly positive. Entry (i,j) of C^n is the probability of being in j exactly n steps after starting in i (Problem 1), so an all-positive C^n means every finish is possible from every start after exactly n steps. Once some power is all-positive, every higher power is too: each entry of C^{n+1}=C\,C^n is a weighted average of entries of C^n, with weights from a row of C that are nonnegative and sum to 1, and an average of positive numbers is positive. A known bound also says when to stop looking: on K states, if any power is all-positive then C^{(K-1)^2+1} already is, so for three states checking up to C^5 settles the question.
  • Unique stationary distribution. There is exactly one pattern of where the chain spends its time that survives a step unchanged. Solve C^\top\pi=\pi together with \pi_1+\pi_2+\pi_3=1. Exactly one solution means unique; a free parameter left over means infinitely many.
  • Convergence. If you run the chain for a long time, the chance of being in each state settles to fixed values, and those values do not depend on where you started. Start in one state, for example \pi_0=(1,0,0), and iterate \pi_t=C^\top\pi_{t-1}. Starting in state i, the distribution after t steps is row i of C^t. Check whether it settles to a limit, and whether that limit is the same for every start.

The last two questions sound alike but ask different things. A stationary distribution is a fixed point: a \pi that one step leaves unchanged. Convergence is about a limit: whether the sequence \pi_0,\pi_1,\pi_2,\dots produced by actually running the chain settles down. They are linked in one direction only. If \pi_t\to q, taking the limit of both sides of \pi_{t+1}=C^\top\pi_t gives q=C^\top q, so any limit is stationary. The reverse fails: a stationary distribution can exist, even a unique one, while the chain never reaches it. The week chapter’s theorem says a regular chain has both properties. The three chains show what happens when regularity is missing, and then why it is enough.

C_1, the deterministic 3-cycle. Each row has a single 1: from 1 the chain always goes to 2, from 2 to 3, and from 3 back to 1.

Irreducible: yes. Following the arrows 1\to2\to3\to1 from any state visits every state.

Regular: no. Two steps follow two arrows (1\to3, 2\to1, 3\to2), and three steps bring every state back to itself: C_1^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0&0&1\\ 1&0&0\\ 0&1&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad C_1^3 = I. So C_1^4=C_1, C_1^5=C_1^2, and the powers cycle through C_1, C_1^2, I forever. Each has six zeros, so no power is all-positive. The underlying reason is a fixed rhythm: the chain can be back at its starting state only after 3, 6, 9,\dots steps, and at those times it cannot be anywhere else.

Unique stationary distribution: yes. State j receives mass only from the state just before it on the cycle, so C_1^\top\pi = (\pi_3,\ \pi_1,\ \pi_2). Setting this equal to (\pi_1,\pi_2,\pi_3) gives \pi_1=\pi_3, \pi_2=\pi_1, \pi_3=\pi_2. All three entries are equal, and normalizing forces (\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13). No free parameter is left, so it is unique. Regularity was not needed for this. (On finitely many states, irreducibility alone already gives a unique stationary distribution. What regularity adds is convergence.)

Convergence: no. Each step moves all the mass one place around the cycle, so multiplying by C_1^\top just rotates the entries. Started in state 1, (1,0,0)\to(0,1,0)\to(0,0,1)\to(1,0,0)\to\cdots, which repeats every three steps and has no limit. From a general start (a,b,c) the sequence cycles through (a,b,c), (c,a,b), (b,c,a), which converges only if a=b=c, that is, only if the chain already started at \pi. This is the three-state version of the week’s swapping chain.

The uniform distribution still describes the long-run fraction of time: the run 1,2,3,1,2,3,\dots spends exactly a third of its steps in each state. What fails is the distribution at a single large time t. Knowing the start and t, we know x_t exactly, so x_t is not a random draw from \pi.

C_2, two absorbing states. A state is absorbing if the chain never leaves it once it arrives, that is, its diagonal entry is 1. Rows 1 and 3 of C_2 are (1,0,0) and (0,0,1), so states 1 and 3 are absorbing. From state 2 the chain stays with probability 0.4 and moves to each end with probability 0.3.

Irreducible: no. No arrow leaves state 1, so states 2 and 3 cannot be reached from it.

Regular: no. Row 1 of C_2^n is the distribution n steps after starting in state 1. The chain never leaves state 1, so that row is (1,0,0) for every n, and every power has zeros. More generally, regular implies irreducible (an all-positive C^n reaches every state in n steps), so a chain that is not irreducible cannot be regular.

Unique stationary distribution: no. Write out C_2^\top\pi=\pi one entry at a time: \pi_1 = \pi_1 + 0.3\,\pi_2, \qquad \pi_2 = 0.4\,\pi_2, \qquad \pi_3 = 0.3\,\pi_2 + \pi_3. All three equations say the same thing, \pi_2=0, and nothing constrains how the remaining mass is split between states 1 and 3. Every (a,\ 0,\ 1-a) with 0\le a\le1 is stationary. In words: mass on an absorbing state stays put, so any split between the two ends sustains itself, while state 2 must be empty because it leaks 60\% of its mass every step and receives nothing back.

Convergence: no, because the limit depends on the start. Convergence means that every starting distribution settles to the same limit. Started in state 2, the chain is still there after t steps only if it stayed every time, which has probability 0.4^t. The rest has left, and because the two exits are equally likely (0.3 each), it is split evenly between the ends: \pi_t = \Big(\tfrac{1-0.4^t}{2},\ \ 0.4^t,\ \ \tfrac{1-0.4^t}{2}\Big). That is (0.3,\ 0.4,\ 0.3) after one step, (0.42,\ 0.16,\ 0.42) after two, (0.468,\ 0.064,\ 0.468) after three, tending to (0.5,\ 0,\ 0.5). Started in state 1, the distribution is (1,0,0) forever; started in state 3, it is (0,0,1). From a general start (a,b,c) the limit is (a+\tfrac b2,\ 0,\ c+\tfrac b2). Each start settles to a stationary distribution (as the fixed-point argument above requires), but the starts do not agree. Three starts give three different limits, so the chain has no single limit that every start reaches, and the test for convergence fails.

Why MCMC cannot allow this. An MCMC sampler is built for one target p, started at an arbitrary point, run, and summarized by averages over the chain. The answer is supposed to depend on p alone. Suppose the target is p=(0.5,\ 0,\ 0.5) and someone used C_2 as the sampler. The target passes every check from the week chapter: it is stationary for C_2 (take a=0.5), and C_2 even satisfies detailed balance with respect to it, because every flow p_i(C_2)_{ij} between two different states is 0 in both directions. Yet:

  • Started in state 1, the chain never moves, and its sample average reports p(x=1)=1. Started in state 3, it reports p(x=3)=1. Both are wrong, and no amount of burn-in helps, because the chain never forgets its start.
  • Started in state 2, the distribution \pi_t does approach the target, but any single run is absorbed at state 1 or state 3 after a few steps and then stays there. Its average reports either p(x=1)\approx1 or p(x=3)\approx1. The correct (0.5,\ 0,\ 0.5) appears only as an average over many separate runs, never within one chain.

This is why the week chapter warns that detailed balance gives stationarity but not convergence. MCMC needs a chain that forgets where it started, and C_2 never does. Running several chains from spread-out starts, as MCMC diagnostics recommends, would expose it: chains absorbed at different ends disagree completely, which is exactly what \hat R is built to flag.

C_3, a cycle with waiting. From 1 the chain always moves to 2. From 2 it stays or moves to 3, each with probability 0.5. From 3 it moves to 1 or stays, each with probability 0.5.

Irreducible: yes. The arrows 1\to2\to3\to1 form a cycle through every state.

Regular: yes, from n=3 on. C_3 itself has zeros, and so does C_3^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0&0.5&0.5\\ 0.25&0.25&0.5\\ 0.25&0.5&0.25\end{pmatrix}. The zero in position (1,1) says the chain cannot be back at state 1 two steps after starting there: the first step must go to 2, and from 2 the second step goes to 2 or 3. One more step fills it in: C_3^3 = \begin{pmatrix} 0.25&0.25&0.5\\ 0.25&0.375&0.375\\ 0.125&0.5&0.375\end{pmatrix}. Every entry is positive, so the chain is regular, and every higher power is all-positive too. Compared with C_1, what breaks the rhythm is the option to wait at states 2 and 3. The chain can return to state 1 in 3 steps (1\to2\to3\to1), in 4 (1\to2\to2\to3\to1), in 5, and so on, so nothing locks it into a fixed phase.

Unique stationary distribution: yes. Write out C_3^\top\pi=\pi: \pi_1 = 0.5\,\pi_3, \qquad \pi_2 = \pi_1 + 0.5\,\pi_2, \qquad \pi_3 = 0.5\,\pi_2 + 0.5\,\pi_3. The second gives \pi_2=2\pi_1 and the third gives \pi_3=\pi_2; the first then holds automatically (0.5\cdot2\pi_1=\pi_1). So \pi\propto(1,2,2), and normalizing, \pi = (0.2,\ 0.4,\ 0.4). One step of the chain confirms it: C_3^\top\pi = (0.5\cdot0.4,\ \ 0.2+0.5\cdot0.4,\ \ 0.5\cdot0.4+0.5\cdot0.4) = (0.2,\ 0.4,\ 0.4).

Convergence: yes, from every start. Started in state 1, the distribution after t steps, and its distance \sum_j|\pi_t(j)-\pi_j| from \pi:

t \pi_t distance from \pi
0 (1,\ 0,\ 0) 1.6
1 (0,\ 1,\ 0) 1.2
2 (0,\ 0.5,\ 0.5) 0.4
3 (0.25,\ 0.25,\ 0.5) 0.3
4 (0.25,\ 0.375,\ 0.375) 0.1
5 (0.1875,\ 0.4375,\ 0.375) 0.075
6 (0.1875,\ 0.40625,\ 0.40625) 0.025
12 (0.2002,\ 0.3999,\ 0.3999) 0.0004

The distance falls by a factor of about 4 every two steps.

Why an all-positive power forces convergence. The week chapter states the theorem without proof. For this chain the argument is short, and the same argument works for any regular chain. Take P=C_3^3, the three-step matrix, and look at the smallest entry in each column: m_1 = 0.125, \qquad m_2 = 0.25, \qquad m_3 = 0.375, \qquad m_1+m_2+m_3 = 0.75. Every row of P has at least m_j in column j. So whatever the starting state, three steps later at least 0.75 of the probability is laid out the same way, as (0.125,\ 0.25,\ 0.375), regardless of the start. Only the remaining 0.25 can still depend on where the chain began.

To make that precise, write P_{ij}=m_j+R_{ij} with R_{ij}\ge0; each row of R sums to 1-0.75=0.25. Take any two starting distributions \mu and \nu and let d_i=\mu_i-\nu_i. Both distributions sum to 1, so \sum_id_i=0. Three steps later the difference in state j is d'_j = \sum_i d_i P_{ij} = m_j\sum_i d_i + \sum_i d_i R_{ij} = \sum_i d_i R_{ij}, because the common part m_j multiplies \sum_id_i=0 and drops out. Taking absolute values, \sum_j |d'_j| \le \sum_i |d_i|\sum_j R_{ij} = 0.25\sum_i|d_i|. Every three steps, the distance between any two distributions shrinks by at least a factor of 4. (A single step never increases it: run the same argument with every m_j=0.) Two conclusions follow.

  • Uniqueness. If \mu and \nu are both stationary, three steps leave both unchanged, so their distance equals at most a quarter of itself. It must be 0, so \mu=\nu.
  • Convergence. Take \mu=\pi_0, any start, and \nu=\pi. Two distributions are never more than 2 apart in this sum, so after 3k steps the distance is at most 2\times0.25^k, which goes to 0.

The bound is loose (the table shrinks faster), but it proves convergence from every start. For any regular chain, some power C^n has every entry positive, so every column minimum is positive and their sum \beta is above 0. The same argument then shrinks distances by a factor 1-\beta<1 every n steps, which proves the week chapter’s theorem. For C_1 and C_2, every power has a zero in every column, so \beta=0 and the argument gives nothing, which matches what we found.

To summarize:

irreducible regular unique \pi converges from every start
C_1 yes no yes, (\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13) no, repeats every 3 steps
C_2 no no no, any (a,0,1-a) no, the limit depends on the start
C_3 yes yes yes, (0.2,0.4,0.4) yes, to \pi

Regularity can take a few steps to show up: a zero in C or C^2 does not rule it out.

Answer. C_1: irreducible, not regular, unique, does not converge. C_2: not irreducible, not regular, not unique, does not converge (each start settles to a different limit). C_3: irreducible, regular, unique \pi=(0.2,0.4,0.4), converges.

Problem 4: A bigram model from a tiny corpus

A first-order language model is fitted by maximum likelihood to four sentences:

“I like turtles”, “you like turtles”, “I like snails”, “you don’t like snails”.

  1. Compute \hat\pi (first-word probabilities) and every nonzero \hat A_{jk}.
  2. Score the sentence “you don’t like turtles”.
  3. Score “I don’t like turtles”. What goes wrong, and why?
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 5, Application: Markov language models.

The model treats a sentence as a Markov chain over words. By the chain rule of probability, p(x_1,\dots,x_T)=p(x_1)\,p(x_2\mid x_1)\,p(x_3\mid x_1,x_2)\cdots, and the first-order Markov assumption cuts each factor down to the previous word only, p(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1})=p(x_t\mid x_{t-1}). So a sentence’s probability is one start probability times one transition per later word: p(x_{1:T}) = \pi(x_1)\,A(x_1,x_2)\cdots A(x_{T-1},x_T). Here \pi is the first-word distribution, not a stationary distribution.

Fitting by maximum likelihood, the log-likelihood of the corpus splits into one piece for \pi and one piece for each row A_{j\cdot}, because the parameters of row j appear only in transitions that leave word j. Each piece has the Multinomial form \sum_kN_k\log q_k with \sum_kq_k=1, whose maximizer is “count divided by total” (Week 1). So every estimate is a ratio of counts.

Part 1. First words: “I” starts two sentences and “you” starts two, so N^1_{\text{I}}=N^1_{\text{you}}=2 and \hat\pi_{\text{I}}=\hat\pi_{\text{you}}=\tfrac24=\tfrac12.

Bigrams (consecutive word pairs), counted across all four sentences:

previous word next word: count row total
I like: 2 2
you like: 1, don’t: 1 2
don’t like: 1 1
like turtles: 2, snails: 2 4

Dividing each count by its row total:

  • \hat A_{\text{I},\text{like}}=\tfrac22=1;
  • \hat A_{\text{you},\text{like}}=\hat A_{\text{you},\text{don't}}=\tfrac12;
  • \hat A_{\text{don't},\text{like}}=\tfrac11=1;
  • \hat A_{\text{like},\text{turtles}}=\hat A_{\text{like},\text{snails}}=\tfrac24=\tfrac12.

Every other transition has count 0 and estimate 0. “turtles” and “snails” only ever end a sentence, so their rows have no counts at all; they are not needed to score the sentences below.

Part 2. One first-word factor and one transition per later word: p(\text{you don't like turtles}) = \hat\pi_{\text{you}}\,\hat A_{\text{you},\text{don't}} \times\,\hat A_{\text{don't},\text{like}}\,\hat A_{\text{like},\text{turtles}} = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12\cdot1\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac18. The corpus never contains this sentence, yet it gets positive probability. The model has seen each of its three bigrams (“you don’t”, “don’t like”, “like turtles”) in other sentences, and the Markov assumption lets it combine them. This is how a bigram model generalizes to new sentences.

Part 3. Scoring “I don’t like turtles” needs \hat\pi_{\text{I}}\cdot\hat A_{\text{I},\text{don't}}\cdot\hat A_{\text{don't},\text{like}}\cdot\hat A_{\text{like},\text{turtles}}. The bigram “I don’t” never occurs in the corpus, so \hat A_{\text{I},\text{don't}}=0, and one zero factor makes the whole product 0. A perfectly reasonable sentence is declared impossible.

Why does maximum likelihood do this? Row “I” has count 2 for “like” and 0 for every other word, so its log-likelihood is 2\log q_{\text{like}} plus 0\cdot\log q_k for every other k. Probability given to an unseen word k adds nothing to the log-likelihood, because its count is 0, but it has to come out of q_{\text{like}}, which lowers 2\log q_{\text{like}}. So the maximum puts all of the row’s probability on the words actually observed, and every unseen transition gets exactly 0. In a small corpus most reasonable bigrams are unseen, so this is a serious weakness of the plain MLE.

Answer. (1) \hat\pi=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12) for I, you; transitions as listed. (2) \tfrac18. (3) 0, because the unseen bigram “I don’t” has MLE probability 0.

Problem 5: Stationary without detailed balance, and a chain built to satisfy it

  1. Show that the uniform distribution is stationary for the 3-cycle C_1 of Problem 3, but that C_1 does not satisfy detailed balance with respect to it.
  2. Now take the target \pi=(0.5,\ 0.3,\ 0.2) on \{1,2,3\}. Build a Metropolis chain: from state i, propose each of the two other states with probability \tfrac12, and accept a proposed j with probability \min\{1,\pi_j/\pi_i\}. Write out the full transition matrix, including the probabilities of staying put.
  3. Verify detailed balance for every pair, and verify A^\top\pi=\pi directly.
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 5, Detailed balance and Why Metropolis-Hastings works.

Both parts compare two kinds of balance. Call \pi_iA_{ij} the flow from i to j: the probability of being in i and then stepping to j.

  • Stationarity, A^\top\pi=\pi, is a balance at each state: the total flow into j, \sum_i\pi_iA_{ij}, equals \pi_j. Equivalently (move the stay term \pi_jA_{jj} to the other side), the flow out of j to other states equals the flow into j from other states.
  • Detailed balance, \pi_iA_{ij}=\pi_jA_{ji}, is a balance along each pair of states: the flow along an edge equals the flow back along it.

If every pair balances, every state balances, because the inflow into j is a sum of pair flows, each equal to its reverse; the week chapter’s proof does exactly this. The reverse need not hold.

Part 1. For C_1 with \pi=(\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13), each state receives \tfrac13\cdot1 from the state before it on the cycle and sends \tfrac13\cdot1 to the state after it. Inflow equals outflow at every state, so \pi is stationary (Problem 3 found the same by solving the equations).

Detailed balance asks the same of each edge separately. Take i=1, j=2: \pi_1A_{12}=\tfrac13\cdot1=\tfrac13, \qquad \pi_2A_{21}=\tfrac13\cdot0=0. These differ, so detailed balance fails. Probability circulates: a flow of \tfrac13 per step goes around the cycle in one direction, and nothing comes back along the same edges. Every state still balances, because what arrives from one neighbour leaves toward the other. This is the week’s point that detailed balance is sufficient for stationarity, not necessary.

The name “time reversible” describes the same failure. Under detailed balance, a chain in its stationary distribution looks the same run backwards in time as forwards. C_1 run forwards goes 1\to2\to3; run backwards it goes 1\to3\to2, which the forward chain never does.

Part 2. The chain moves from i to a different state j only if it proposes j (probability \tfrac12) and then accepts (probability \min\{1,\pi_j/\pi_i\}): A_{ij} = \tfrac12\min\{1,\ \pi_j/\pi_i\}, \qquad j\neq i. The proposal never suggests staying, so the chain stays at i only after a rejection, and the stay-put probability is what remains of the row, A_{ii}=1-\sum_{j\neq i}A_{ij}. The proposal is symmetric (\tfrac12 each way), so the Hastings correction is 1 and this is the Metropolis rule.

  • From 1 (\pi_1=0.5): to 2, \tfrac12\min\{1,\ 0.6\}=0.3; to 3, \tfrac12\min\{1,\ 0.4\}=0.2; stay 1-0.3-0.2=0.5. Both moves go downhill, so both can be rejected, and the stay probability is the sum of the two rejection chances, \tfrac12(0.4)+\tfrac12(0.6)=0.5.
  • From 2 (\pi_2=0.3): to 1, \tfrac12\min\{1,\ \tfrac53\}=0.5 (uphill, always accepted); to 3, \tfrac12\min\{1,\ \tfrac23\}=\tfrac13; stay 1-0.5-\tfrac13=\tfrac16.
  • From 3 (\pi_3=0.2): to 1, \tfrac12\cdot1=0.5; to 2, \tfrac12\cdot1=0.5; stay 0. Both alternatives are more probable, so nothing is ever rejected.

So A = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0.3 & 0.2\\ 0.5 & \tfrac16 & \tfrac13\\ 0.5 & 0.5 & 0\end{pmatrix}. The diagonal entries come entirely from rejected proposals, which is why a rejection must be recorded as a repeat of the current state.

Part 3. Detailed balance, pair by pair: \pi_1A_{12} = 0.5(0.3) = 0.15 = 0.3(0.5) = \pi_2A_{21}, \pi_1A_{13} = 0.5(0.2) = 0.1 = 0.2(0.5) = \pi_3A_{31}, \pi_2A_{23} = 0.3(\tfrac13) = 0.1 = 0.2(0.5) = \pi_3A_{32}. Each flow equals \tfrac12\min\{\pi_i,\pi_j\}, and the reason is general: \pi_iA_{ij} = \pi_i\cdot\tfrac12\min\Big\{1,\ \frac{\pi_j}{\pi_i}\Big\} = \tfrac12\min\{\pi_i,\ \pi_j\}. The right side does not change when i and j swap, so the flow back is the same number. This is the week chapter’s proof with q=\tfrac12.

Directly, column by column (\pi dotted with each column of A): 0.5(0.5)+0.3(0.5)+0.2(0.5) = 0.5, 0.5(0.3)+0.3(\tfrac16)+0.2(0.5) = 0.3, 0.5(0.2)+0.3(\tfrac13)+0.2(0) = 0.2. Each returns the matching entry of \pi, so A^\top\pi=\pi.

Detailed balance makes \pi stationary, but as Problem 3 showed, a sampler also has to converge to it. This chain does. A has a zero only in position (3,3), and A^2 is all-positive (its (3,3) entry is 0.5(0.2)+0.5(\tfrac13)+0=\tfrac4{15}), so the chain is regular. Its stationary distribution is therefore unique and reached from any start, so this Metropolis chain really does sample (0.5,\ 0.3,\ 0.2).

Answer. (1) \tfrac13\cdot1\neq\tfrac13\cdot0, so no detailed balance despite stationarity. (2) A as above. (3) All three pair flows match, and A^\top\pi=\pi.

Problem 6: When the Hastings correction matters

The target on (0,1) is \tilde p(\theta)=\theta^3(1-\theta)^5. The proposal ignores the current state and draws \theta' from the density q(\theta')=2\theta' on (0,1), which favours large values.

  1. The chain is at \theta=0.3 and proposes \theta'=0.5. Compute the Metropolis-Hastings acceptance probability, and the value you would get by wrongly using the Metropolis rule (no q ratio).
  2. Repeat for the reverse move, from 0.5 to 0.3.
  3. Explain the direction of the correction.
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 5, The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm and Why Metropolis-Hastings works.

The MH acceptance probability for a move from x to x' multiplies two ratios: \alpha(x'\mid x) = \min\Big\{1,\ \frac{\tilde p(x')}{\tilde p(x)}\cdot\frac{q(x\mid x')}{q(x'\mid x)}\Big\}. The first ratio favours moves toward higher target density. The second compares how readily the proposal would suggest the reverse move with how readily it suggested this one. Here the proposal ignores the current state: q(x'\mid x)=2x' whatever x is. So q(\theta\mid\theta')=2\theta and q(\theta'\mid\theta)=2\theta', and the correction factor is \theta/\theta'.

The target ratio for the move 0.3\to0.5 is \frac{\tilde p(0.5)}{\tilde p(0.3)} = \Big(\frac{0.5}{0.3}\Big)^3\Big(\frac{0.5}{0.7}\Big)^5 = 4.630\times0.1859 \approx 0.861. In logs, 3\ln\tfrac53+5\ln\tfrac57=1.5325-1.6824=-0.1499, and e^{-0.1499}\approx0.861. Raising \theta from 0.3 to 0.5 increases the \theta^3 factor but shrinks the (1-\theta)^5 factor by more, so the candidate is slightly less probable than the current point.

Part 1. The proposal density is q(0.5)=1.0 at the candidate and q(0.3)=0.6 at the current point. MH multiplies by q(0.3\mid0.5)/q(0.5\mid0.3)=0.6/1.0: \alpha = \min\{1,\ 0.861\times0.6\} \approx 0.516. The Metropolis rule ignores the proposal and would give \min\{1,0.861\}=0.861.

Part 2. Reverse: the target ratio is 1/0.861\approx1.162 and the q ratio is 1.0/0.6, so \alpha=\min\{1,\ 1.936\}=1. The Metropolis rule also gives 1. The move goes uphill in the target and toward the value the proposal under-suggests, so both rules accept it.

Part 3. Compare the flows between 0.3 and 0.5 under each rule. The flow from \theta to \theta' is (target density at \theta) \times (proposal density of \theta') \times (acceptance probability). Write \tilde p(0.5)=0.861\,\tilde p(0.3).

  • With the MH rule: from 0.3, \tilde p(0.3)\times1.0\times0.516=0.516\,\tilde p(0.3); from 0.5, 0.861\,\tilde p(0.3)\times0.6\times1=0.517\,\tilde p(0.3). Equal up to rounding: detailed balance holds.
  • With the Metropolis rule: from 0.3, \tilde p(0.3)\times1.0\times0.861=0.861\,\tilde p(0.3); from 0.5, still 0.517\,\tilde p(0.3). The upward flow is too large by the factor q(0.5)/q(0.3)=1/0.6.

The proposal suggests 0.5 more often than 0.3, so accepting on the target ratio alone lets too much probability flow toward large \theta. The correction factor lowers acceptance for exactly the moves the proposal favours. In general, with the correction, the flow from \theta to \theta' is \tilde p(\theta)\,q(\theta')\min\Big\{1,\ \frac{\tilde p(\theta')q(\theta)}{\tilde p(\theta)q(\theta')}\Big\} = \min\big\{\tilde p(\theta)q(\theta'),\ \tilde p(\theta')q(\theta)\big\}, which is unchanged when \theta and \theta' swap, so the target satisfies detailed balance (the week chapter’s proof).

Without the correction, we can work out what the chain samples instead. Try the density s(\theta)\propto\tilde p(\theta)\,q(\theta). Its flow from \theta to \theta' under the Metropolis rule is \tilde p(\theta)q(\theta)\cdot q(\theta')\min\Big\{1,\ \frac{\tilde p(\theta')}{\tilde p(\theta)}\Big\} = q(\theta)\,q(\theta')\min\big\{\tilde p(\theta),\ \tilde p(\theta')\big\}, which is also unchanged by swapping. So the uncorrected chain satisfies detailed balance with respect to s, and s is its stationary distribution. Here s(\theta)\propto\theta^3(1-\theta)^5\cdot2\theta\propto\theta^4(1-\theta)^5, a \mathrm{Beta}(5,6) density, while the target \theta^3(1-\theta)^5 is \mathrm{Beta}(4,6). A \mathrm{Beta}(a,b) distribution has mean a/(a+b), so the uncorrected sampler would settle around a mean of 5/11\approx0.455 instead of the correct 4/10=0.4: biased toward the values the proposal favours.

Answer. (1) \approx0.516 (Metropolis would give 0.861). (2) 1 either way. (3) The correction discounts moves the proposal makes too often.

Problem 7: One Gibbs sweep, and a tempting bug

Target: a bivariate Gaussian with mean \mu=(1,-1), unit variances, and correlation \rho=-0.5. Start at x^{(0)}=(0,0). The standard-normal draws for the first sweep are \varepsilon_1=0.4 (for x_1) and \varepsilon_2=-1.0 (for x_2), used as \text{mean}+\text{sd}\cdot\varepsilon.

  1. Carry out one Gibbs sweep.
  2. Someone writes the sweep as
x1_new = m1(x2) + sd * eps1
x2_new = m2(x1) + sd * eps2   # uses the old x1
x1, x2 = x1_new, x2_new

where m1, m2 are the conditional means. What value of x_2 does this produce from the same start and draws?

  1. Suppose (x_1,x_2) is already an exact draw from the target. Under the buggy update, what is \mathrm{cov}(x_1',x_2')? Is the target still stationary?
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 5, The conditional distribution of a bivariate Gaussian and Gibbs sampling.

Gibbs sampling updates one coordinate at a time, drawing it from its full conditional given the most recent values of the others. For a bivariate Gaussian with unit variances and correlation \rho, x_1\mid x_2 \sim \mathcal N\big(\mu_1+\rho(x_2-\mu_2),\ 1-\rho^2\big), x_2\mid x_1 \sim \mathcal N\big(\mu_2+\rho(x_1-\mu_1),\ 1-\rho^2\big). The conditional mean shifts by \rho times how far the other coordinate is from its own mean. With \rho=-0.5, a coordinate above its mean pulls the other below its mean, and the reverse. Both conditionals have variance 1-\rho^2=0.75, so \text{sd}=\sqrt{0.75}=0.866, and each draw is “conditional mean +\ 0.866\,\varepsilon” with the given \varepsilon.

Part 1. Update x_1 given x_2=0: mean 1+(-0.5)(0-(-1))=0.5, so x_1=0.5+0.866(0.4)=0.846. Update x_2 given the new x_1=0.846: mean -1+(-0.5)(0.846-1)=-0.923, so x_2=-0.923+0.866(-1.0)=-1.789. After one sweep, x^{(1)}=(0.846,\ -1.789). The negative correlation shows: x_1 came out a little below its mean of 1, which nudged x_2’s conditional mean a little above -1.

Part 2. The buggy line conditions on the old x_1=0: mean -1+(-0.5)(0-1)=-0.5, so x_2=-0.5-0.866=-1.366. Both coordinates are now drawn from the previous state at once. That is not the algorithm in the week chapter, where each coordinate conditions on the most recent value of the others.

Part 3. First, why the correct order works. Suppose (x_1,x_2) is an exact draw from p. Updating x_1 replaces it by a draw from p(x_1\mid x_2), so the new pair has density p(x_2)\,p(x_1'\mid x_2)=p(x_1',x_2), which is still exactly p. The x_2 update that follows does the same, so a full sweep leaves p unchanged.

We can watch this happen in the covariance. Write each update as its conditional mean plus independent noise e of variance 0.75: x_1' = \mu_1+\rho(x_2-\mu_2)+e_1, \qquad x_2' = \mu_2+\rho(x_1'-\mu_1)+e_2. Then \mathrm{var}(x_1')=\rho^2\cdot1+(1-\rho^2)=1 and \mathrm{cov}(x_1',x_2)=\rho\,\mathrm{var}(x_2)=\rho: after the first update the pair still has the target’s variances and covariance. The second update builds x_2' from the new x_1', so \mathrm{cov}(x_1',x_2')=\rho\,\mathrm{var}(x_1')=\rho. The sweep ends with covariance \rho=-0.5, as it should.

The buggy line builds x_2' from the old x_1 instead: x_1' = \mu_1+\rho(x_2-\mu_2)+e_1, \qquad x_2' = \mu_2+\rho(x_1-\mu_1)+e_2. Now x_1' depends on the old x_2 and x_2' depends on the old x_1. The noises are independent of each other and of the old state, so only the old coordinates link the new ones: \mathrm{cov}(x_1',x_2') = \rho\cdot\rho\cdot\mathrm{cov}(x_2,x_1) = \rho^2\cdot\rho = \rho^3 = -0.125. The target has covariance -0.5, so one buggy step moves an exact draw away from the target, and the target is not stationary. Each factor of the buggy update is a correct conditional, but the pair is drawn from p(x_1\mid x_2^{\text{old}})\,p(x_2\mid x_1^{\text{old}}), which is not the joint p.

Each coordinate’s marginal is still right: \mathrm{var}(x_1')=\mathrm{var}(x_2')=1, and the means are unchanged. Repeating the buggy step multiplies the covariance by \rho^2=0.25 each time, so it decays toward 0, and the buggy chain settles on two independent unit-variance Gaussians centred at \mu. Its means and variances match the target, which is why this bug can survive a check of means and variances alone; only the dependence between the coordinates is lost.

Answer. (1) x^{(1)}\approx(0.846,\ -1.789). (2) x_2\approx-1.366. (3) \rho^3=-0.125\neq-0.5; the target is not stationary under the buggy update.

Problem 8: R-hat versus split R-hat

Two chains, each of length n=4 after burn-in, record a scalar quantity:

\text{chain 1: } 1.0,\ 2.2,\ 2.8,\ 4.0, \qquad \text{chain 2: } 1.2,\ 1.8,\ 3.2,\ 3.8.

  1. Compute B, W, \widehat{\mathrm{var}}^+, and \hat R for the two chains.
  2. Now split each chain into halves and compute \hat R for the four half-chains.
  3. Which value tells the truth about these chains, and why?
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 5, The R-hat statistic, including split-\hat R.

\hat R compares two estimates of the target’s variance. W, the average variance inside each chain, is too small if the chains have not yet explored the whole target, because each has seen only part of it. \widehat{\mathrm{var}}^+=\frac{n-1}{n}W+\frac1nB adds a share of B, the disagreement between chain means, so it is too large while chains that started far apart have not yet come together. Once the chains have mixed, both estimate the same variance and \hat R=\sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{var}}^+/W} is near 1. With m chains of length n, B = \frac{n}{m-1}\sum_{j=1}^m\big(\bar x_{\cdot j}-\bar x_{\cdot\cdot}\big)^2, \qquad W = \frac1m\sum_{j=1}^m s_j^2.

Part 1. Here m=2 and n=4. Chain 1’s mean is (1.0+2.2+2.8+4.0)/4=2.5 and chain 2’s is (1.2+1.8+3.2+3.8)/4=2.5, so the overall mean is 2.5 and B=0: the chain means agree exactly. For the sample variances, divide by n-1=3. Chain 1 has deviations -1.5,-0.3,0.3,1.5, whose squares sum to 2.25+0.09+0.09+2.25=4.68, so s_1^2=1.56. Chain 2 has deviations -1.3,-0.7,0.7,1.3, whose squares sum to 1.69+0.49+0.49+1.69=4.36, so s_2^2=1.453. Then W = 1.507, \qquad \widehat{\mathrm{var}}^+ = \tfrac34(1.507)+\tfrac14(0) = 1.13, \hat R = \sqrt{1.13/1.507} = \sqrt{0.75} \approx 0.87. With B=0 the formula reduces to \hat R=\sqrt{(n-1)/n}, slightly below 1 for short chains. By this statistic nothing is wrong.

Part 2. Splitting turns the two chains of length 4 into m=4 chains of length n=2: [1.0,2.2], [2.8,4.0], [1.2,1.8], [3.2,3.8]. Their means are 1.6, 3.4, 1.5, 3.5, with overall mean 2.5, so the squared deviations of the means are 0.81, 0.81, 1, 1. Each half’s sample variance (dividing by n-1=1) is the sum of its two squared deviations: 2(0.6)^2=0.72 for the first two halves and 2(0.3)^2=0.18 for the last two. So B = \tfrac23\big[0.81+0.81+1+1\big] = 2.413, \qquad W = 0.45, \widehat{\mathrm{var}}^+ = \tfrac12(0.45)+\tfrac12(2.413) = 1.432, \qquad \hat R = \sqrt{1.432/0.45} \approx 1.78.

Part 3. The split value. Both chains increase at every step. They agree with each other at every point, so their means coincide, and plain \hat R, which only compares chains with each other, has nothing to detect. But neither chain has settled: the first half of each sits around 1.5 and the second half around 3.5. A chain that has reached its stationary distribution should look the same in its first and second halves. Splitting turns that requirement into a comparison between chains, the halves disagree strongly, and split-\hat R comes out large. Toy chains of length 4 are only for the arithmetic; real diagnostics use far longer runs.

Answer. (1) B=0, W\approx1.507, \widehat{\mathrm{var}}^+\approx1.13, \hat R\approx0.87. (2) Split \hat R\approx1.78. (3) Split-\hat R: the chains drift in step, which only the split comparison detects.

Problem 9: One leapfrog step on a one-player skill posterior

A single player has skill z with prior z\sim\mathcal N(0,1). They play one game against an opponent whose skill is known to be 0, and win. With the course’s win probability \sigma(z-0), where \sigma is the logistic function, the unnormalized log-posterior (dropping constants) is

\log\tilde p(z) = -\tfrac12z^2 + \log\sigma(z).

  1. Using \frac{d}{dy}\log\sigma(y)=1-\sigma(y)=\sigma(-y), write \nabla\log\tilde p(z). Which term pulls z toward the prior mean, and which pushes it up because of the win?
  2. Start HMC at z=0 with fresh momentum v=1 and step size \epsilon=0.5. Do one leapfrog step (L=1) in the gradient-of-log-density form.
  3. With E(z)=-\log\tilde p(z) and K(v)=\tfrac12v^2, compute H at the start and at the end, and the acceptance probability.
TipWorked solution

This uses the energy and Hamiltonian of Week 5, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and the integrator of The leapfrog integrator and the HMC algorithm.

HMC treats the target as a landscape. The potential energy E(z)=-\log\tilde p(z) is low where probability is high. A momentum v\sim\mathcal N(0,1) carries kinetic energy K(v)=\tfrac12v^2, and the total energy is H=E+K. The joint density p(z,v)\propto e^{-H(z,v)} has \tilde p(z) as its z-part, so samples of (z,v) give samples of z. One iteration draws a fresh v, simulates a ball rolling on the landscape with leapfrog steps, and accepts the end point with probability \min\{1,\ e^{H_{\text{start}}-H_{\text{end}}}\}.

Part 1. Differentiate term by term. The prior term gives \frac{d}{dz}\big(-\tfrac12z^2\big)=-z. For the win term, \log\sigma(z)=-\log(1+e^{-z}), so \frac{d}{dz}\log\sigma(z) = \frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}} = \frac{1}{e^{z}+1} = \sigma(-z), the identity given in the stem. Together, \nabla\log\tilde p(z) = -z + \sigma(-z). The -z comes from the Gaussian prior and always points back toward 0. The \sigma(-z) comes from the win and is always positive, so it pushes z upward; it fades as z grows, because a large skill already explains the win.

The two terms cancel where z=\sigma(-z), at z\approx0.40 (check: \sigma(-0.40)\approx0.401). That point is the posterior mode: the gradient of \log\tilde p is positive to its left and negative to its right.

Part 2. The week chapter’s leapfrog step is a half step in momentum, a full step in position, and another half step in momentum, each momentum step using -\tfrac\epsilon2\nabla E. Since E=-\log\tilde p, that is +\tfrac\epsilon2\nabla\log\tilde p. At z=0 the gradient is 0+\sigma(0)=0.5, and the three updates are: v_{1/2} = 1 + 0.25\,(0.5) = 1.125, z' = 0 + 0.5\,(1.125) = 0.5625, \nabla\log\tilde p(0.5625) = -0.5625 + \sigma(-0.5625) = -0.5625+0.3630 = -0.1995, v' = 1.125 + 0.25\,(-0.1995) = 1.0751. The momentum grew on the first half step: at z=0, left of the mode, the gradient points uphill in probability. It shrank slightly on the second: at z=0.5625, past the mode at 0.40, the prior pull wins and the gradient is negative.

Part 3. At the start, E(0)=0-\log\sigma(0)=\log2=0.6931 and K=0.5, so H=1.1931. At the end, E(0.5625) = 0.1582 - \log\sigma(0.5625) = 0.1582+0.4510 = 0.6091, K = \tfrac12(1.0751)^2 = 0.5779, \qquad H = 1.1871. The total energy changed by only -0.006. Potential energy fell by 0.084 as the ball moved toward the mode, and kinetic energy rose by 0.078 to make up for it, as in the week’s worked example. Exact Hamiltonian dynamics would conserve H perfectly; the leftover -0.006 is leapfrog’s discretization error. Because H went down, e^{H_{\text{start}}-H_{\text{end}}}=e^{0.006} is above 1, and the acceptance probability is \min\{1,\ e^{0.006}\}=1.

Answer. (1) \nabla\log\tilde p(z)=-z+\sigma(-z): -z is the prior pull, \sigma(-z) the push from the win. (2) v_{1/2}=1.125, z'=0.5625, v'\approx1.0751. (3) H\approx1.1931\to1.1871; accepted with probability 1.

Problem 10: Which gradient goes in the momentum update

A leapfrog function contains these two momentum lines:

v_half = v + 0.5 * eps * grad(x)
v_new  = v_half + 0.5 * eps * grad(x_new)
  1. For this to be the leapfrog integrator of the week chapter, must grad return \nabla\log\tilde p(x) or \nabla E(x)?
  2. Suppose someone passes the other one by mistake. Using the target of Problem 9, at z=0 with v=1 and \epsilon=0.5, what is v_{1/2} now, and in which direction does the trajectory bend over many steps? What happens to the acceptance rate?
TipWorked solution

This uses the remark on writing leapfrog with the log density in Week 5, The leapfrog integrator and the HMC algorithm.

Hamilton’s equations say how the ball moves: dx/dt=v and dv/dt=-\nabla E(x). The momentum is pushed downhill in energy, which is uphill in probability. Leapfrog approximates these equations step by step, so its momentum updates must carry the same sign.

Part 1. The lecture’s momentum update is v\leftarrow v-\tfrac\epsilon2\nabla E(x). The code adds the gradient, and since -\nabla E=\nabla\log\tilde p, grad must return \nabla\log\tilde p(x), the gradient of the log density. The gradient of the energy would need a minus sign.

Part 2. Passing \nabla E=-\nabla\log\tilde p flips every momentum kick. At z=0 the energy gradient is -0.5, so v_{1/2}=1+0.25(-0.5)=0.875 instead of 1.125: the kick now slows the ball as it heads toward the mode.

Over many steps, the flipped sign means the integrator simulates a different landscape, with potential -E in place of E: the bowl turned upside down. Leapfrog approximately conserves the total energy of the landscape it simulates, which is now -E+K. So along the trajectory K-E stays roughly constant, K and E rise together, and the true Hamiltonian H = E + K = 2E + (K-E) grows by about twice the rise in E. On the upside-down bowl the mode is a peak rather than a valley, so the ball rolls away from it and E keeps rising: the trajectory heads into low-probability regions. By the end, H_{\text{end}} is far above H_{\text{start}}, the acceptance probability \min\{1,\ e^{H_{\text{start}}-H_{\text{end}}}\} is close to 0, and almost every proposal is rejected. The chain sits still.

A collapsed acceptance rate with a small step size is therefore worth checking for a sign error before tuning \epsilon.

Answer. (1) \nabla\log\tilde p. (2) v_{1/2}=0.875; trajectories bend away from high probability, H grows, and acceptance collapses toward 0.

Problem 11: A Metropolis accept step in log space

An accept step is written as

accept_prob = exp(logp(new_state) - logp(old_state))
if random.random() < accept_prob:
    state = new_state

where logp returns \log p(x,v) up to a constant, that is -H(x,v), for a (position, momentum) pair.

  1. If logp(old_state) is -12.4 and logp(new_state) is -13.1, what is the acceptance probability?
  2. If instead logp(new_state) is -12.1, what does accept_prob evaluate to, and is the move accepted? The code never takes \min\{1,\cdot\}. Is that a bug?
  3. Why compute \exp of a difference of logs, rather than p(new) / p(old)?
TipWorked solution

This uses the acceptance rule of Week 5, The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm and its HMC form in The leapfrog integrator and the HMC algorithm.

For HMC the accept step is MH with a symmetric proposal, so the acceptance probability is \min\{1,\ r\} with r=p(\text{new})/p(\text{old}). In logs, r=\exp\big(\log p(\text{new})-\log p(\text{old})\big), which is what the code computes, apart from the \min. With \log p=-H, this is the week chapter’s e^{H_{\text{start}}-H_{\text{end}}}.

Part 1. e^{-13.1-(-12.4)}=e^{-0.7}\approx0.497. In energy terms, H rose by 0.7, and the move is accepted about half the time.

Part 2. e^{-12.1+12.4}=e^{0.3}\approx1.35. random.random() returns a number u uniform on [0,1), which is always below 1.35, so the move is always accepted. In general, the rule “accept if u<r” accepts with probability P(u<r) = \begin{cases} r, & 0\le r\le1,\\ 1, & r>1,\end{cases} which is exactly \min\{1,r\}. The \min is built into the comparison, so leaving it out is not a bug. It would matter if the code also reported accept_prob, for example to average acceptance rates, because values above 1 would inflate the average.

Part 3. A double-precision number cannot represent anything positive below about 10^{-324}, roughly e^{-745}. A log density of -800 is unremarkable for a posterior over many players and games, yet e^{-800} evaluates to exactly 0, and so does e^{-801}. Their ratio is 0/0, which is undefined, even though the true ratio is e^{1}\approx2.7. Subtracting first gives -800-(-801)=1, and e^{1} is computed without trouble. Any constant shared by both terms, including \log Z, cancels in the difference, which is the same reason MH never needs Z.

Answer. (1) \approx0.497. (2) \approx1.35, always accepted; the missing \min is harmless. (3) To avoid underflow; the difference is small and constants cancel.

Problem 12: What the stored chain contains

A sampler starts at \theta=0 and runs this loop six times:

samples = []
for t in range(6):
    samples.append(theta)
    # propose theta_new, then accept or reject
    theta = accept_or_reject(theta, theta_new)

The six proposals and decisions are: 0.8 accepted, 1.4 rejected, 1.1 accepted, 2.0 rejected, 1.5 rejected, 0.9 accepted.

  1. Write out samples. Is the last accepted value, 0.9, in it?
  2. What is the acceptance rate?
  3. Compute the mean of samples, and the mean you would get if repeated values were dropped. Which one is the MCMC estimate, and why?
  4. Why is the first stored value suspect, and what is it called when we discard such early values?
  5. For a posterior over N players’ skills, what shape are theta and the momentum drawn in each HMC iteration?
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 5, Why MCMC and the remark “a rejection is still a sample” in The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm.

Part 1. The state is appended before each update, so the list records the state at the start of each iteration. A rejection leaves \theta unchanged, so the next append repeats it: [\,0,\ 0.8,\ 0.8,\ 1.1,\ 1.1,\ 1.1\,]. The final value 0.9 is reached after the last append and is not stored. It would be the first entry of a seventh iteration.

Part 2. Three of six proposals were accepted: 3/6=0.5.

Part 3. With repeats, the mean is 4.9/6\approx0.817. Dropping repeats leaves [0,0.8,1.1] with mean 0.633. The first is the MCMC estimate. The chain’s transition rule includes the stay-put probability (the week chapter’s r(x\mid x)), and it is that whole rule, stays included, whose stationary distribution is the target. Staying at 1.1 for three iterations is how the chain records that 1.1 has more posterior mass than the proposals it rejected.

Problem 5’s Metropolis chain shows concretely what dropping repeats does. Its target is \pi=(0.5,\ 0.3,\ 0.2) and its stay probabilities are A_{11}=0.5, A_{22}=\tfrac16, A_{33}=0. Dropping repeats counts each visit to a state once, however many steps it lasts. A visit to j ends with probability 1-A_{jj} at each step spent there, so in a long run the number of separate visits to j is proportional to \pi_j(1-A_{jj}). Those weights are 0.25, 0.25, 0.2, which normalize to (0.357,\ 0.357,\ 0.286). The most probable state drops from 0.5 to 0.357, because its long stays were exactly what gave it its weight.

Part 4. The chain was started at an arbitrary point, 0, not at a draw from the posterior. The convergence theorem says the distribution of the state approaches the target as the chain runs, not that it starts there; Problem 3’s C_3 needed several steps before its distribution was close to \pi. Early states therefore reflect the starting point more than the target, and discarding them is the burn-in. With only six samples nothing here can be called converged; the arithmetic is for bookkeeping only.

Part 5. Both have one entry per player: shape (N,). HMC samples all coordinates jointly. The momentum must have the same shape as the position, because each leapfrog position update adds \epsilon v to \theta and each momentum update adds a multiple of the gradient, which has one entry per coordinate.

Answer. (1) [0,0.8,0.8,1.1,1.1,1.1]; 0.9 is not stored. (2) 0.5. (3) \approx0.817 with repeats (correct) versus 0.633 without. (4) It is the arbitrary start; dropping early values is burn-in. (5) Both shape (N,).

Problem 13: From a sample matrix to a ranking

After burn-in, a sampler has produced six draws of four players’ skills, stored as an array S of shape (6, 4): one row per sample, one column per player.

sample player 0 player 1 player 2 player 3
1 0.9 −0.4 1.6 0.2
2 1.1 −0.2 1.2 0.5
3 0.7 −0.6 1.5 0.9
4 1.3 0.1 1.0 0.4
5 0.8 −0.5 1.4 1.0
6 1.0 −0.3 1.1 0.6
  1. Estimate each player’s posterior mean and standard deviation. Along which axis of S do the averages run?
  2. Rank the players from highest to lowest mean skill.
  3. Estimate \mathbb P(z_3\ge z_0\mid\text{games}).
  4. These draws come from a Markov chain. Why is the estimate in part 3 less precise than p(1-p)/R suggests?
TipWorked solution

This uses the Monte Carlo average of Week 5, Why MCMC and Effective sample size.

Every summary of a posterior that MCMC gives us is a Monte Carlo average: an expectation \mathbb E[\phi(z)\mid\text{games}] is estimated by averaging \phi over the draws, \frac1R\sum_{r=1}^R\phi(z^{(r)}), with R=6 draws here. A posterior mean takes \phi(z)=z_k, a variance takes \phi(z)=(z_k-\text{mean})^2, and a probability takes \phi to be an indicator. Each row of S is one draw z^{(r)} of all four skills jointly.

Part 1. A column holds every draw of one player’s skill, which is a sample from that player’s marginal posterior, so the averages run down the rows (axis 0), giving one number per player. For player 0, the mean is 5.8/6=0.967; the squared deviations sum to 0.2333, so dividing by 6 and taking the square root gives a standard deviation of 0.197. The same steps for every column:

player 0 player 1 player 2 player 3
mean 0.967 −0.317 1.300 0.600
std 0.197 0.227 0.216 0.277

(Dividing by 5 instead of 6 gives slightly larger values; with thousands of samples the difference vanishes.) Part 2. Sorting by mean: player 2, player 0, player 3, player 1. In code this is an argsort of the mean vector, reversed for highest first; the result is a list of player indices, not of skills.

Part 3. A probability is the expectation of an indicator, \mathbb P(z_3\ge z_0\mid\text{games})=\mathbb E\big[\mathbf 1[z_3\ge z_0]\mid\text{games}\big], so its estimate is the fraction of draws in which the event holds. Row by row, z_3\ge z_0 holds in samples 3 (0.9\ge0.7) and 5 (1.0\ge0.8) only, so the estimate is 2/6\approx0.333.

The comparison has to be made within each row, because each row is one joint draw and the event is about the two skills together. The means alone say only that player 0 is higher on average (0.967 against 0.600), not how often player 3 comes out ahead. Comparing values from different rows would mix skills from different draws, a pairing the posterior never produced.

Part 4. For independent draws, the indicator \mathbf 1[z_3\ge z_0] is a Bernoulli variable with variance p(1-p), so the average of R of them has variance p(1-p)/R (Week 4’s simple Monte Carlo). That formula assumes independence. Consecutive MCMC draws are positively correlated, so R draws carry the information of only n_{\text{eff}}<R independent ones, and the variance is closer to p(1-p)/n_{\text{eff}}. With p\approx\tfrac13, six independent draws would give a standard error of \sqrt{(1/3)(2/3)/6}\approx0.19; if the correlation cut the effective sample size to 2, it would be about 0.33.

Answer. (1) Means (0.967,\ -0.317,\ 1.300,\ 0.600), standard deviations (0.197,\ 0.227,\ 0.216,\ 0.277), averaging over axis 0. (2) 2,0,3,1. (3) 1/3. (4) Correlated samples reduce the effective sample size.

Problem 14: Step size, trajectory length, and what samples are worth

A posterior has standard deviation about 1 in each coordinate. Three HMC settings are tried:

setting \epsilon L
A 0.02 10
B 0.1 15
C 1.5 1
  1. For each setting, how far can one trajectory move (in units of the posterior’s standard deviation), and how many gradient evaluations does each iteration cost?
  2. Which setting behaves most like a random walk, and which is most likely to have a low acceptance rate? Explain.
  3. A run of 4000 samples has lag-one autocorrelation 0.95. Assuming \rho_t=\rho^t as in the week’s example, estimate n_{\text{eff}}. Repeat for \rho=0.5.
  4. Four chains of setting A all pass \hat R\approx1. Is that enough to trust them?
TipWorked solution

This uses the two knobs of Week 5, The leapfrog integrator and the HMC algorithm, Effective sample size, and Diagnostics rule of thumb.

HMC has two knobs. The step size \epsilon sets how accurately leapfrog follows the true dynamics, and so how close to constant H stays and how often proposals are accepted. The number of steps L, together with \epsilon, sets how far one trajectory travels before it is proposed.

Part 1. Each leapfrog step moves the position by \epsilon times the current momentum. A fresh momentum is drawn from \mathcal N(0,1) in each coordinate, so it is typically of size about 1, and L steps move the position by about L\epsilon at most (less if the momentum turns around). So the trajectory length is L\epsilon: A 0.2, B 1.5, C 1.5 standard deviations. Each leapfrog step evaluates the gradient once, so A costs about 10 gradients per iteration, B 15, and C 1.

Part 2. A behaves most like a random walk. Each proposal moves at most about 0.2 standard deviations, so the chain needs many iterations to cross the posterior, consecutive samples are strongly correlated, and the acceptance rate is high for the wrong reason, as with the tutorial’s tiny-step chain. C is most likely to have a low acceptance rate. Leapfrog follows the true dynamics well only when each step is small compared with the scale on which the gradient changes. A single step of 1.5 standard deviations is not small, so the error in H is large and e^{H_{\text{start}}-H_{\text{end}}} is often small (the tutorial saw acceptance collapse when \epsilon went from 0.2 to 0.7). B covers the same distance as C in fifteen small steps, so it travels far while keeping H nearly constant, at fifteen times the cost per iteration.

Part 3. The effective sample size divides the number of draws by 1+2\sum_{t\ge1}\rho_t. With \rho_t=\rho^t the sum is a geometric series, \sum_{t\ge1}\rho^t=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}, so 1+\frac{2\rho}{1-\rho} = \frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}. For \rho=0.95 the factor is 1.95/0.05=39, so n_{\text{eff}}\approx4000/39\approx103. For \rho=0.5 it is 1.5/0.5=3, so n_{\text{eff}}\approx1333. The same number of iterations is worth about thirteen times more in the second case.

Part 4. Not on its own. \hat R compares chains with each other, so it can only detect chains that disagree. Slow chains started near each other can agree while all of them have explored only a small part of the posterior. The rule of thumb therefore asks for \hat R near 1 and \hat n_{\text{eff}} above 10 per chain. Setting A’s small moves make its draws highly correlated, which shows up as a low \hat n_{\text{eff}} even when \hat R looks fine. Even passing both checks shows only that the chains appear mixed.

How the specific settings in a homework behave on its data is left to the homework.

Answer. (1) L\epsilon=0.2,\ 1.5,\ 1.5; costs 10, 15, 1 gradients. (2) A is random-walk-like; C risks low acceptance. (3) n_{\text{eff}}\approx103 and \approx1333. (4) No: also check \hat n_{\text{eff}}, and passing shows apparent mixing only.
