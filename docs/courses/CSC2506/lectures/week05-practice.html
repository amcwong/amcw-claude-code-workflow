Lecture practice

Problem 1: Two steps on a three-state chain

A stationary Markov chain on states \{1,2,3\} has transition matrix

A = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0.3 & 0.2\\ 0.2 & 0.6 & 0.2\\ 0.1 & 0.3 & 0.6\end{pmatrix}, \qquad A_{ij}=p(x_t=j\mid x_{t-1}=i).

  1. Check that A is a valid transition matrix. Do its columns need to sum to 1?
  2. Starting in state 1, what is the probability of being in state 3 two steps later? Compute it as a sum over the intermediate state.
  3. Compute the full two-step matrix A(2) and check one of its rows.
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 5, Transition matrices and state transition diagrams and n-step transitions and the Chapman-Kolmogorov equations.

  1. Every entry is nonnegative, and the rows sum to 0.5+0.3+0.2=1, 0.2+0.6+0.2=1, 0.1+0.3+0.6=1. Row i is the distribution of the next state given the current state i, so rows must sum to 1. Columns need not: here they sum to 0.8, 1.2, 1.0.
  2. The chain must pass through some state k after one step: A_{13}(2) = \sum_k A_{1k}A_{k3} = 0.5(0.2)+0.3(0.2)+0.2(0.6) = 0.10+0.06+0.12 = 0.28. The three terms are “stay at 1, then move to 3”, “move to 2, then to 3”, and “move to 3, then stay”.
  3. By Chapman-Kolmogorov, A(2)=A^2: A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0.33 & 0.39 & 0.28\\ 0.24 & 0.48 & 0.28\\ 0.17 & 0.39 & 0.44\end{pmatrix}. Row 1 sums to 0.33+0.39+0.28=1, as every row of a transition matrix must.

Answer. (1) Valid; only rows must sum to 1. (2) 0.28. (3) A^2 as above.

Problem 2: The stationary distribution of the same chain

For the chain of Problem 1:

  1. Find the stationary distribution \pi by solving A^\top\pi=\pi with \pi_1+\pi_2+\pi_3=1.
  2. Check your answer by applying one step of the chain.
  3. Which state does the chain spend most time in, in the long run? Explain from the matrix why.
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 5, The stationary distribution.

  1. The condition \pi_j=\sum_i\pi_iA_{ij} gives one equation per column. Column 1 and column 3: \pi_1 = 0.5\pi_1+0.2\pi_2+0.1\pi_3 \ \Rightarrow\ 5\pi_1 = 2\pi_2+\pi_3, \pi_3 = 0.2\pi_1+0.2\pi_2+0.6\pi_3 \ \Rightarrow\ 2\pi_3 = \pi_1+\pi_2. (The column-2 equation is implied by these two and the rows summing to 1, so it adds nothing.) Substituting \pi_3=5\pi_1-2\pi_2 into the second equation gives 10\pi_1-4\pi_2=\pi_1+\pi_2, so \pi_2=\tfrac95\pi_1 and \pi_3=\tfrac75\pi_1. Normalizing, \pi_1(1+\tfrac95+\tfrac75)=1 gives \pi_1=\tfrac5{21}, so \pi = \big(\tfrac{5}{21},\ \tfrac{9}{21},\ \tfrac{7}{21}\big) \approx (0.238,\ 0.429,\ 0.333).
  2. Column 2 of A is (0.3,0.6,0.3), so (A^\top\pi)_2=\tfrac{0.3(5)+0.6(9)+0.3(7)}{21}=\tfrac{9}{21}, which equals \pi_2. The other two entries check the same way.
  3. State 2. Its self-transition probability, 0.6, ties for the largest, and every state sends at least 0.3 of its probability into state 2. State 1 keeps only half its probability and receives little from the others. As with the two-state chain in the week chapter, states that are easy to enter and hard to leave collect long-run probability.

Answer. \pi=(5,9,7)/21\approx(0.238,0.429,0.333); state 2 is visited most.

Problem 3: Unique? Convergent?

Three chains on states \{1,2,3\}:

C_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0&1&0\\ 0&0&1\\ 1&0&0\end{pmatrix}, C_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1&0&0\\ 0.3&0.4&0.3\\ 0&0&1\end{pmatrix}, C_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 0&1&0\\ 0&0.5&0.5\\ 0.5&0&0.5\end{pmatrix}.

For each chain: is it irreducible? Is it regular? Is its stationary distribution unique? Started in a single state, does the distribution of x_t converge?

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 5, When is the stationary distribution unique?

C_1, the deterministic 3-cycle 1\to2\to3\to1. Every state reaches every other, so it is irreducible. But C_1^3=I, so the powers cycle through C_1, C_1^2, I, and each has zeros: not regular. The stationary equations A^\top\pi=\pi read \pi_1=\pi_3, \pi_2=\pi_1, \pi_3=\pi_2, so the only solution is (\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13): unique. Started in state 1, the distribution goes (1,0,0)\to(0,1,0)\to(0,0,1)\to(1,0,0) and never converges. This is the three-state version of the week’s swapping chain.

C_2, two absorbing states. States 1 and 3 can never be left, so state 1 cannot reach state 3: not irreducible, hence not regular. Both (1,0,0) and (0,0,1) are stationary, and so is any mixture (a,0,1-a): not unique. Each run does converge: from state 2, every step leaks 0.3 to each side, so the distribution tends to (0.5,0,0.5), while from state 1 it stays (1,0,0). The limit depends on the start, which is exactly what MCMC cannot allow.

C_3. The path 1\to2\to3\to1 shows it is irreducible. C_3^2 still has a zero (state 1 cannot reach itself in two steps), but C_3^3 = \begin{pmatrix} 0.25&0.25&0.5\\ 0.25&0.375&0.375\\ 0.125&0.5&0.375\end{pmatrix} is all positive, so the chain is regular. By the theorem, the stationary distribution is unique and the chain converges to it from any start. Solving gives \pi=(0.2,\ 0.4,\ 0.4); check: (C_3^\top\pi)_1=0.5(0.4)=0.2.

Regularity can take a few steps to show up: a zero in A or A^2 does not rule it out.

Answer. C_1: irreducible, not regular, unique, does not converge. C_2: not irreducible, not regular, not unique, converges to a start-dependent limit. C_3: irreducible, regular, unique \pi=(0.2,0.4,0.4), converges.

Problem 4: A bigram model from a tiny corpus

A first-order language model is fitted by maximum likelihood to four sentences:

“I like turtles”, “you like turtles”, “I like snails”, “you don’t like snails”.

  1. Compute \hat\pi (first-word probabilities) and every nonzero \hat A_{jk}.
  2. Score the sentence “you don’t like turtles”.
  3. Score “I don’t like turtles”. What goes wrong, and why?
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 5, Application: Markov language models.

  1. Two sentences start with “I” and two with “you”, so N^1_{\text{I}}=N^1_{\text{you}}=2 and \hat\pi_{\text{I}}=\hat\pi_{\text{you}}=\tfrac12. The MLE of each row is “count over row total”:
    • after “I”: like twice, so \hat A_{\text{I},\text{like}}=1;
    • after “you”: like once, don’t once, so \tfrac12 each;
    • after “don’t”: like once, so \hat A_{\text{don't},\text{like}}=1;
    • after “like”: turtles twice, snails twice, so \tfrac12 each.
  2. One first-word factor and one transition per later word: p(\text{you don't like turtles}) = \hat\pi_{\text{you}}\,\hat A_{\text{you},\text{don't}} \times\,\hat A_{\text{don't},\text{like}}\,\hat A_{\text{like},\text{turtles}} = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12\cdot1\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac18. This sentence never appears in the corpus, yet it gets positive probability, because each of its bigrams does appear.
  3. The factor \hat A_{\text{I},\text{don't}} is 0: “don’t” never follows “I” in the corpus. One zero factor makes the whole product 0, so a perfectly reasonable sentence is called impossible. Maximum likelihood gives zero probability to every transition it has not seen, because the count-over-total formula of the Multinomial MLE turns a zero count into a zero probability.

Answer. (1) \hat\pi=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12) for I, you; transitions as listed. (2) \tfrac18. (3) 0, because the unseen bigram “I don’t” has MLE probability 0.

Problem 5: Stationary without detailed balance, and a chain built to satisfy it

  1. Show that the uniform distribution is stationary for the 3-cycle C_1 of Problem 3, but that C_1 does not satisfy detailed balance with respect to it.
  2. Now take the target \pi=(0.5,\ 0.3,\ 0.2) on \{1,2,3\}. Build a Metropolis chain: from state i, propose each of the two other states with probability \tfrac12, and accept a proposed j with probability \min\{1,\pi_j/\pi_i\}. Write out the full transition matrix, including the probabilities of staying put.
  3. Verify detailed balance for every pair, and verify A^\top\pi=\pi directly.
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 5, Detailed balance and Why Metropolis-Hastings works.

  1. Problem 3 showed (\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13) is stationary. For detailed balance we need \pi_iA_{ij}=\pi_jA_{ji}. Take i=1, j=2: the left side is \tfrac13\cdot1 and the right side is \tfrac13\cdot0. Probability flows around the cycle in one direction only, so the flow along each edge is not balanced, even though the total flow into each state equals the total flow out. This is the week’s point that detailed balance is sufficient, not necessary.
  2. The proposal is symmetric (q=\tfrac12 both ways), so this is the Metropolis rule. A move off state i to j has probability \tfrac12\min\{1,\pi_j/\pi_i\}:
    • from 1: to 2, \tfrac12\cdot0.6=0.3; to 3, \tfrac12\cdot0.4=0.2; stay 0.5;
    • from 2: to 1, \tfrac12\cdot1=0.5; to 3, \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac23=\tfrac13; stay \tfrac16;
    • from 3: to 1, 0.5; to 2, 0.5; stay 0.
    So A = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0.3 & 0.2\\ 0.5 & \tfrac16 & \tfrac13\\ 0.5 & 0.5 & 0\end{pmatrix}. The stay-put entries are the rejected proposals. State 3 never rejects, because both alternatives are more probable.
  3. Detailed balance, pair by pair: \pi_1A_{12} = 0.5(0.3) = 0.15 = 0.3(0.5) = \pi_2A_{21}, \pi_1A_{13} = 0.5(0.2) = 0.1 = 0.2(0.5) = \pi_3A_{31}, \pi_2A_{23} = 0.3(\tfrac13) = 0.1 = 0.2(0.5) = \pi_3A_{32}. Each flow equals \tfrac12\min\{\pi_i,\pi_j\}, symmetric in i and j, as in the proof. Directly, column by column: 0.5(0.5)+0.3(0.5)+0.2(0.5) = 0.5, 0.5(0.3)+0.3(\tfrac16)+0.2(0.5) = 0.3, 0.5(0.2)+0.3(\tfrac13)+0.2(0) = 0.2.

Answer. (1) \tfrac13\cdot1\neq\tfrac13\cdot0, so no detailed balance despite stationarity. (2) A as above. (3) All three pair flows match, and A^\top\pi=\pi.

Problem 6: When the Hastings correction matters

The target on (0,1) is \tilde p(\theta)=\theta^3(1-\theta)^5. The proposal ignores the current state and draws \theta' from the density q(\theta')=2\theta' on (0,1), which favours large values.

  1. The chain is at \theta=0.3 and proposes \theta'=0.5. Compute the Metropolis-Hastings acceptance probability, and the value you would get by wrongly using the Metropolis rule (no q ratio).
  2. Repeat for the reverse move, from 0.5 to 0.3.
  3. Explain the direction of the correction.
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 5, The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm.

Here q(x'\mid x)=2x', so q(\theta\mid\theta')=2\theta and q(\theta'\mid\theta)=2\theta'. The target ratio is \frac{\tilde p(0.5)}{\tilde p(0.3)} = \Big(\frac{0.5}{0.3}\Big)^3\Big(\frac{0.5}{0.7}\Big)^5 = 4.630\times0.1859 \approx 0.861.

  1. MH multiplies by q(0.3\mid0.5)/q(0.5\mid0.3)=0.6/1.0: \alpha = \min\{1,\ 0.861\times0.6\} \approx 0.516. The Metropolis rule would give \min\{1,0.861\}=0.861.
  2. Reverse: the target ratio is 1/0.861\approx1.162 and the q ratio is 1.0/0.6, so \alpha=\min\{1,\ 1.936\}=1. The Metropolis rule also gives 1.
  3. This proposal suggests 0.5 more readily than 0.3 (density 1.0 against 0.6). Without correction, the chain would accept those frequent upward proposals too often and overweight large \theta; its stationary distribution would be proportional to \tilde p(\theta)\,q(\theta) rather than \tilde p(\theta). The factor q(x\mid x')/q(x'\mid x) lowers acceptance for moves the proposal favours, so the flows \min\{p(x)q(x'\mid x),\,p(x')q(x\mid x')\} balance again.

Answer. (1) \approx0.516 (Metropolis would give 0.861). (2) 1 either way. (3) The correction discounts moves the proposal makes too often.

Problem 7: One Gibbs sweep, and a tempting bug

Target: a bivariate Gaussian with mean \mu=(1,-1), unit variances, and correlation \rho=-0.5. Start at x^{(0)}=(0,0). The standard-normal draws for the first sweep are \varepsilon_1=0.4 (for x_1) and \varepsilon_2=-1.0 (for x_2), used as \text{mean}+\text{sd}\cdot\varepsilon.

  1. Carry out one Gibbs sweep.
  2. Someone writes the sweep as
x1_new = m1(x2) + sd * eps1
x2_new = m2(x1) + sd * eps2   # uses the old x1
x1, x2 = x1_new, x2_new

where m1, m2 are the conditional means. What value of x_2 does this produce from the same start and draws?

  1. Suppose (x_1,x_2) is already an exact draw from the target. Under the buggy update, what is \mathrm{cov}(x_1',x_2')? Is the target still stationary?
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 5, The conditional distribution of a bivariate Gaussian and Gibbs sampling.

Both full conditionals have variance 1-\rho^2=0.75, so \text{sd}=0.866.

  1. Update x_1 given x_2=0: mean 1+(-0.5)(0-(-1))=0.5, so x_1=0.5+0.866(0.4)=0.846. Update x_2 given the new x_1=0.846: mean -1+(-0.5)(0.846-1)=-0.923, so x_2=-0.923+0.866(-1.0)=-1.789. After one sweep, x^{(1)}=(0.846,\ -1.789). The negative correlation shows: x_1 came out a little below its mean, which nudged x_2’s conditional mean a little above -1.
  2. The buggy line conditions on the old x_1=0: mean -1+(-0.5)(0-1)=-0.5, so x_2=-0.5-0.866=-1.366. Both coordinates are now drawn from the previous state at once, which is not the algorithm in the week chapter, where each coordinate conditions on the most recent value of the others.
  3. Under the buggy update, x_1'=\mu_1+\rho(x_2-\mu_2)+\text{noise} and x_2'=\mu_2+\rho(x_1-\mu_1)+\text{noise}, with independent noises. So \mathrm{cov}(x_1',x_2') = \rho^2\,\mathrm{cov}(x_2,x_1) = \rho^3 = -0.125. The target has covariance -0.5, so one step of the buggy update moves an exact draw away from the target: the target is not stationary. Each coordinate’s marginal is still correct, which is why this bug can survive a check of means and variances alone.

Answer. (1) x^{(1)}\approx(0.846,\ -1.789). (2) x_2\approx-1.366. (3) \rho^3=-0.125\neq-0.5; the target is not stationary under the buggy update.

Problem 8: R-hat versus split R-hat

Two chains, each of length n=4 after burn-in, record a scalar quantity:

\text{chain 1: } 1.0,\ 2.2,\ 2.8,\ 4.0, \qquad \text{chain 2: } 1.2,\ 1.8,\ 3.2,\ 3.8.

  1. Compute B, W, \widehat{\mathrm{var}}^+, and \hat R for the two chains.
  2. Now split each chain into halves and compute \hat R for the four half-chains.
  3. Which value tells the truth about these chains, and why?
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 5, The R-hat statistic, including split-\hat R.

  1. Both chain means are 2.5, so the overall mean is 2.5 and B=0. Sample variances: chain 1 has deviations -1.5,-0.3,0.3,1.5, so s_1^2=4.68/3=1.56; chain 2 has -1.3,-0.7,0.7,1.3, so s_2^2=4.36/3=1.453. Then W = 1.507, \qquad \widehat{\mathrm{var}}^+ = \tfrac34(1.507)+\tfrac14(0) = 1.13, \hat R = \sqrt{1.13/1.507} = \sqrt{0.75} \approx 0.87. With B=0, \hat R=\sqrt{(n-1)/n}, slightly below 1 for short chains. Nothing here signals a problem.
  2. The halves [1.0,2.2], [2.8,4.0], [1.2,1.8], [3.2,3.8] have means 1.6, 3.4, 1.5, 3.5 (overall 2.5) and sample variances 0.72, 0.72, 0.18, 0.18. With m=4, n=2: B = \tfrac23\big[0.81+0.81+1+1\big] = 2.413, \qquad W = 0.45, \widehat{\mathrm{var}}^+ = \tfrac12(0.45)+\tfrac12(2.413) = 1.432, \qquad \hat R = \sqrt{1.432/0.45} \approx 1.78.
  3. The split value. Both chains drift steadily upward together, so they agree with each other at every point and plain \hat R cannot see the drift. Splitting compares each chain’s first half with its second half, and the halves disagree strongly, which reveals that neither chain is stationary yet. Toy chains of length 4 are only for the arithmetic; real diagnostics use far longer runs.

Answer. (1) B=0, W\approx1.507, \widehat{\mathrm{var}}^+\approx1.13, \hat R\approx0.87. (2) Split \hat R\approx1.78. (3) Split-\hat R: the chains drift in step, which only the split comparison detects.

Problem 9: One leapfrog step on a one-player skill posterior

A single player has skill z with prior z\sim\mathcal N(0,1). They play one game against an opponent whose skill is known to be 0, and win. With the course’s win probability \sigma(z-0), where \sigma is the logistic function, the unnormalized log-posterior (dropping constants) is

\log\tilde p(z) = -\tfrac12z^2 + \log\sigma(z).

  1. Using \frac{d}{dy}\log\sigma(y)=1-\sigma(y)=\sigma(-y), write \nabla\log\tilde p(z). Which term pulls z toward the prior mean, and which pushes it up because of the win?
  2. Start HMC at z=0 with fresh momentum v=1 and step size \epsilon=0.5. Do one leapfrog step (L=1) in the gradient-of-log-density form.
  3. With E(z)=-\log\tilde p(z) and K(v)=\tfrac12v^2, compute H at the start and at the end, and the acceptance probability.
TipWorked solution

This uses the energy and Hamiltonian of Week 5, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and the integrator of The leapfrog integrator and the HMC algorithm.

  1. Differentiate term by term: \nabla\log\tilde p(z) = -z + \sigma(-z). The -z comes from the Gaussian prior and always points back toward 0. The \sigma(-z) comes from the win and is always positive, so it pushes z upward; it fades as z grows, because a large skill already explains the win.

  2. At z=0 the gradient is 0+\sigma(0)=0.5. The three leapfrog updates, with +\tfrac\epsilon2\nabla\log\tilde p in place of -\tfrac\epsilon2\nabla E: v_{1/2} = 1 + 0.25\,(0.5) = 1.125, z' = 0 + 0.5\,(1.125) = 0.5625, \nabla\log\tilde p(0.5625) = -0.5625 + \sigma(-0.5625) = -0.5625+0.3630 = -0.1995, v' = 1.125 + 0.25\,(-0.1995) = 1.0751. The momentum grew on the first half step (the win pushed uphill in probability) and shrank slightly on the second (past z\approx0.4 the prior pull wins).

  3. At the start, E(0)=0-\log\sigma(0)=\log2=0.6931 and K=0.5, so H=1.1931. At the end, E(0.5625) = 0.1582 - \log\sigma(0.5625) = 0.1582+0.4510 = 0.6091, K = \tfrac12(1.0751)^2 = 0.5779, \qquad H = 1.1871. The total energy changed by only -0.006: potential energy dropped and kinetic energy rose by almost the same amount, as in the week’s worked example. The acceptance probability is \min\{1,\ e^{1.1931-1.1871}\}=\min\{1,\ e^{0.006}\}=1.

Answer. (1) \nabla\log\tilde p(z)=-z+\sigma(-z): -z is the prior pull, \sigma(-z) the push from the win. (2) v_{1/2}=1.125, z'=0.5625, v'\approx1.0751. (3) H\approx1.1931\to1.1871; accepted with probability 1.

Problem 10: Which gradient goes in the momentum update

A leapfrog function contains these two momentum lines:

v_half = v + 0.5 * eps * grad(x)
v_new  = v_half + 0.5 * eps * grad(x_new)
  1. For this to be the leapfrog integrator of the week chapter, must grad return \nabla\log\tilde p(x) or \nabla E(x)?
  2. Suppose someone passes the other one by mistake. Using the target of Problem 9, at z=0 with v=1 and \epsilon=0.5, what is v_{1/2} now, and in which direction does the trajectory bend over many steps? What happens to the acceptance rate?
TipWorked solution

This uses the remark on writing leapfrog with the log density in Week 5, The leapfrog integrator and the HMC algorithm.

  1. The lecture’s momentum update is v\leftarrow v-\tfrac\epsilon2\nabla E(x). The code adds the gradient, and since -\nabla E=\nabla\log\tilde p, grad must return \nabla\log\tilde p(x), the gradient of the log density. The gradient of the energy would need a minus sign.
  2. Passing \nabla E=-\nabla\log\tilde p flips every momentum kick. At z=0 the energy gradient is -0.5, so v_{1/2}=1+0.25(-0.5)=0.875 instead of 1.125. Over many steps the momentum is pushed toward higher energy, that is toward low-probability regions: the ball rolls uphill and accelerates away from the mode. Total energy is no longer close to conserved along the simulated path, H at the end is typically far larger than at the start, and e^{H_{\text{start}}-H_{\text{end}}} is close to 0. Almost every proposal is rejected and the chain sits still.

A collapsed acceptance rate with a small step size is therefore worth checking for a sign error before tuning \epsilon.

Answer. (1) \nabla\log\tilde p. (2) v_{1/2}=0.875; trajectories bend away from high probability, H grows, and acceptance collapses toward 0.

Problem 11: A Metropolis accept step in log space

An accept step is written as

accept_prob = exp(logp(new_state) - logp(old_state))
if random.random() < accept_prob:
    state = new_state

where logp returns \log p(x,v) up to a constant, that is -H(x,v), for a (position, momentum) pair.

  1. If logp(old_state) is -12.4 and logp(new_state) is -13.1, what is the acceptance probability?
  2. If instead logp(new_state) is -12.1, what does accept_prob evaluate to, and is the move accepted? The code never takes \min\{1,\cdot\}. Is that a bug?
  3. Why compute \exp of a difference of logs, rather than p(new) / p(old)?
TipWorked solution

This uses the acceptance rule of Week 5, The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm and its HMC form in The leapfrog integrator and the HMC algorithm.

  1. e^{-13.1-(-12.4)}=e^{-0.7}\approx0.497. In energy terms, H rose by 0.7, and the move is accepted about half the time.
  2. e^{-12.1+12.4}=e^{0.3}\approx1.35. random.random() returns a number in [0,1), which is always below 1.35, so the move is always accepted. Accepting with probability \min\{1,r\} and testing u<r for a uniform u give the same decision for every r, so leaving out the \min is not a bug.
  3. The log densities of a posterior with many coordinates are large negative numbers (hundreds or thousands). Exponentiating each one separately underflows to 0, and 0/0 is undefined. The difference of logs is a modest number, and only that is exponentiated. Any constant, including \log Z, cancels in the difference, which is the same reason MH never needs Z.

Answer. (1) \approx0.497. (2) \approx1.35, always accepted; the missing \min is harmless. (3) To avoid underflow; the difference is small and constants cancel.

Problem 12: What the stored chain contains

A sampler starts at \theta=0 and runs this loop six times:

samples = []
for t in range(6):
    samples.append(theta)
    # propose theta_new, then accept or reject
    theta = accept_or_reject(theta, theta_new)

The six proposals and decisions are: 0.8 accepted, 1.4 rejected, 1.1 accepted, 2.0 rejected, 1.5 rejected, 0.9 accepted.

  1. Write out samples. Is the last accepted value, 0.9, in it?
  2. What is the acceptance rate?
  3. Compute the mean of samples, and the mean you would get if repeated values were dropped. Which one is the MCMC estimate, and why?
  4. Why is the first stored value suspect, and what is it called when we discard such early values?
  5. For a posterior over N players’ skills, what shape are theta and the momentum drawn in each HMC iteration?
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 5, Why MCMC and the remark “a rejection is still a sample” in The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm.

  1. The state is appended before each update, so the list records the state at the start of each iteration. A rejection leaves \theta unchanged, so the next append repeats it: [\,0,\ 0.8,\ 0.8,\ 1.1,\ 1.1,\ 1.1\,]. The final value 0.9 is reached after the last append and is not stored. It would be the first entry of a seventh iteration.
  2. Three of six proposals were accepted: 3/6=0.5.
  3. With repeats, the mean is 4.9/6\approx0.817. Dropping repeats leaves [0,0.8,1.1] with mean 0.633. The first is the MCMC estimate. Staying at 1.1 for three iterations is how the chain says that 1.1 has more posterior mass nearby than the rejected proposals; the stay-put probability is part of the transition rule whose stationary distribution is the target, as the detailed-balance proof shows.
  4. The chain was started at an arbitrary point, 0, not at a draw from the posterior. Early states reflect the starting point more than the target. Discarding them is the burn-in. With only six samples nothing here can be called converged; the arithmetic is for bookkeeping only.
  5. Both have one entry per player: shape (N,). HMC samples all coordinates jointly, and the momentum always has the same dimension as the position.

Answer. (1) [0,0.8,0.8,1.1,1.1,1.1]; 0.9 is not stored. (2) 0.5. (3) \approx0.817 with repeats (correct) versus 0.633 without. (4) It is the arbitrary start; dropping early values is burn-in. (5) Both shape (N,).

Problem 13: From a sample matrix to a ranking

After burn-in, a sampler has produced six draws of four players’ skills, stored as an array S of shape (6, 4): one row per sample, one column per player.

sample player 0 player 1 player 2 player 3
1 0.9 −0.4 1.6 0.2
2 1.1 −0.2 1.2 0.5
3 0.7 −0.6 1.5 0.9
4 1.3 0.1 1.0 0.4
5 0.8 −0.5 1.4 1.0
6 1.0 −0.3 1.1 0.6
  1. Estimate each player’s posterior mean and standard deviation. Along which axis of S do the averages run?
  2. Rank the players from highest to lowest mean skill.
  3. Estimate \mathbb P(z_3\ge z_0\mid\text{games}).
  4. These draws come from a Markov chain. Why is the estimate in part 3 less precise than p(1-p)/R suggests?
TipWorked solution

This uses the Monte Carlo average of Week 5, Why MCMC and Effective sample size.

  1. Each column is a sample from one player’s marginal posterior, so the averages run down the rows (axis 0), giving one number per player. For player 0, the mean is 5.8/6=0.967; the squared deviations sum to 0.2333, so dividing by 6 and taking the square root gives a standard deviation of 0.197. The same steps for every column:

    player 0 player 1 player 2 player 3
    mean 0.967 −0.317 1.300 0.600
    std 0.197 0.227 0.216 0.277

    (Dividing by 5 instead of 6 gives slightly larger values; with thousands of samples the difference vanishes.)

  2. Sorting by mean: player 2, player 0, player 3, player 1. In code this is an argsort of the mean vector, reversed for highest first; the result is a list of player indices, not of skills.

  3. The probability is the expectation of an indicator. Row by row, z_3\ge z_0 holds in samples 3 and 5 only, so the estimate is 2/6\approx0.333. The comparison is made within each row, because each row is one joint draw. Comparing the two players’ means alone would only say player 0 is higher on average, not how often.

  4. The formula p(1-p)/R assumes independent draws. Consecutive MCMC draws are positively correlated, so R draws carry the information of only n_{\text{eff}}<R independent ones, and the variance is closer to p(1-p)/n_{\text{eff}}.

Answer. (1) Means (0.967,\ -0.317,\ 1.300,\ 0.600), standard deviations (0.197,\ 0.227,\ 0.216,\ 0.277), averaging over axis 0. (2) 2,0,3,1. (3) 1/3. (4) Correlated samples reduce the effective sample size.

Problem 14: Step size, trajectory length, and what samples are worth

A posterior has standard deviation about 1 in each coordinate. Three HMC settings are tried:

setting \epsilon L
A 0.02 10
B 0.1 15
C 1.5 1
  1. For each setting, how far can one trajectory move (in units of the posterior’s standard deviation), and how many gradient evaluations does each iteration cost?
  2. Which setting behaves most like a random walk, and which is most likely to have a low acceptance rate? Explain.
  3. A run of 4000 samples has lag-one autocorrelation 0.95. Assuming \rho_t=\rho^t as in the week’s example, estimate n_{\text{eff}}. Repeat for \rho=0.5.
  4. Four chains of setting A all pass \hat R\approx1. Is that enough to trust them?
TipWorked solution

This uses the two knobs of Week 5, The leapfrog integrator and the HMC algorithm, Effective sample size, and Diagnostics rule of thumb.

  1. The trajectory length is L\epsilon: A 0.2, B 1.5, C 1.5. Each leapfrog step evaluates the gradient once, so A costs about 10 gradients per iteration, B 15, C 1.
  2. A behaves most like a random walk. Each proposal moves at most about 0.2 standard deviations, so the chain needs many iterations to cross the posterior, consecutive samples are strongly correlated, and the acceptance rate is high for the wrong reason, as with the tutorial’s tiny-step chain. C is most likely to have a low acceptance rate: a single step of size 1.5 is large compared with the posterior’s width, the integration error in H is large, and e^{H_{\text{start}}-H_{\text{end}}} is often small. B covers the same distance as C in small steps, so it travels far while keeping H nearly constant.
  3. The factor is \frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}. For \rho=0.95 it is 1.95/0.05=39, so n_{\text{eff}}\approx4000/39\approx103. For \rho=0.5 it is 3, so n_{\text{eff}}\approx1333. The same number of iterations is worth about thirteen times more in the second case.
  4. Not on its own. The rule of thumb asks for \hat R near 1 and \hat n_{\text{eff}} above 10 per chain, and even passing both shows only that the chains appear mixed. Slow chains started near each other can agree while all of them have explored only a small part of the posterior.

How the specific settings in a homework behave on its data is left to the homework.

Answer. (1) L\epsilon=0.2,\ 1.5,\ 1.5; costs 10, 15, 1 gradients. (2) A is random-walk-like; C risks low acceptance. (3) n_{\text{eff}}\approx103 and \approx1333. (4) No: also check \hat n_{\text{eff}}, and passing shows apparent mixing only.
