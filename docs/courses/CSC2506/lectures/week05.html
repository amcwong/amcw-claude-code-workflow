Week 5: Sampling with MCMC

Week 5: Sampling with MCMC

Overview. Week 4 ended with two ways to use a distribution p(x)=\tilde p(x)/Z that we can evaluate only up to its normalizing constant: importance sampling and rejection sampling. Both draw from one fixed proposal q(x), and both work only when q already resembles p, which is hopeless in high dimensions. This lecture gives up on independent samples. Instead we build a random sequence x^{(1)},x^{(2)},\dots in which each sample depends on the one before, arranged so that the sequence spends time in each region in proportion to p. The first half develops the needed theory of Markov chains: transition matrices, the stationary distribution a chain settles into, and the detailed balance condition that certifies it. The second half uses that theory to build Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) samplers: Metropolis-Hastings, Gibbs sampling, and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo. It ends with diagnostics for judging whether a run has gone on long enough.

From independent to dependent samples

Every sampler in Week 4 produced i.i.d. samples: T draws x_{1:T}=\{x_1,\dots,x_T\} with each x_t\sim p(x) independently of the others. This week the samples will be dependent.

Dependence is not only a feature of samplers. It also comes up when we model data. We usually assume data points are i.i.d., but for sequential data that is a poor assumption:

  • time series, such as stock prices, speech, or video, where each value is measured at successive times;
  • ordered data, such as text or gene sequences, where position matters even without a clock.

Week 2’s chain rule (Why factor the joint in Week 2) factorizes any joint distribution over a sequence without assumptions:

p(x_{1:T}) = \prod_{t=1}^T p(x_t\mid x_{t-1},\dots,x_1), \qquad p(x_1\mid x_0) := p(x_1).

The factor for x_t conditions on all t-1 earlier values. If each x_t takes K values, specifying that factor as a table needs on the order of K^{t} numbers, so the factorization is exact but quickly intractable. The same lesson as Week 2 applies: to get a usable model, we must assume some conditional independence.

Markov chains

What is the simplest assumption that makes the chain rule manageable? Let each value depend on the past only through the most recent value.

Definition. A sequence x_1,x_2,\dots is a (first-order) Markov chain if p(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1}) = p(x_t\mid x_{t-1}) \qquad\text{for all } t. Each x_t is called the state of the chain at time t.

Under this assumption every chain-rule factor shrinks to a one-step conditional, and the joint becomes

p(x_{1:T}) = \prod_{t=1}^T p(x_t\mid x_{t-1}).

This is the first-order chain that Week 2 listed among DGMs in the wild (DGMs in the wild, briefly in Week 2): as a DAG, it is a single path x_1\to x_2\to\cdots\to x_T.

A further assumption is often useful: that the rule for moving from one state to the next does not change over time.

Definition. A Markov chain is stationary (also called homogeneous) if its transition probabilities do not depend on t: p(x_{t+1}=y\mid x_t=x) = p(x_{t+2}=y\mid x_{t+1}=x)\qquad\text{for all } t,\,x,\,y. If the transition probabilities depend on t, the chain is non-stationary.

The lecture considers only stationary chains from here on. The word “stationary” is overloaded in this chapter. Here it describes the transition rule, which stays fixed over time. In The stationary distribution it describes a distribution over states that stays fixed over time. A stationary (homogeneous) chain usually does not have a fixed distribution over states at early times; whether it settles into one is exactly the question of The stationary distribution.

Higher-order chains. Sometimes one step of memory is too little. Natural language is the lecture’s example: a word can depend on words several positions back. The first-order assumption generalizes directly:

  • second order: p(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1}) = p(x_t\mid x_{t-1},x_{t-2});
  • m-th order: p(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1}) = p(x_t\mid x_{t-1:t-m}).

The rest of the lecture uses first-order chains.

Transition matrices and state transition diagrams

When the state is discrete, say x_t\in\{1,\dots,K\}, the one-step conditional is a table with one row per current state and one column per next state.

Definition. The transition matrix (or stochastic matrix) of a stationary Markov chain on K states is A\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K} with A_{ij} = p(x_t = j\mid x_{t-1}=i), the probability of moving from state i to state j in one step.

Row i of A is the distribution of the next state given that the current state is i, so every entry is nonnegative and each row sums to one: \sum_j A_{ij}=1. Columns need not sum to one.

The matrix also tells us how the probability of each state evolves. Summing over where the chain was one step earlier,

p(x_t=j) = \sum_i p(x_t=j\mid x_{t-1}=i)\,p(x_{t-1}=i) = \sum_i A_{ij}\,p(x_{t-1}=i).

In words: the probability of being in j now is the total probability flowing into j from every state i, each weighted by how likely the chain was to be in i.

Definition. A state transition diagram draws a Markov chain as a directed graph: one node per state, and an arrow from i to j labelled A_{ij} whenever A_{ij}>0 (a legal transition).

This graph is not a graphical model in the Week 2 sense. Its nodes are the possible values of a single variable, not different random variables.

Figure 1. The lecture’s two-state chain. Each arrow carries its transition probability, and the arrows leaving each state sum to 1.

The transition matrix of the chain in Figure 1 is

A = \begin{pmatrix} 1-\alpha & \alpha \\ \beta & 1-\beta\end{pmatrix}.

Row 1 says: from state 1, move to state 2 with probability \alpha, otherwise stay. Row 2 says: from state 2, move to state 1 with probability \beta, otherwise stay. We reuse this chain as the running example for the whole first half, sometimes with the numbers \alpha=0.1, \beta=0.3, which give

A = \begin{pmatrix} 0.9 & 0.1 \\ 0.3 & 0.7\end{pmatrix}.

In this chapter \alpha and \beta are always the two transition probabilities of this chain. (From The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm on, the same letter \alpha is also used for an acceptance probability; we will say so each time.)

n-step transitions and the Chapman-Kolmogorov equations

A answers “where can I be after one step?” What about after n steps?

Definition. The n-step transition matrix A(n) has entries A_{ij}(n) = p(x_{t+n}=j\mid x_t=i), the probability of getting from i to j in exactly n steps. In particular A(1)=A.

To get from i to j in m+n steps, the chain must be in some state k after the first m steps. Summing over that intermediate state gives the Chapman-Kolmogorov equations:

A_{ij}(m+n) = \sum_{k=1}^K A_{ik}(m)\,A_{kj}(n), \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad A(m+n) = A(m)\,A(n).

The sum over k is exactly a matrix product: row i of A(m) times column j of A(n). Applying the equation repeatedly with m=1,

A(n) = A\,A(n-1) = A\,A\,A(n-2) = \cdots = A^n.

So the n-step transition matrix is just the n-th power of the one-step matrix.

Note

Worked example: two steps on the two-state chain. With \alpha=0.1, \beta=0.3, A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0.9 & 0.1 \\ 0.3 & 0.7\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0.9 & 0.1 \\ 0.3 & 0.7\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.84 & 0.16 \\ 0.48 & 0.52\end{pmatrix}. Check the top-right entry by the sum over intermediate states: from 1 to 2 in two steps, either stay at 1 then move (0.9\times0.1) or move to 2 then stay (0.1\times0.7), for a total of 0.09+0.07=0.16. Each row of A^2 still sums to 1, as it must.

Application: Markov language models

A Markov chain can serve as a language model, a probability distribution over sequences of words. The state space is the set of all words (the vocabulary, of size K), and x_t is the t-th word of a sentence. A first-order model predicts each word from the previous word alone, so it is built from word pairs, or bigrams.

The model has two sets of parameters, \theta=(\pi,A):

  • \pi_j, the probability that a sentence starts with word j;
  • A_{jk}, the probability that word k follows word j.

Here \pi is the distribution of the first state. From The stationary distribution on, \pi also names the stationary distribution, which is a different object; the slides use the same letter for both.

Figure 2. The lecture’s word-level state transition diagram. The slide prints the fractions as decimals (0.66, 0.33, 1.0).

The likelihood of one sentence. Writing A(x_{t-1},x_t) for A_{x_{t-1}x_t}, the probability of a sentence x_{1:T} is the first-word probability times one transition per later word:

p(x_{1:T}\mid\theta) = \pi(x_1)\,A(x_1,x_2)\cdots A(x_{T-1},x_T).

To prepare for maximum likelihood, the lecture rewrites this product with indicator functions \mathbf{1}[\cdot] (1 when the condition holds, 0 otherwise), so that every parameter appears explicitly:

p(x_{1:T}\mid\theta) = \prod_{j=1}^K \pi_j^{\mathbf{1}[x_1=j]}\ \prod_{t=2}^T\prod_{j=1}^K\prod_{k=1}^K A_{jk}^{\mathbf{1}[x_{t-1}=j,\ x_t=k]}.

Every factor whose indicator is 0 equals 1, so the only factors that survive are \pi of the actual first word and A of each actual consecutive pair. It is the same product as before.

Maximum likelihood from a corpus. Given a data set \mathcal{D}=\{x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(N)}\} of N sentences, sentence i having length T_i, treated as independent, the log-likelihood is a sum of logs of the factors above. Collecting equal terms gives

\log p(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^N\log p(x^{(i)}\mid\theta) = \sum_j N^1_j\log\pi_j + \sum_j\sum_k N_{jk}\log A_{jk},

with the counts

N^1_j = \sum_{i=1}^N\mathbf{1}\big[x^{(i)}_1=j\big], \qquad N_{jk} = \sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{t=1}^{T_i-1}\mathbf{1}\big[x^{(i)}_t=j,\ x^{(i)}_{t+1}=k\big].

N^1_j counts the sentences that start with word j; N_{jk} counts how often word k immediately follows word j anywhere in the corpus. These counts are the sufficient statistics (Sufficient statistics in Week 1).

The log-likelihood splits into separate pieces: one for \pi, and one for each row A_{j\cdot}. Each piece has the form \sum_k N_k\log q_k subject to \sum_k q_k=1, which is exactly the Multinomial MLE problem of Week 1 (Multinomial as an exponential family in Week 1), whose answer is “count divided by total”. So

\hat\pi_j = \frac{N^1_j}{\sum_{j'}N^1_{j'}}, \qquad \hat A_{jk} = \frac{N_{jk}}{\sum_{k'}N_{jk'}}.

The estimated probability that k follows j is the fraction of times, among all occurrences of j followed by something, that the something was k. This is the “natural form” the lecture refers to.

Note

Worked example: recovering Figure 2 from counts. The slide does not say which corpus produced Figure 2, but one corpus consistent with it is the three sentences

“I like rabbits”, “I like turtles”, “I don’t like snails”.

Every sentence starts with “I”, so N^1_{\text{I}}=3 and \hat\pi_{\text{I}}=1. The bigram counts are

  • after “I”: like 2 times, don’t 1 time, so \hat A_{\text{I},\text{like}}=2/3 and \hat A_{\text{I},\text{don't}}=1/3;
  • after “don’t”: like 1 time, so \hat A_{\text{don't},\text{like}}=1;
  • after “like”: rabbits, turtles, snails once each, so each gets 1/3.

These are the arrows of Figure 2. The fitted model then scores sentences, for example p(\text{I don't like snails}) = \hat\pi_{\text{I}}\cdot\tfrac13\cdot1\cdot\tfrac13 = \tfrac19.

The stationary distribution

Run a chain for a long time. Does the probability of being in each state settle down, and if so, to what?

Let \pi_t(j)=p(x_t=j) be the probability of being in state j at time t, and \pi_0\in\mathbb{R}^K the initial distribution. The update from Transition matrices and state transition diagrams reads

\pi_1(j) = \sum_{i=1}^K p(x_1=j\mid x_0=i)\,\pi_0(i) = \sum_{i=1}^K A_{ij}\,\pi_0(i) = \sum_{i=1}^K (A^\top)_{ji}\,\pi_0(i).

Treating each \pi_t as a column vector, this is \pi_1 = A^\top\pi_0, and repeating it,

\pi_t = A^\top\pi_{t-1} = A^\top A^\top\pi_{t-2} = \cdots = (A^\top)^t\pi_0.

The transpose appears because A is indexed (from, to) and we are summing over the “from” index. If, as t grows, \pi_t converges to some \pi, then one more step cannot change it.

Definition. A distribution \pi over the states is a stationary distribution (also called an invariant distribution) of the chain with transition matrix A if \pi = A^\top\pi, \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad \pi_j = \sum_i \pi_i A_{ij}\ \text{ for every } j.

If the chain’s state is distributed according to \pi at some time, it stays distributed according to \pi forever after. (As noted in Markov chains, this is the “distribution over states” meaning of stationary, not the “fixed transition rule” meaning.)

Finding it with linear algebra. The definition says \pi is an eigenvector of A^\top with eigenvalue 1. So we solve

A^\top v = v

and set \pi\propto v, rescaling so the entries sum to 1 (an eigenvector is only determined up to a constant factor, so this normalization step is required). Here v is an eigenvector, unrelated to the momentum v that appears in Hamiltonian Monte Carlo.

Why is 1 always an eigenvalue of A^\top? Because each row of A sums to 1, A\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}, where \mathbf{1} is the all-ones vector. So 1 is an eigenvalue of A, and a matrix and its transpose have the same eigenvalues. An eigenvector v for eigenvalue 1 therefore exists. (That a nonnegative one exists, so that it can be rescaled into a distribution, is a further fact about stochastic matrices that the lecture uses without proof.)

The lecture adds a warning: the stationary distribution may not be unique. When is the stationary distribution unique? says when it is.

Note

Worked example: the two-state chain. With \alpha=0.1, \beta=0.3, A^\top = \begin{pmatrix} 0.9 & 0.3 \\ 0.1 & 0.7\end{pmatrix}. The vector v=(3,1)^\top satisfies A^\top v=v: 0.9\cdot3+0.3\cdot1=3 and 0.1\cdot3+0.7\cdot1=1. Normalizing, \pi=(0.75,\ 0.25).

For general \alpha,\beta>0, the second equation of A^\top\pi=\pi reads \alpha\pi_1+(1-\beta)\pi_2=\pi_2, that is \alpha\pi_1=\beta\pi_2. Together with \pi_1+\pi_2=1, \pi = \Big(\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta},\ \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\Big). The chain spends more time in the state that is harder to leave: state 1 is left with probability \alpha=0.1 per step, state 2 with probability \beta=0.3, so state 1 gets three times the long-run probability.

When is the stationary distribution unique?

This subsection adds a result from the assigned reading (MLPP 17.2.3) that the slides set up but do not state. The slides define two properties of a chain without saying what they are for:

Definition. A Markov chain is irreducible if every state can be reached from every other state (in some number of steps). It is regular if some power A^n has all entries strictly positive, that is, for some single n the chain can go from any state to any state in exactly n steps.

Regular implies irreducible, but not the other way round. The payoff is the following theorem, stated in MLPP for finite state spaces.

Theorem (MLPP §17.2.3). A regular Markov chain on finitely many states has a unique stationary distribution \pi, and the chain converges to it from any starting distribution: \pi_t\to\pi as t\to\infty, whatever \pi_0 is.

The two-state chain shows what goes wrong without each property.

  • Not irreducible: \alpha=\beta=0, so A=I. The chain never moves, and every distribution satisfies \pi=A^\top\pi. Stationary distributions exist but are not unique.
  • Irreducible but not regular: \alpha=\beta=1, so the chain swaps state at every step. Its only stationary distribution is (0.5,0.5), but the powers of A alternate between the swap matrix and the identity, so no power is all-positive. Started in state 1, the distribution of x_t alternates (1,0),(0,1),(1,0),\dots and never converges.
  • Regular: \alpha=0.1, \beta=0.3. Every entry of A is already positive. Starting from \pi_0=(1,0), we get \pi_1=(0.9,0.1), \pi_2=(0.84,0.16), \pi_3=(0.804,0.196). The first entry approaches its stationary value 0.75, with gaps 0.25,\ 0.15,\ 0.09,\ 0.054 at t=0,1,2,3, shrinking by a factor 0.6=1-\alpha-\beta each step.

For MCMC this is the property we will want: a chain that forgets where it started and settles into one target distribution. For continuous state spaces the precise conditions are more technical (MLPP states them under the name ergodicity); the lecture and these notes do not go into them.

Detailed balance

Solving A^\top\pi=\pi requires knowing all of A and doing linear algebra. MCMC needs the reverse direction: a target \pi is given, and we want to design A so that \pi is stationary. A simpler, local condition does the job.

Definition. A Markov chain with transition matrix A is time reversible if there is a distribution \pi such that \pi_i A_{ij} = \pi_j A_{ji} \qquad\text{for all } i,j. These are the detailed balance equations, and we say the chain satisfies detailed balance with respect to \pi.

The left side is the probability of being in i and then moving to j; the right side is the probability of being in j and then moving to i. Detailed balance says that, in equilibrium, the flow along every edge is the same in both directions.

Theorem. If a Markov chain with transition matrix A satisfies detailed balance with respect to a distribution \pi, then \pi is a stationary distribution of the chain.

Proof. We need A^\top\pi=\pi, that is, \sum_i\pi_iA_{ij}=\pi_j for every j. Use detailed balance on each term, then the fact that row j of A sums to 1:

\sum_{i=1}^K\pi_iA_{ij} \overset{\text{(DB)}}{=} \sum_{i=1}^K\pi_jA_{ji} = \pi_j\sum_{i=1}^K A_{ji} = \pi_j.

The converse is false: a chain can have a stationary distribution without satisfying detailed balance. Detailed balance is a sufficient condition, chosen because it is easy to check one pair of states at a time. It also says nothing about uniqueness or convergence; for those we still need the conditions of When is the stationary distribution unique?. The swapping chain with \alpha=\beta=1 satisfies detailed balance with respect to (0.5,0.5), since 0.5\cdot1=0.5\cdot1, and still never converges.

Note

Worked example: detailed balance on the two-state chain. There is only one pair of distinct states, so detailed balance is the single equation \pi_1\alpha=\pi_2\beta. That is exactly the equation we solved in The stationary distribution. With \alpha=0.1, \beta=0.3 and \pi=(0.75,0.25): 0.75\times0.1 = 0.075 = 0.25\times0.3. Every two-state chain with \alpha,\beta>0 is therefore time reversible.

Why MCMC

We now return to sampling. The goal is the same as in Week 4 (What sampling is for in Week 4): draw samples from p(x)=\tilde p(x)/Z, or estimate \mathbb{E}_p[\phi(x)], when we can evaluate \tilde p but not Z.

The trouble with a global proposal. Importance sampling and rejection sampling (Importance sampling and Rejection sampling in Week 4) work only if the proposal q(x) is similar to p(x), and in high dimensions such a q is hard to find. The tutorial puts a number on this. Take p=\mathcal N(0,I_d) and a proposal that is only slightly too wide, q=\mathcal N(0,1.1^2I_d). Rejection sampling then accepts a fraction 1.1^{-d} of its proposals, about 10^{-4} at d=100. For a real posterior we do not even know in advance where the mass is.

A local proposal instead. The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm uses a proposal density that depends on the current state x^{(t)}.

Definition. In MCMC, a proposal density q(x'\mid x^{(t)}) is a conditional distribution, centred or otherwise depending on the current state x^{(t)}, from which we can draw a candidate next state x'.

This is a stricter sense of “proposal” than in Week 4, where q(x) was a single fixed distribution. A typical choice is a Gaussian centred at the current state, q(x'\mid x^{(t)})=\mathcal N(x';\,x^{(t)},\sigma^2I), but any density we can sample from will do. Unlike importance and rejection sampling, q(x'\mid x^{(t)}) need not look like p(x) at all. It only has to propose nearby moves; the algorithm decides which moves to keep.

Figure 3. After the lecture’s picture of local proposals. The target \tilde p (solid) is wide and has two peaks; the proposal (dashed) is a narrow bump that moves with the current state. Neither bump resembles \tilde p, and neither needs to.

Definition. Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods generate a sequence x^{(1)},x^{(2)},\dots in which each sample x^{(t)} is drawn from a distribution that depends on the previous value x^{(t-1)}, designed so that the sequence is a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is the target p.

Here the superscript (t) is the iteration number and x^{(t)} is the whole state (possibly a vector); in Markov chains we wrote the same thing as x_t. From Gibbs sampling on, a subscript x_j will mean the j-th coordinate of the state instead.

Three consequences follow from building samples this way.

  • The samples are dependent. Rejection sampling’s accepted points are independent; an MCMC chain’s are not.
  • The chain needs time. It starts wherever we put it, and only approaches p as it runs (When is the stationary distribution unique?). MCMC methods must therefore run for a while before their samples are (approximately) from p, and it is a good idea to throw away a number of initial samples. That discarded stretch is called the burn-in.
  • Monte Carlo estimation still works. For large enough T, the average of a test function over the chain estimates its expectation, \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}[\phi(x)] \approx \frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T\phi(x^{(t)}). (The slide writes the test function as f; it is Week 4’s \phi.) This is the simple Monte Carlo estimator of Simple Monte Carlo in Week 4 applied to dependent samples. It is no longer the case that its variance is \mathrm{var}[\phi]/T; Effective sample size measures how much the dependence costs.

The lake picture from Week 4 (Why the normalizing constant is hard in Week 4) fits this exactly. We can measure the depth \tilde p wherever we steer the boat, but never the volume Z of the whole lake. An MCMC sampler steers toward deep water and lingers there, without ever surveying the whole lake.

Note

Homework-relevant. Assignment 2’s TrueSkill question (Latent variables and the TrueSkill model in Week 4) approximates the posterior over player skills by MCMC and then summarizes it with sample averages: posterior means, standard deviations, and probabilities written as expectations of indicators (What sampling is for in Week 4). The ideas to bring to it are the ones above: the chain targets a posterior known only up to Z, early samples are not yet from that posterior, and every summary is an average over the chain. The specific estimates are left to the assignment.

The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm

How do we turn a local proposal into a chain whose stationary distribution is p? Propose a move, then accept or reject it with a carefully chosen probability.

Metropolis-Hastings (MH)

Given the current state x^{(t)}:

  1. Draw a candidate x'\sim q(x'\mid x^{(t)}).
  2. Compute the acceptance probability \alpha(x'\mid x^{(t)}) = \min\Bigg\{1,\ \frac{\tilde p(x')\,q(x^{(t)}\mid x')}{\tilde p(x^{(t)})\,q(x'\mid x^{(t)})}\Bigg\}.
  3. With probability \alpha(x'\mid x^{(t)}) accept: set x^{(t+1)}=x'. Otherwise reject: set x^{(t+1)}=x^{(t)}.

In this section \alpha is the acceptance probability, not the transition probability of the two-state chain.

Reading the ratio. It has two factors.

  • \tilde p(x')/\tilde p(x^{(t)}) compares the target at the candidate and the current state. A move to a more probable point is always accepted (if the other factor is 1); a move to a less probable point is accepted with probability equal to the ratio. Sometimes moving “downhill” is what makes this a sampler rather than an optimizer: a chain that only moved uphill would get stuck at a mode.
  • q(x^{(t)}\mid x')/q(x'\mid x^{(t)}) corrects for a proposal that favours some directions. If q tends to propose moves toward x' more readily than back, the correction lowers the acceptance to compensate.

Z cancels. Only the ratio \tilde p(x')/\tilde p(x^{(t)}) appears, and p(x')/p(x^{(t)})=\tilde p(x')/\tilde p(x^{(t)}) because the two factors of 1/Z cancel. So MH needs to evaluate only \tilde p, never Z.

A rejection is still a sample. When a proposal is rejected, the chain stays put and the current state is recorded again as the next sample. The tutorial notes that dropping these repeats is the most common implementation bug; the repeated states are part of the chain, and the proof in Why Metropolis-Hastings works depends on them.

Definition. When the proposal is symmetric, q(x'\mid x)=q(x\mid x') for all x,x', the correction factor is 1 and the acceptance probability simplifies to \min\{1,\ \tilde p(x')/\tilde p(x^{(t)})\}. This special case is the Metropolis algorithm. A Gaussian centred at the current state is symmetric, so a Gaussian random walk is a Metropolis sampler.

The lecture states the key property as a theorem: the MH procedure defines a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is the target p(x). Why Metropolis-Hastings works proves it.

Tutorial example: Clemente’s batting average

The tutorial runs MH on a one-dimensional posterior where the answer is also available in closed form, so the sampler can be checked. Roberto Clemente started the 1970 baseball season with H=18 hits in N=45 at-bats, a .400 average. Let \theta\in(0,1) be his true (unknown) probability of a hit. The data D=(N,H) is fixed; the quantity we sample is \theta. In the slides’ notation, the tutorial’s \theta plays the role of x.

Two distributions build the model.

Definition. The Binomial distribution \mathrm{Binomial}(N,\theta) is the distribution of the number of successes H in N independent Bernoulli(\theta) trials, p(H\mid\theta)=\binom{N}{H}\theta^H(1-\theta)^{N-H}. The Beta distribution \mathrm{Beta}(a,b) with a,b>0 is the distribution on (0,1) with density proportional to \theta^{a-1}(1-\theta)^{b-1}. \mathrm{Beta}(1,1) is the uniform distribution on (0,1).

With likelihood H\mid\theta\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(45,\theta) and the uniform prior \theta\sim\mathrm{Beta}(1,1), Bayes’ rule gives

p(\theta\mid D) = \frac{\tilde p(\theta)}{Z}, \qquad \tilde p(\theta) = p(D\mid\theta)\,p(\theta) \propto \theta^{18}(1-\theta)^{27},

where Z=p(D)=\int p(D\mid\theta)p(\theta)\,d\theta is the normalizer we pretend not to know. In this particular model the posterior happens to be another Beta distribution, \mathrm{Beta}(1+18,\ 1+27)=\mathrm{Beta}(19,28), with mean 19/47\approx0.404, because a Beta prior is conjugate to a Binomial likelihood. The closed form is used only to check the sampler.

Note

Worked example: one acceptance decision. The tutorial uses a Gaussian random walk, \theta'=\theta+0.2\,\varepsilon with \varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,1), which is symmetric, so the Metropolis rule applies. Suppose the chain is at \theta=0.40 and proposes \theta'=0.30. The ratio is \frac{\tilde p(0.30)}{\tilde p(0.40)} = \Big(\frac{0.30}{0.40}\Big)^{18}\Big(\frac{0.70}{0.60}\Big)^{27} \approx \frac{1}{2.76} \approx 0.36. The constant \binom{45}{18} cancelled, along with Z. So the move is accepted with probability about 0.36. The reverse move, from 0.30 to 0.40, has ratio 2.76>1 and is always accepted. Over many steps, the chain visits \theta=0.40 about 2.76 times as often as \theta=0.30 (per unit of \theta), which is exactly the ratio of the posterior densities.

A proposal outside (0,1) has \tilde p(\theta')=0, so its acceptance probability is 0 and it is always rejected.

Started deliberately far away at \theta=0.9 and run for 5000 steps with the first 200 discarded as burn-in, the tutorial’s chain accepts 39% of its proposals. The histogram of its samples matches the \mathrm{Beta}(19,28) density, and the sample mean is 0.403 against the exact 0.404.

The step size matters. The tutorial reruns the chain with the random-walk step size \sigma changed:

  • \sigma=0.03 (too small): almost every proposal is accepted, but each move is tiny. The chain spends most of the burn-in just reaching the bulk of the posterior, then wanders slowly; consecutive samples have correlation about 0.9.
  • \sigma=2.0 (too large): most proposals land outside (0,1) or deep in the tails and are rejected, so the chain sits still for long stretches. Consecutive samples are again nearly identical.
  • \sigma=0.2: in between. The tutorial suggests that for a Gaussian random walk, an acceptance rate of roughly 25–40% is a good sign.

So a high acceptance rate is not the goal in itself. What we want is a chain whose consecutive states are as uncorrelated as possible, because many nearly identical samples carry the information of far fewer independent ones. Effective sample size turns this into a number.

Why Metropolis-Hastings works

We prove that the MH chain satisfies detailed balance with respect to p, which by the theorem of Detailed balance makes p stationary. The lecture writes the proof for a discrete state space with sums; the continuous case is the same with sums replaced by integrals.

First rewrite the acceptance probability with p in place of \tilde p (the Z’s cancel):

\alpha(x'\mid x) = \min\Big\{1,\ \frac{p(x')\,q(x\mid x')}{p(x)\,q(x'\mid x)}\Big\}.

The transition probabilities. From state x, the chain moves to a different state x' only if x' is proposed and accepted. It ends up at x again either by proposing x itself or by proposing something else and rejecting it:

r(x'\mid x) = q(x'\mid x)\,\alpha(x'\mid x) \qquad\text{if } x'\neq x, r(x\mid x) = q(x\mid x) + \sum_{x''\neq x} q(x''\mid x)\big(1-\alpha(x''\mid x)\big).

The second line is the “stay-put” probability, the reason rejected proposals must be recorded as repeats.

Detailed balance. We need r(x'\mid x)\,p(x) = r(x\mid x')\,p(x') for all x,x'. If x'=x the two sides are identical, so take x'\neq x. Moving p(x) inside the minimum,

r(x'\mid x)\,p(x) = p(x)\,q(x'\mid x)\,\min\Big\{1,\ \frac{p(x')q(x\mid x')}{p(x)q(x'\mid x)}\Big\} = \min\big\{p(x)\,q(x'\mid x),\ p(x')\,q(x\mid x')\big\}.

Doing the same with the roles of x and x' swapped,

r(x\mid x')\,p(x') = p(x')\,q(x\mid x')\,\min\Big\{1,\ \frac{p(x)q(x'\mid x)}{p(x')q(x\mid x')}\Big\} = \min\big\{p(x')\,q(x\mid x'),\ p(x)\,q(x'\mid x)\big\}.

The two minima contain the same two numbers, so they are equal. The MH chain satisfies detailed balance with respect to p, so p is a stationary distribution of the chain. The acceptance rule is shaped exactly so that the flow from x to x', \min\{p(x)q(x'\mid x),\,p(x')q(x\mid x')\}, is symmetric in x and x'.

As Detailed balance warned, detailed balance gives stationarity, not convergence. Whether the chain actually reaches p from its starting point, and how fast, depends on the proposal; the step-size experiments above and the diagnostics of MCMC diagnostics are the practical answer.

Gibbs sampling

Metropolis-Hastings needs a proposal. If the target is a distribution over a vector, there is a natural proposal that never needs to be rejected: resample one coordinate at a time from its exact conditional distribution.

Divide the state into d components, x=(x_1,\dots,x_d). Here the subscript j indexes a coordinate of the state, and the superscript (t) the iteration.

Gibbs sampling

Start with any x^{(0)}=(x^{(0)}_1,\dots,x^{(0)}_d). In iteration t:

For j=1,\dots,d, sample x^{(t)}_j \sim p\big(x_j\mid x^{(t-1)}_{-j}\big), where x^{(t-1)}_{-j} means all components except x_j, at their current values: x^{(t-1)}_{-j} = \big(x^{(t)}_1,\dots,x^{(t)}_{j-1},\ x^{(t-1)}_{j+1},\dots,x^{(t-1)}_d\big).

The notation needs a careful read. Despite the superscript (t-1) on x_{-j}, the components before j have already been updated in this iteration and are used at their new values x^{(t)}; only the components after j still hold last iteration’s values. Each coordinate is drawn conditioned on the most recent value of every other coordinate.

Definition. The full conditional of coordinate x_j is p(x_j\mid x_{-j}), its distribution given all other coordinates.

There is no accept/reject step: every draw is accepted. Gibbs sampling is useful exactly when the joint p(x) is hard to sample but every full conditional is easy. The lecture’s example of this is the Ising model (The Ising model in Week 3). In an MRF, the full conditional of one node depends only on its neighbours, so for a \pm1 spin it is a single coin flip whose probability is computed from a few neighbouring values. The partition function Z never appears.

A note from the reading (MLPP 24.3.2): Gibbs sampling is a special case of Metropolis-Hastings. Updating coordinate j is an MH step with proposal q(x'\mid x)=p(x'_j\mid x_{-j}) that leaves the other coordinates unchanged. Plugging this into the MH acceptance ratio, every factor cancels and the ratio is exactly 1, which is why every Gibbs draw is accepted. In particular, each Gibbs update satisfies detailed balance with respect to p by Why Metropolis-Hastings works.

The conditional distribution of a bivariate Gaussian

Inserted prerequisite. The lecture’s Gibbs example relies on the conditional distributions of a two-dimensional Gaussian, which the slides describe as seen in a previous lecture but which have not appeared in these notes. Here is the case the example needs.

Let X=(X_1,X_2)^\top follow a bivariate Gaussian with mean \mu=(\mu_1,\mu_2)^\top and covariance

\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho\\ \rho & 1\end{pmatrix},

so each coordinate has variance 1 and the two have correlation \rho\in(-1,1). Then

X_1\mid X_2=x_2 \ \sim\ \mathcal N\big(\mu_1+\rho(x_2-\mu_2),\ 1-\rho^2\big), X_2\mid X_1=x_1 \ \sim\ \mathcal N\big(\mu_2+\rho(x_1-\mu_1),\ 1-\rho^2\big).

Knowing x_2 shifts the mean of X_1 by \rho times how far x_2 is from its own mean, and shrinks its variance from 1 to 1-\rho^2. The stronger the correlation, the more x_2 pins down x_1.

Where this comes from. Take \mu=0 for brevity (a nonzero mean just shifts both coordinates). The precision matrix is \Sigma^{-1}=\frac{1}{1-\rho^2}\begin{pmatrix}1&-\rho\\-\rho&1\end{pmatrix} (Week 3 called it K, Gaussian graphical models in Week 3), so the joint density is

p(x_1,x_2)\propto\exp\Big\{-\frac{x_1^2-2\rho x_1x_2+x_2^2}{2(1-\rho^2)}\Big\}.

As a function of x_1 with x_2 fixed, complete the square: x_1^2-2\rho x_1x_2 = (x_1-\rho x_2)^2-\rho^2x_2^2. The second term does not involve x_1, so

p(x_1\mid x_2)\propto\exp\Big\{-\frac{(x_1-\rho x_2)^2}{2(1-\rho^2)}\Big\},

which is a Gaussian density in x_1 with mean \rho x_2 and variance 1-\rho^2. By symmetry the same holds with the roles of x_1 and x_2 swapped.

Example: Gibbs sampling a bivariate Gaussian

The lecture applies Gibbs sampling to a problem where direct sampling would be easy, so we can watch how it behaves: the bivariate Gaussian of The conditional distribution of a bivariate Gaussian. Using its two full conditionals, start at X^{(0)}=(0,0) and for t\ge1 draw

X^{(t)}_1\sim\mathcal N\big(\mu_1+\rho(x^{(t-1)}_2-\mu_2),\ 1-\rho^2\big), X^{(t)}_2\sim\mathcal N\big(\mu_2+\rho(x^{(t)}_1-\mu_1),\ 1-\rho^2\big).

The second line conditions on the x_1 value just drawn in the same iteration, as the Gibbs algorithm prescribes.

After the first 10 iterations, the axis-parallel zig-zag paths are visible. After 500 iterations, the chains overlap and fill the elliptical region. Keeping the second half of each chain gives a cloud of draws shaped like the target. Worked example: the first Gibbs steps. Take \mu=(0,0) and \rho=0.8, so each full conditional has variance 1-0.8^2=0.36 (standard deviation 0.6), and start, as in Figure 4, at x^{(0)}=(-2.5,\ 2.5).

  • Update x_1 given x_2=2.5: draw from \mathcal N(0.8\times2.5,\ 0.36)=\mathcal N(2.0,\ 0.36). Suppose the draw is 1.7.
  • Update x_2 given the new x_1=1.7: draw from \mathcal N(0.8\times1.7,\ 0.36)=\mathcal N(1.36,\ 0.36). Suppose the draw is 1.2.

After one full iteration, x^{(1)}=(1.7,\ 1.2). The starting value x^{(0)}_1=-2.5 was never used: in a two-coordinate Gibbs sampler, only x_2^{(0)} matters for the first update. Each conditional mean pulls the state toward the line x_1=x_2, where this target’s mass lies, and the chain then moves along that line in steps of size roughly 0.6.

When Gibbs is slow. Each move is parallel to a coordinate axis, with conditional standard deviation \sqrt{1-\rho^2}. The target, though, stretches along the diagonal with standard deviation 1 in each coordinate. With \rho=0.8 the steps are 0.6, which is fine. With \rho=0.99 they shrink to about 0.14, and the chain needs many small zig-zags to travel along the ridge. Strongly correlated coordinates are the typical failure case of Gibbs sampling, and they motivate the next method.

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo

A random-walk proposal picks a direction blindly. On a long, narrow ridge like the \rho=0.9 Gaussian of the tutorial, most random directions leave the ridge, so the step size must be small, and small steps crawl along it. Gibbs sampling, restricted to axis directions, has the same problem. Can we use the shape of \tilde p to propose large moves that stay in high-probability regions?

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) is essentially a Metropolis-Hastings algorithm with a specific proposal mechanism inspired by physics.

Position and energy. Call the state x the position, and define its potential energy

E(x) = -\log\tilde p(x), \qquad\text{so that}\qquad p(x)\propto e^{-E(x)}.

Low energy means high probability. Here E(x) is an energy function, not an expectation (which is always written \mathbb{E}). For a posterior, E is minus the log-likelihood minus the log-prior, the kind of loss function minimized in maximum likelihood or MAP estimation. The tutorial’s \rho=0.9 Gaussian has E(x)=\tfrac12x^\top\Sigma^{-1}x, a quadratic bowl stretched along the diagonal.

Momentum and the Hamiltonian. Next, introduce an auxiliary variable v, of the same dimension as x, called the momentum. It carries the kinetic energy

K(v) = \tfrac12\|v\|^2 = \tfrac12v^\top v.

This momentum has nothing to do with the damping “momentum” of loopy BP in Week 4 (Loopy belief propagation in Week 4); it is a physical velocity-like variable.

Definition. The Hamiltonian, or total energy, is H(x,v) = E(x) + K(v).

The physical picture in the lecture: E is a landscape, and (x,v) describes a frictionless ball rolling on it. The ball speeds up going downhill, as potential energy turns into kinetic energy, and slows going uphill. Two properties of this motion matter.

  • Energy is preserved. If the ball moves from (x,v) to (x',v'), then H(x,v)=H(x',v').
  • The motion is reversible. Flip the momentum at the end and the ball retraces its path: starting from (x',-v') it returns to (x,-v).

The joint distribution. Define a distribution over position and momentum together:

p(x,v) \propto e^{-E(x)}\,e^{-K(v)} = e^{-E(x)-K(v)} = e^{-H(x,v)}.

It factorizes, so x and v are independent. The x-part is e^{-E(x)}=\tilde p(x), our target. The v-part is e^{-\frac12\|v\|^2}, the standard Gaussian \mathcal N(0,I). So if we can sample pairs (x,v) from this joint, the x’s are samples from p, and we simply discard the v’s.

Hamiltonian dynamics. The motion of the ball is governed by Hamilton’s equations:

\frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial v} = \frac{\partial K}{\partial v} = v, \frac{dv}{dt} = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial x} = -\frac{\partial E}{\partial x}.

Here t is a continuous “physical” time inside one HMC proposal, not the iteration counter of the chain. Position changes in the direction of the momentum, and momentum changes in the direction of steepest descent of the energy, the negative gradient -\nabla E(x)=\nabla\log\tilde p(x). That is how the shape of \tilde p steers the proposal.

The lecture says one can show these dynamics conserve energy. The check is one line with the chain rule:

\frac{dH}{dt} = \nabla E(x)^\top\frac{dx}{dt} + v^\top\frac{dv}{dt} = \nabla E(x)^\top v + v^\top\big(-\nabla E(x)\big) = 0.

The MCMC procedure, in outline. One iteration does three things.

  1. Sample a fresh momentum from the standard Gaussian. This is an exact draw from the v-part of the joint.
  2. Simulate Hamiltonian dynamics from (x,v) for a while, then flip the sign of the momentum. Flipping makes the proposal time-reversible: applying the same procedure to the end point leads back to the start.
  3. Accept or reject with the MH rule. With exact dynamics, energy is constant, so p(x,v)=p(x',v')=p(x',-v') (flipping v' does not change K), and the acceptance probability would be 1.

In practice the dynamics can only be simulated approximately, which is where the next section comes in.

The leapfrog integrator and the HMC algorithm

Hamilton’s equations rarely have a closed-form solution, so we approximate them in small time steps of size \epsilon. The simplest scheme, Euler’s method, updates both variables using their derivatives at the start of the step, x(t+\epsilon)\approx x(t)+\epsilon\,\frac{dx}{dt}(t). The lecture uses a standard alternative that is slightly more accurate.

Definition. One step of the leapfrog integrator with step size \epsilon is a half step in momentum, a full step in position, and another half step in momentum: v(t+\tfrac\epsilon2) = v(t) - \tfrac\epsilon2\,\nabla E\big(x(t)\big), x(t+\epsilon) = x(t) + \epsilon\,v(t+\tfrac\epsilon2), v(t+\epsilon) = v(t+\tfrac\epsilon2) - \tfrac\epsilon2\,\nabla E\big(x(t+\epsilon)\big).

The position update uses \partial K/\partial v=v, evaluated at the half-step momentum; the momentum updates use \partial E/\partial x=\nabla E. The lecture says there is no need to memorize these formulas. Two properties matter: HMC runs a fixed number L of leapfrog steps per proposal, and the resulting map from (x,v) to (x',v') is still deterministic and reversible, like the exact dynamics. Unlike the exact dynamics, it does not conserve H exactly; the small change in H is the discretization error.

Since -\nabla E=\nabla\log\tilde p, the momentum updates can equally be written v\leftarrow v+\tfrac\epsilon2\nabla\log\tilde p(x). Code that works with the gradient of a log density, rather than of an energy, implements exactly the same step.

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo

Run until the samples are “good enough” (see MCMC diagnostics). In each iteration, with current position x^{(t-1)}:

  1. Sample a fresh momentum v^{(t)}\sim\mathcal N(0,I).
  2. Starting at (x,v)=(x^{(t-1)},v^{(t)}), run the leapfrog integrator for L steps, reaching (x',v').
  3. Propose (x',-v') and accept it with probability \min\Bigg\{1,\ \frac{\exp\big(-H(x',v')\big)}{\exp\big(-H(x^{(t-1)},v^{(t)})\big)}\Bigg\} = \min\Big\{1,\ \exp\big(H(x^{(t-1)},v^{(t)})-H(x',v')\big)\Big\}.
  4. If accepted, x^{(t)}=x'; otherwise x^{(t)}=x^{(t-1)}. Discard the momentum.

The slide writes the old momentum in the acceptance ratio as v^{(t-1)}. The momentum that belongs there is the one the trajectory actually started from, the fresh draw v^{(t)} of step 1, and that is what the tutorial’s code uses.

Why this acceptance rule. The proposal is deterministic given (x,v) and, thanks to the momentum flip, reversible: proposing from (x',-v') leads back to (x,v). The lecture notes that this makes HMC a Metropolis-Hastings algorithm with a symmetric proposal, q(x'\mid x)=q(x\mid x'), so only the target ratio p(x',-v')/p(x,v)=e^{-H(x',v')}/e^{-H(x,v)} remains. If leapfrog conserved H exactly, the ratio would be 1. The accept step corrects for its small errors. Moves that end at lower total energy are always accepted (the lecture: low energy points are favoured), and moves that gain energy are accepted with probability e^{-\Delta H}.

Note

Worked example: one HMC acceptance. The tutorial runs one trajectory on its \rho=0.9 Gaussian with \epsilon=0.2 and L=25, starting from a typical point. Along the trajectory, potential and kinetic energy trade off as the ball rolls across the narrow direction of the ridge, while the total stays nearly constant: H=4.12 at the start and H=4.17 at the end. The acceptance probability is \min\{1,\ e^{4.12-4.17}\} = e^{-0.05} \approx 0.95. The end point is far from the start along the ridge, yet it is accepted with high probability, which is the combination a random walk cannot achieve.

Figure 5. After the tutorial’s comparison on a \rho=0.9 Gaussian. Left: a random walk from the gold start point makes small, nearly cancelling moves. Right: one leapfrog trajectory from the same start oscillates across the narrow direction while travelling along the ridge, and its end point (green) is the proposal.

The tutorial runs both samplers from the same corner for 300 iterations. Random-walk MH (step size 0.5) accepts about half its proposals, but each accepted move is small, so after 300 iterations it has explored only part of the ridge. HMC (\epsilon=0.2, L=12) accepts nearly every proposal, and each proposal lands far from the previous state, so its trace looks like noise around the centre: nearly independent samples.

What HMC costs, and its two knobs.

  • Gradients. Each iteration evaluates \nabla E once per leapfrog step, so L gradient evaluations per sample, and the gradient must be available at all. The tutorial notes that in practice it comes from automatic differentiation of the log-posterior.
  • Step size \epsilon controls the integration error. In the tutorial, raising \epsilon from 0.2 to 0.7 makes leapfrog unstable and the acceptance rate collapses.
  • Trajectory length L\epsilon controls how far the ball rolls. Too short, and HMC behaves like a random walk. It also should not match the period of the oscillation across the ridge: with L=10 in the tutorial, the ball returns to almost where it started across the ridge on every iteration, and that direction stops mixing. The tutorial notes that practical implementations randomize L a little for this reason.

The pieces to understand before starting it are in this section and the previous one: the energy is minus the log of the unnormalized posterior (log-prior plus log-likelihood), its gradient drives the leapfrog steps (the assignment’s code writes them with the gradient of the log density, which is -\nabla E), and the accept step corrects for leapfrog error. MCMC diagnostics

Every MCMC method above comes with the same practical questions, which the lecture lists:

  • How do we know we have run the algorithm long enough, that the chain mixes, meaning it has converged to and moves freely around the target distribution?
  • What if we started very far from where the distribution’s mass is?
  • Consecutive samples are correlated (autocorrelation, the correlation of a sequence with a shifted copy of itself). What is the “effective” number of samples?

The lecture’s practical advice:

  • Run several chains. Parallel computation is cheap, so run multiple chains in parallel from different (preferably widely spread) starting points, as in the Gibbs figure of Example: Gibbs sampling a bivariate Gaussian.
  • Discard a burn-in. Throw away the initial stretch of each chain (Why MCMC).
  • Check how well the chains have mixed. The next two sections give a statistic for this (\hat R) and a measure of how much information the correlated samples carry (effective sample size). The lecture says there is no need to memorize their formulas.

The \hat R statistic

The idea is to compare chains with each other. If the chains have all reached the target and mix well, each chain on its own should look like the whole collection. If one or more chains have not mixed, for example because they are stuck in different regions, the chains disagree with each other, and the variance of all the chains pooled together is larger than the variance within each one.

Start with m chains, each of length n after a fixed burn-in, and write x_{ij} for draw i of chain j (for a scalar quantity; with a vector, apply this to each coordinate or test function separately). Define the chain means and the overall mean

\bar x_{\cdot j} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n x_{ij}, \qquad \bar x_{\cdot\cdot} = \frac1m\sum_{j=1}^m\bar x_{\cdot j} = \frac{1}{mn}\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^m x_{ij}.

Definition. The between-sequence variance B and the within-sequence variance W are B = \frac{n}{m-1}\sum_{j=1}^m\big(\bar x_{\cdot j}-\bar x_{\cdot\cdot}\big)^2, \qquad W = \frac1m\sum_{j=1}^m s_j^2, where s_j^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n\big(x_{ij}-\bar x_{\cdot j}\big)^2 is the sample variance of chain j.

B measures how much the chain means disagree (scaled by n); W is the average spread inside a chain. Combine them into an estimate of the target’s variance,

\widehat{\mathrm{var}}^+(x) = \frac{n-1}{n}\,W + \frac1n\,B,

and compare it with W:

Definition. The \hat R (“R-hat”) statistic is \hat R = \sqrt{\frac{\widehat{\mathrm{var}}^+(x)}{W}}.

When the chains agree, B is small, \widehat{\mathrm{var}}^+\approx W, and \hat R is near 1. If the chains have not mixed well, \hat R is larger than 1, because the disagreement between chains inflates \widehat{\mathrm{var}}^+ but not W.

Split-\hat R. Two chains could agree with each other while each one is still drifting. To catch this, split every chain into its first and second halves and compute \hat R treating the 2m halves as separate chains. A chain whose first half differs from its second half (non-stationarity within a single chain) then inflates \hat R too.

Figure 6. Trace plots (sample value against iteration) of two chains. Left: each chain is internally steady, but they sit at different levels, so B is large compared with W. Right: the chains are indistinguishable.

Note

Worked example: \hat R by hand. Take m=2 chains of n=4 draws each. These are toy numbers chosen to make the arithmetic visible, not output from a real sampler.

Not mixed. Chain 1 is 1,2,3,2 and chain 2 is 5,6,7,6. The chain means are 2 and 6, the overall mean is 4, and each chain’s sample variance is \frac{1+0+1+0}{3}=\frac23. So W=\tfrac23, \qquad B=\frac{4}{1}\big[(2-4)^2+(6-4)^2\big]=32, \widehat{\mathrm{var}}^+ = \tfrac34\cdot\tfrac23+\tfrac14\cdot32 = 0.5+8 = 8.5, \qquad \hat R=\sqrt{8.5/(2/3)}\approx3.57.

Mixed. Keep chain 1 and replace chain 2 by 2,3,3,2, with mean 2.5 and sample variance \frac{0.25\times4}{3}=\frac13. Now W=\frac12\big(\frac23+\frac13\big)=0.5, the overall mean is 2.25, and B=4\big[(0.25)^2+(0.25)^2\big]=0.5, \qquad \widehat{\mathrm{var}}^+ = \tfrac34\cdot0.5+\tfrac14\cdot0.5 = 0.5, \qquad \hat R = 1. The within-chain spreads are similar in both cases; what drives \hat R is how far apart the chain means are.

Effective sample size

How much is a correlated sample worth? Start from i.i.d. draws. If x_1,\dots,x_n are i.i.d. with variance \sigma^2, their mean has \mathrm{var}(\bar x_n)=\sigma^2/n (Simple Monte Carlo in Week 4). Without assuming independence, the variance of a sum is the sum of all pairwise covariances, so

\mathrm{var}(\bar x) = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n\mathrm{cov}(x_i,x_j) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n\mathrm{corr}(x_i,x_j),

where the second form assumes every x_i has the same variance \sigma^2. Compare with the i.i.d. formula \sigma^2/n_{\text{eff}}: the correlated sample gives the same accuracy as

n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{n^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n\mathrm{corr}(x_i,x_j)}

independent draws. With independent draws only the n diagonal terms \mathrm{corr}(x_i,x_i)=1 survive and n_{\text{eff}}=n; positive correlations make the denominator larger and n_{\text{eff}} smaller.

For a stationary chain, the correlation between two draws depends only on how far apart they are. Let \rho_t=\mathrm{corr}(x_0,x_t) be the autocorrelation at lag t. (This \rho_t is unrelated to the correlation \rho of the bivariate Gaussian above.) For long chains, the double sum is then about n times 1+2\sum_{t\ge1}\rho_t: the 1 from each draw with itself, and the 2 because each lag appears on both sides of the diagonal. For m chains of length n this leads to the lecture’s definition.

Definition. The effective sample size of m chains of length n is n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{mn}{1+2\sum_{t=1}^\infty\rho_t}. In practice the \rho_t are unknown and are themselves estimated from the chains, giving an estimate \hat n_{\text{eff}}.

Note

Worked example: what a correlation of 0.9 costs. Suppose, for illustration, that the autocorrelation decays geometrically, \rho_t=\rho^t (an assumption, not something the lecture claims about any particular sampler). Then \sum_{t\ge1}\rho^t=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho} and 1+\frac{2\rho}{1-\rho} = \frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}. With lag-one correlation \rho=0.9, as for the tutorial’s tiny-step chain (\sigma=0.03), this factor is 1.9/0.1=19. So 5000 such samples would be worth only about 5000/19\approx260 independent ones. This is the number behind the tutorial’s warning that a high acceptance rate with tiny steps is not efficient.

Diagnostics rule of thumb

The lecture’s practical rule: once \hat R is near 1 and \hat n_{\text{eff}} is more than 10 per chain, we can use all mn simulations (excluding the burn-in) and draw inference from them, for example by the Monte Carlo averages of Why MCMC.

The lecture is candid about the limits of this rule. Even when the simulations appear to have converged and pass every test, they may still be far from convergence: all chains might, for example, have missed a region of high probability that none of them ever found. When we declare “convergence”, we mean only that all chains appear stationary and well mixed.

Key takeaways from Week 5:

  • Markov chains. A first-order chain assumes p(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1})=p(x_t\mid x_{t-1}); a stationary (homogeneous) chain uses the same transition matrix A_{ij}=p(x_t=j\mid x_{t-1}=i) at every step. Rows of A sum to 1, and the n-step transition matrix is A^n (Chapman-Kolmogorov).
  • Language models. A bigram model is a Markov chain over words; the MLE of each transition probability is a ratio of bigram counts, \hat A_{jk}=N_{jk}/\sum_{k'}N_{jk'}, the Multinomial MLE applied row by row.
  • Stationary distributions. \pi is stationary if \pi=A^\top\pi, an eigenvector of A^\top with eigenvalue 1. A regular finite chain has a unique stationary distribution and converges to it from any start. Detailed balance, \pi_iA_{ij}=\pi_jA_{ji}, is a simple sufficient condition for \pi to be stationary.
  • Metropolis-Hastings. Propose x'\sim q(x'\mid x) and accept with probability \min\{1,\ \tilde p(x')q(x\mid x')/(\tilde p(x)q(x'\mid x))\}, recording the old state again on rejection. Only ratios of \tilde p appear, so Z is never needed, and detailed balance makes p stationary. The proposal scale trades acceptance rate against step length.
  • Gibbs sampling. Resample each coordinate from its full conditional p(x_j\mid x_{-j}), always accepting. It suits models with easy conditionals, such as the Ising model, and slows down when coordinates are strongly correlated.
  • Hamiltonian Monte Carlo. With energy E=-\log\tilde p, momentum v\sim\mathcal N(0,I), and Hamiltonian H=E+\tfrac12\|v\|^2, simulate the dynamics with L leapfrog steps and accept with probability \min\{1,e^{H_{\text{start}}-H_{\text{end}}}\}. Gradients let it make long moves that are still usually accepted.
  • Diagnostics. Run several chains from spread-out starts, discard burn-in, and check \hat R (near 1 when chains agree) and the effective sample size mn/(1+2\sum_t\rho_t). Passing these checks shows the chains appear mixed, not that they have converged.
