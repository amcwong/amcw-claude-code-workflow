Week 6: HMMs and Variational Inference I

Week 6: HMMs and Variational Inference I

Overview. This lecture has two halves joined by one theme: computing with a posterior over unobserved variables. The first half returns to the Markov chains of Week 5, but hides the chain. In a hidden Markov model the states z_1,\dots,z_T form a Markov chain we never see, and each state emits an observation x_t we do see. Two recursions exploit the chain structure: the forward-backward algorithm computes the posterior of each hidden state, and the Viterbi algorithm finds the single most probable hidden sequence. Both cost time linear in T instead of exponential. The second half turns to models where no such recursion exists, such as the TrueSkill model of Week 4. There the posterior p(z\mid x) needs a normalizing constant we cannot compute. Week 5 answered with sampling (MCMC). This week starts a second answer, variational inference: pick a tractable family of distributions and find the member closest to the posterior, with closeness measured by the KL divergence. The two directions of KL behave differently, and the lecture ends with the naive mean-field method for MRFs (Week 3), which turns inference into a coordinate-wise optimization.

From Markov chains to hidden states

Week 5 (Markov chains in Week 5) modelled sequential data, such as stock prices, speech, video, text, or gene sequences, without assuming the values are independent. The chain rule factorizes any sequence exactly,

p(x_{1:T}) = \prod_{t=1}^T p(x_t\mid x_{t-1},\dots,x_1), \qquad p(x_1\mid x_0) := p(x_1),

but each factor conditions on the whole history, which quickly becomes intractable. The first-order Markov assumption p(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1}) = p(x_t\mid x_{t-1}) cut every factor down to one step.

That assumption is sometimes too restrictive. Often what evolves step by step is not the thing we measure but some underlying condition that we never observe directly, and the measurements only reflect it. If we force a Markov chain onto the measurements themselves, the one-step dependence has to carry all of the history the hidden condition would have carried. The lecture’s fix is to put the Markov chain on latent variables (Week 4, Latent variables and the TrueSkill model in Week 4: unobserved variables that govern the observed data) and let each observation depend only on the latent state at its own time step. This is the hidden Markov model, which Week 2 previewed (DGMs in the wild, briefly in Week 2).

Hidden Markov models

Definition. A hidden Markov model (HMM) pairs each observation x_t with an unobserved hidden state z_t, for t=1,\dots,T. The hidden states form a first-order Markov chain, and each observation depends only on the hidden state at the same time: p(x_{1:T}, z_{1:T}) = p(z_1)\prod_{t=2}^T p(z_t\mid z_{t-1})\prod_{t=1}^T p(x_t\mid z_t).

The first two factors are exactly a Week 5 Markov chain on z. The last product adds one emission factor per time step: the hidden state “emits” the observation. The lecture’s phrase is that an HMM hides the temporal dependence by keeping it in the unobserved state. The observations x_t are not a Markov chain on their own: once z is summed out, x_t can depend on the entire past.

Figure 1. After the lecture’s HMM diagram. Hidden states (light) form a Markov chain; each emits one observation (shaded, because it is observed).

Reading the graph with Week 2’s rules (Conditional independence: three canonical structures in Week 2): every path from the past to the future passes through the current hidden state z_t, and z_t is a chain or fork node on that path. So observing z_t separates the past from the future. The recursions below use exactly this fact, in three forms:

  • x_t \perp x_{1:t-1} \mid z_t (an observation depends only on its own state),
  • z_t \perp x_{1:t-1} \mid z_{t-1} (the next state depends only on the current state),
  • x_{t+1:T} \perp x_{1:t} \mid z_t (the future observations depend on the past only through z_t).

As in Week 5, we assume the model is homogeneous (stationary): the transition and emission distributions do not change with t. With K hidden states, z_t\in\{1,\dots,K\}, the model is then specified by three sets of distributions.

Definition. The parameters of a homogeneous HMM with K hidden states are:

  1. the initial distribution \pi(i) = p(z_1 = i), the probability that the first hidden state is i;
  2. the transition matrix \Psi(i,j) = p(z_{t+1}=j\mid z_t=i) for i,j\in\{1,\dots,K\}, the probability of moving from hidden state i to hidden state j;
  3. the emission probabilities \psi_t(i) = p(x_t\mid z_t=i), the probability of the observed x_t given the hidden state that emitted it.

\Psi is Week 5’s transition matrix A under a new name (Transition matrices and state transition diagrams in Week 5): row i is the distribution of the next state given state i, so each row sums to 1.

The emission vector \psi_t is easy to misread. The observation x_t is fixed (we have seen it), so \psi_t is a vector of K numbers, one per possible hidden state, each saying how well that state explains what we saw at time t. As a function of i it is a likelihood, not a distribution: its entries need not sum to 1. The subscript t is there because the observed value changes with t, not because the emission distribution does.

Two inference questions

Given the parameters and an observed sequence x_{1:T}, the lecture asks two questions about the hidden states.

  1. Marginal posteriors. For each t, what is p(z_t\mid x_{1:T}), the distribution of the hidden state at time t given all the observations, before and after t? The forward-backward algorithm answers this.
  2. The most probable sequence. Which whole hidden sequence is most probable given the observations, z^\star = \arg\max_{z_{1:T}} p(z_{1:T}\mid x_{1:T})? This is maximum a posteriori (MAP) inference, and the Viterbi algorithm answers it.

Both could be answered by brute force: list all K^T hidden sequences, compute the joint probability of each from the factorization, then sum or maximize. That is exponential in T. Both algorithms below instead cost on the order of TK^2 operations, by working through the chain one step at a time.

The forward algorithm

We start with an easier question: the posterior of z_t given only the observations up to time t.

Definition. The forward vector at time t is the probability vector \alpha_t(j) = p(z_t = j\mid x_{1:t}), \qquad j=1,\dots,K.

Computing \alpha_t from left to right is often called filtering: at each step we update our belief about the current state using the newest observation.

The recursion is written with one more piece of notation.

Definition. The Hadamard product u\odot v of two vectors of the same length is their entrywise product, (u\odot v)(j) = u(j)\,v(j).

\alpha_1 \propto \psi_1\odot\pi, \qquad \alpha_t \propto \psi_t\odot\left(\Psi^\top\alpha_{t-1}\right)\quad (t>1).

Each \propto means “rescale so the entries sum to 1”. In words: push yesterday’s belief one step through the chain (\Psi^\top\alpha_{t-1}), then reweight each state by how well it explains today’s observation (\psi_t), then renormalize. This is prediction followed by a Bayes-rule update.

Derivation, t=1. By Bayes’ rule, for any state j,

\alpha_1(j) = p(z_1=j\mid x_1) = \frac{p(x_1\mid z_1=j)\,p(z_1=j)}{p(x_1)} \propto \psi_1(j)\,\pi(j).

The denominator p(x_1) does not depend on j, so it is absorbed by the normalization.

Derivation, t>1. Bayes’ rule again, now treating x_{1:t-1} as already given:

\begin{aligned} \alpha_t(j) &= p(z_t=j\mid x_{1:t}) \\ &\propto p(x_t\mid z_t=j)\;p(z_t=j\mid x_{1:t-1}). \end{aligned}

The first factor dropped x_{1:t-1} from the conditioning because x_t\perp x_{1:t-1}\mid z_t, so it is \psi_t(j). For the second factor, sum over the previous state z_{t-1}=i:

\begin{aligned} p(z_t=j\mid x_{1:t-1}) &= \sum_i p(z_t=j\mid z_{t-1}=i, x_{1:t-1})\,p(z_{t-1}=i\mid x_{1:t-1}) \\ &= \sum_i p(z_t=j\mid z_{t-1}=i)\;p(z_{t-1}=i\mid x_{1:t-1}) \\ &= \sum_i \Psi(i,j)\,\alpha_{t-1}(i). \end{aligned}

The second line used z_t\perp x_{1:t-1}\mid z_{t-1}, and the third recognizes the two factors as \Psi(i,j) and \alpha_{t-1}(i). Putting the pieces together,

\alpha_t(j) \propto \psi_t(j)\sum_i \Psi(i,j)\,\alpha_{t-1}(i).

The sum \sum_i\Psi(i,j)\alpha_{t-1}(i) is entry j of \Psi^\top\alpha_{t-1}, which is Week 5’s update p(x_t=j)=\sum_i A_{ij}\,p(x_{t-1}=i) applied to beliefs instead of unconditional probabilities.

Each step multiplies a K\times K matrix by a vector, so filtering all T steps costs on the order of TK^2 operations.

The forward-backward algorithm

The forward vector uses only the past. What we often actually want is the posterior of each hidden state given all the observations,

\gamma_t(i) = p(z_t=i\mid x_{1:T}) \qquad\text{for every } t,

assuming, as before, that \pi, \Psi, and the emissions are known. Computing \gamma_t is often called smoothing. The later observations x_{t+1:T} carry information about z_t, so we need a second vector that summarizes them.

Definition. The backward vector at time t is \beta_t(i) = p(x_{t+1:T}\mid z_t=i), \qquad i=1,\dots,K, the probability of the future observations given that the hidden state at time t is i.

Like \psi_t, \beta_t is a likelihood in i, not a distribution, so its entries need not sum to 1.

Forward-backward algorithm

  1. Forward pass. Compute \alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_T with the recursion of The forward algorithm.
  2. Backward pass. Set \beta_T(i)=1 for all i. For t=T-1,\dots,1, compute \beta_t = \Psi\left(\psi_{t+1}\odot\beta_{t+1}\right).
  3. Combine. For any t, \gamma_t \propto \alpha_t\odot\beta_t, normalized to sum to 1.

The convention \beta_T(i)=1 says that there are no future observations to explain after time T. Note the matrix in the backward step is \Psi, not \Psi^\top: the backward pass runs the chain in reverse, so it sums over the next state j for each current state i, along row i of \Psi.

Why \gamma_t\propto\alpha_t\odot\beta_t. Split the observations into past-and-present x_{1:t} and future x_{t+1:T}:

\begin{aligned} \gamma_t = p(z_t\mid x_{1:T}) &= p(z_t, x_{1:T})/p(x_{1:T}) \\ &\propto p(z_t, x_{1:T}) \\ &= p(z_t, x_{1:t})\,p(x_{t+1:T}\mid z_t, x_{1:t}) \\ &= p(z_t, x_{1:t})\,p(x_{t+1:T}\mid z_t) \\ &\propto \alpha_t\odot\beta_t. \end{aligned}

The fourth line used x_{t+1:T}\perp x_{1:t}\mid z_t. In the last line, p(z_t, x_{1:t}) = \alpha_t\,p(x_{1:t}) and p(x_{1:t}) does not depend on z_t, so it is absorbed by the normalization.

Why the backward recursion holds. Sum over the next state z_{t+1}=j and peel off one observation:

\begin{aligned} \beta_t(i) &= p(x_{t+1:T}\mid z_t=i) = \sum_j p(z_{t+1}=j,\ x_{t+1:T}\mid z_t=i) \\ &= \sum_j p(x_{t+2:T}\mid z_{t+1}=j, z_t=i, x_{t+1}) \\ &\qquad\quad\times p(x_{t+1}\mid z_{t+1}=j, z_t=i)\;p(z_{t+1}=j\mid z_t=i) \\ &= \sum_j p(x_{t+2:T}\mid z_{t+1}=j)\;p(x_{t+1}\mid z_{t+1}=j)\;p(z_{t+1}=j\mid z_t=i) \\ &= \sum_j \beta_{t+1}(j)\,\psi_{t+1}(j)\,\Psi(i,j). \end{aligned}

The second line is the chain rule. The third drops everything that z_{t+1} separates: given z_{t+1}, the later observations no longer depend on z_t or x_{t+1}, and x_{t+1} no longer depends on z_t. The last line is entry i of \Psi(\psi_{t+1}\odot\beta_{t+1}).

Forward-backward is the sum-product belief propagation of Week 4 (Belief propagation on trees in Week 4) run on a chain. The HMM graph is a tree, so two passes (one in each direction) give every marginal exactly. The \alpha’s are the messages flowing forward in time and the \beta’s are the messages flowing backward.

Worked example: a two-state HMM

The lecture gives the recursions without numbers. Here is a small teaching example with invented parameters, worked by hand. Two hidden states \{1,2\}, binary observations x_t\in\{0,1\}, and T=3:

\pi = \begin{pmatrix}0.5\\0.5\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Psi = \begin{pmatrix}0.8 & 0.2\\ 0.3 & 0.7\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \begin{array}{c|cc} & x=0 & x=1\\\hline z=1 & 0.8 & 0.2\\ z=2 & 0.4 & 0.6\end{array}

State 1 tends to emit 0 and state 2 tends to emit 1. Both states tend to stay put. We observe x_{1:3} = (0, 1, 1), so the emission vectors are the matching columns of the table:

\psi_1 = \begin{pmatrix}0.8\\0.4\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \psi_2 = \psi_3 = \begin{pmatrix}0.2\\0.6\end{pmatrix}.

Note

Forward pass.

  • t=1: \psi_1\odot\pi = (0.4,\ 0.2). These sum to 0.6, so \alpha_1 = (2/3,\ 1/3)\approx(0.667,\ 0.333). The first observation was a 0, so state 1 is favoured.
  • t=2: predict, \Psi^\top\alpha_1 = (0.8\cdot\tfrac23 + 0.3\cdot\tfrac13,\ \ 0.2\cdot\tfrac23 + 0.7\cdot\tfrac13) \approx (0.633,\ 0.367). Update, \psi_2\odot(\Psi^\top\alpha_1) \approx (0.127,\ 0.220), which sums to 0.347. So \alpha_2\approx(0.365,\ 0.635).
  • t=3: predict, \Psi^\top\alpha_2\approx(0.483,\ 0.517). Update, \psi_3\odot(\Psi^\top\alpha_2)\approx(0.097,\ 0.310), which sums to 0.407. So \alpha_3\approx(0.237,\ 0.763).

Backward pass.

  • \beta_3 = (1,\ 1).
  • \beta_2 = \Psi(\psi_3\odot\beta_3) = \Psi\,(0.2,\ 0.6)^\top = (0.8\cdot0.2+0.2\cdot0.6,\ \ 0.3\cdot0.2+0.7\cdot0.6) = (0.28,\ 0.48).
  • \beta_1 = \Psi(\psi_2\odot\beta_2) = \Psi\,(0.056,\ 0.288)^\top = (0.1024,\ 0.2184).

Combine.

  • \gamma_1\propto\alpha_1\odot\beta_1 \approx (0.0683,\ 0.0728), so \gamma_1\approx(0.484,\ 0.516).
  • \gamma_2\propto\alpha_2\odot\beta_2 \approx (0.102,\ 0.305), so \gamma_2\approx(0.251,\ 0.749).
  • \gamma_3 = \alpha_3 \approx (0.237,\ 0.763), since \beta_3 is all ones.

Compare \alpha_1 with \gamma_1. Using only x_1=0, state 1 was favoured two to one. Once the two later 1’s are taken into account, state 2 becomes slightly more probable at t=1: the chain tends to stay put, and the later observations point strongly to state 2. This is what smoothing adds over filtering.

Two checks on the arithmetic. First, the product of the three normalizers from the forward pass is the probability of the data, p(x_{1:3}) = 0.6\times0.347\times0.407\approx0.0846, because each normalizer is p(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1}). Second, the backward pass gives the same number: \sum_i \pi(i)\,\psi_1(i)\,\beta_1(i) = 0.4\cdot0.1024 + 0.2\cdot0.2184 = 0.08464. Enumerating all 2^3=8 hidden sequences by brute force reproduces every \gamma_t above.

The Viterbi algorithm

The second question asks for the single most probable hidden sequence,

\hat z = \arg\max_{z_{1:T}}\ p(z_{1:T}\mid x_{1:T}).

Since p(z_{1:T}\mid x_{1:T}) = p(z_{1:T}, x_{1:T})/p(x_{1:T}) and the denominator does not depend on z, we can maximize the joint p(z_{1:T}, x_{1:T}) instead. The Viterbi algorithm (Viterbi, 1967) does this with the same left-to-right structure as the forward algorithm, replacing each sum by a maximum.

Definition. The Viterbi score \delta_t(j) is the joint probability of the most likely hidden path that ends in state j at time t, together with the observations so far: \delta_t(j) = \max_{z_{1:t-1}}\ p(z_{1:t-1},\ z_t=j,\ x_{1:t}).

Derivation of the recursion (t>1). Split off the last step of the joint using the HMM factorization, then do the maximization in two stages, first over the second-to-last state z_{t-1}=i and then over everything before it:

\begin{aligned} \delta_t(j) &= \max_{z_{1:t-1}}\ p(z_{1:t-1}, x_{1:t-1})\;p(z_t=j\mid z_{t-1})\;p(x_t\mid z_t=j) \\ &= p(x_t\mid z_t=j)\ \max_i\Big[p(z_t=j\mid z_{t-1}=i)\ \max_{z_{1:t-2}} p(z_{1:t-2},\ z_{t-1}=i,\ x_{1:t-1})\Big] \\ &= p(x_t\mid z_t=j)\ \max_i\big[\delta_{t-1}(i)\;p(z_t=j\mid z_{t-1}=i)\big] \\ &= \psi_t(j)\ \max_i\big[\delta_{t-1}(i)\,\Psi(i,j)\big]. \end{aligned}

The emission factor came out of the maximum because it does not depend on the earlier states. The inner maximum on the second line is \delta_{t-1}(i) by definition. In words: the best path into j at time t is the best path into some i at time t-1, extended by one transition and one emission, with i chosen to make that product largest.

To recover the path itself, not only its probability, we remember which i won each maximum.

Viterbi algorithm

  1. Initialization (t=1). For each state j, \delta_1(j) = p(z_1=j, x_1) = \pi(j)\,\psi_1(j).
  2. Forward recursion (t=2,\dots,T). For each state j, compute \delta_t(j) = \psi_t(j)\max_i\big[\delta_{t-1}(i)\,\Psi(i,j)\big] and store the maximizing previous state, the back-pointer \theta_t(j) = \arg\max_i\big[\delta_{t-1}(i)\,\Psi(i,j)\big].
  3. Termination. The final state of the most probable path is z^*_T = \arg\max_j \delta_T(j).
  4. Traceback. For t=T-1,\dots,1, set z^*_t = \theta_{t+1}(z^*_{t+1}).

The cost is again on the order of TK^2. Viterbi is the max-product version of belief propagation from Week 4 (Max-product BP and MAP inference in Week 4), specialized to a chain. Two practical remarks, both hedged: in long sequences the \delta’s shrink toward zero, so implementations typically work with \log\delta (products become sums, and the maximum is unchanged); and in general the most probable sequence need not agree, position by position, with the most probable state under each marginal \gamma_t, because the two algorithms answer different questions.

Note

Worked example: Viterbi on the two-state HMM. Same parameters and observations x_{1:3}=(0,1,1) as Worked example: a two-state HMM.

  • t=1: \delta_1 = \pi\odot\psi_1 = (0.4,\ 0.2).
  • t=2, j=1: candidates \delta_1(1)\Psi(1,1) = 0.4\cdot0.8 = 0.32 and \delta_1(2)\Psi(2,1) = 0.2\cdot0.3 = 0.06. The max is 0.32 from i=1, so \theta_2(1)=1 and \delta_2(1) = 0.2\cdot0.32 = 0.064.
  • t=2, j=2: candidates 0.4\cdot0.2 = 0.08 and 0.2\cdot0.7 = 0.14. The max is 0.14 from i=2, so \theta_2(2)=2 and \delta_2(2) = 0.6\cdot0.14 = 0.084.
  • t=3, j=1: candidates 0.064\cdot0.8 = 0.0512 and 0.084\cdot0.3 = 0.0252, so \theta_3(1)=1 and \delta_3(1) = 0.2\cdot0.0512 = 0.01024.
  • t=3, j=2: candidates 0.064\cdot0.2 = 0.0128 and 0.084\cdot0.7 = 0.0588, so \theta_3(2)=2 and \delta_3(2) = 0.6\cdot0.0588 = 0.03528.
  • Termination: \delta_3(2) > \delta_3(1), so z^*_3 = 2. Traceback: z^*_2 = \theta_3(2) = 2, then z^*_1 = \theta_2(2) = 2.

The most probable path is (2,2,2), with joint probability p(z_{1:3}=(2,2,2), x_{1:3}) = 0.03528. Dividing by p(x_{1:3})=0.08464 gives its posterior probability, about 0.417.

Figure 2. The Viterbi computation for the worked example. Each node shows its score \delta_t(j). Dashed arrows are transitions that lost their maximum; solid arrows are the back-pointers \theta_t. The green path is the traceback from the larger final score, giving z^*=(2,2,2).

At t=1 alone, state 1 had the larger score (0.4 against 0.2), yet the best path starts in state 2. The traceback, not the step-by-step leader, decides.

HMMs in one sentence. An HMM is a latent Markov chain with observations; forward-backward gives the posterior of each hidden state, Viterbi gives the MAP sequence, and both get their efficiency from recursions that follow the chain structure.

Posterior inference in latent variable models

The second half of the lecture asks what to do when no such recursion exists. We have worked with several latent variable models: the TrueSkill model of Week 4, a generative image model, and now HMMs. All of them have the factorization

p(x, z) = p(z)\,p(x\mid z),

where x are the observations (the data) and z are the unobserved latent variables.

Definition. In a latent variable model p(x,z)=p(z)\,p(x\mid z):

  • p(z) is the prior, what we believe about z before seeing data;
  • p(x\mid z) is the likelihood, how probable the data are for each setting of z;
  • p(z\mid x) is the posterior, the conditional distribution of the unobserved variables given the observed ones;
  • p(x) = \int p(x,z)\,dz is the evidence (or marginal likelihood), the probability of the data with z integrated out.

Bayes’ rule connects them:

p(z\mid x) = \frac{p(x\mid z)\,p(z)}{p(x)} = \frac{p(x\mid z)\,p(z)}{\int p(x,z)\,dz}.

The numerator is easy: it is the model itself, evaluated at one z. The denominator is the problem.

What the TrueSkill posterior looks like

The lecture illustrates with the two-player TrueSkill model of Week 4 (Latent variables and the TrueSkill model in Week 4): skills z_A, z_B with independent Gaussian priors, and p(A\text{ beats }B\mid z_A,z_B) = \sigma(z_A-z_B). The slides plot contours of the joint density over (z_A, z_B) at four stages.

  1. Prior. Round contours centred at the origin. The prior says we are very uncertain about both players’ skills.
  2. Posterior after one game. The contours shift and tilt. The lecture’s point is that the posterior is not Gaussian anymore: multiplying a Gaussian prior by a logistic likelihood does not give a Gaussian.
  3. Posterior after A beats B ten times. The posterior is now certain that A is better than B.
  4. Posterior after each beats the other ten times. The posterior is certain that neither player is much better than the other, but still uncertain how good they both are in an absolute sense.

The fourth picture has a direct explanation in the model. The likelihood of every game depends on the skills only through the difference z_A - z_B. Data can therefore pin down the difference, but they say nothing about the sum z_A+z_B, which stays as uncertain as the prior made it. The posterior is narrow across the line z_A=z_B and wide along it.

Why the posterior is hard

What exactly is hard about the posterior? The evidence

p(x) = \int p(x, z)\,dz

is an integral over every latent variable at once. When z is high dimensional, this integral is intractable, for the same reason Week 4 found the normalizing constant hard (Why the normalizing constant is hard in Week 4): a grid fine enough to integrate accurately has exponentially many points. Without p(x) we can neither evaluate the normalized posterior p(z\mid x) at a given z nor sample from it directly.

Week 5’s MCMC is one way around this: it samples from p(z\mid x) using only the unnormalized p(x,z). The rest of this lecture starts a second way, which replaces sampling with optimization.

Variational methods

The name comes from the calculus of variations, developed in the 1700s by Euler and Lagrange, which optimizes over functions rather than over numbers. Here the functions are probability distributions.

Definition. Variational inference (VI) is an approximate inference method that replaces an intractable target distribution, typically the posterior p(z\mid x), by the closest member of a tractable family. It has three ingredients:

  1. a variational family Q of tractable distributions q(z), from which we choose the approximation;
  2. a measure of the difference between q and p(z\mid x) that can be estimated efficiently, usually the KL divergence;
  3. an optimization method, usually iterative, that minimizes this difference over q\in Q.

For example, Q could be all Gaussians, q(z) = \mathcal N(z\mid\mu,\Sigma), with the mean \mu and covariance \Sigma as the quantities we optimize. The art is to choose a Q whose members are easy to work with but flexible enough that one of them is a good approximation to the true posterior.

Whatever Q we choose, there is usually no q\in Q that matches the true posterior exactly. The TrueSkill posterior after one game is not Gaussian, so no Gaussian q equals it. VI accepts this error on purpose: computing the true posterior is intractable, so a shortcut has to be taken somewhere, and VI takes it in the choice of Q.

Jensen’s inequality

The properties of the KL divergence in the next section rest on one inequality about convex functions, which the tutorial uses without proof.

Definition. A function \phi is convex if the chord between any two points on its graph lies on or above the graph: \phi(\lambda a + (1-\lambda)b) \le \lambda\phi(a) + (1-\lambda)\phi(b) for all a,b and \lambda\in[0,1]. A twice-differentiable function is convex when \phi''\ge0.

For example, -\log y is convex on y>0, since its second derivative is 1/y^2>0. Equivalently, \log is concave.

Definition. Jensen’s inequality. For any convex function \phi and any probability distribution p, \mathbb{E}_p[\phi(y)] \ge \phi\big(\mathbb{E}_p[y]\big), with equality if and only if y is constant (with probability 1 under p) or \phi is linear on the range of y.

The two-point case is the definition of convexity, with \lambda and 1-\lambda as the probabilities of the two points. Averaging first and then applying a convex function gives a smaller value than applying it first and then averaging.

The KL divergence

How should we measure how far q is from the posterior? The lecture uses the Kullback-Leibler divergence. Two information-theoretic quantities, recalled in the tutorial, help read it.

Definition. For distributions p and q over the same discrete variable,

  • the entropy of p is H(p) = -\sum_x p(x)\log p(x) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}[-\log p(x)], which measures the uncertainty in p;
  • the cross-entropy of q relative to p is H(p,q) = -\sum_x p(x)\log q(x).

For continuous variables, the sums become integrals.

Definition. The Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence of q from p is \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p) = \int q(z)\log\frac{q(z)}{p(z)}\,dz = \mathbb{E}_{z\sim q}\left[\log\frac{q(z)}{p(z)}\right], with a sum in place of the integral for discrete z.

In VI, p is the posterior, so the lecture writes \mathrm{KL}(q(z)\,\|\,p(z\mid x)). The KL divergence is the average, under the first argument, of the log ratio between the two densities. Writing the other direction out with the definitions above, the tutorial shows

\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q) = \mathbb{E}_{p}[\log p(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{p}[\log q(x)] = H(p,q) - H(p),

the extra cross-entropy we pay for describing data from p with the distribution q. With natural logarithms, as throughout these notes, the KL divergence is measured in nats.

The lecture lists three properties.

  1. \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p)\ge0.
  2. \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p)=0 if and only if q=p.
  3. In general \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p)\ne\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q).

Because of property 3, the KL divergence is not a metric (a distance): a distance must be symmetric. It is still a useful measure of closeness because of properties 1 and 2.

Proof of property 1 (from the tutorial). Write the log ratio upside down and apply Jensen’s inequality with the convex function -\log:

\begin{aligned} \mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q) = \mathbb{E}_p\left[-\log\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\right] &\ge -\log\mathbb{E}_p\left[\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\right] \\ &= -\log\int p(x)\,\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\,dx = -\log 1 = 0. \end{aligned}

The same argument with p and q swapped gives \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p)\ge0.

Proof of property 2. If p=q then \log(p/q)=0 everywhere, so the divergence is 0. Conversely, if the divergence is 0, the inequality in the proof above holds with equality. Since -\log is not linear, Jensen’s equality condition forces q(x)/p(x) to be constant, and two densities that each integrate to 1 can only have a constant ratio of 1. So p=q.

Note

Worked example: the two directions differ (from the tutorial). Let p and q be distributions on \{0,1\} with p(0)=1/4, p(1)=3/4 and q(0)=q(1)=1/2. Then

\begin{aligned} \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p) &= \tfrac12\log\frac{1/2}{1/4} + \tfrac12\log\frac{1/2}{3/4} = \tfrac12\log2 + \tfrac12\log\tfrac23 \approx 0.144, \\ \mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q) &= \tfrac14\log\frac{1/4}{1/2} + \tfrac34\log\frac{3/4}{1/2} = \tfrac14\log\tfrac12 + \tfrac34\log\tfrac32 \approx 0.131. \end{aligned}

These are not equal. (The tutorial prints 0.062 and 0.057, which are the same expressions with base-10 logarithms. The values above are in nats.)

Which direction? I-projection and M-projection

Since the two directions differ, we could minimize either one. Which should we choose? The lecture’s answer, “as always, we will go with the tractable one”, comes in Why variational inference uses the I-projection. First we see what each direction does. In this section, as on the slides, p(x) is any target distribution and q(x) is the approximation.

Definition. For a target p and a family Q:

  • the information projection (I-projection) minimizes the reverse KL, q^* = \arg\min_{q\in Q}\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p) = \arg\min_{q\in Q}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}\left[\log\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\right];
  • the moment projection (M-projection) minimizes the forward KL, q^* = \arg\min_{q\in Q}\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q) = \arg\min_{q\in Q}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\left[\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right].

If q is close to p, both divergences are small. They differ in which mistakes they punish.

The I-projection underestimates support. In \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p) the average is over q. Where q puts mass but p is nearly zero, \log(q/p) is very large, so q is heavily penalized for putting mass where p has none. Where p has mass but q does not, the integrand is weighted by q\approx0 and contributes almost nothing. So the I-projection prefers a q that sits inside one high-density region of p and ignores the rest. It also does not, in general, give the correct mean and covariance. This behaviour is often called mode-seeking.

The M-projection covers the mass. In \mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q) the average is over p. Where p has mass but q is nearly zero, \log(p/q) is very large, so q is heavily penalized for missing any region where p has mass. The M-projection therefore spreads q out to cover all of p. The lecture states that when Q is the family of Gaussians, the M-projection has exactly the mean and covariance of p, which is where the name “moment projection” comes from.

The slides show both on a target p that is a mixture of two 2D Gaussians, with Q the set of all 2D Gaussians with arbitrary covariance. The I-projection places a single Gaussian on one of the two components. There are two such solutions, one per component, and the slide calls them equally good. The M-projection is one broad Gaussian stretched over both components, putting much of its mass in the gap between them, where p is small.

Tutorial example: fitting a Gaussian to a two-component mixture

The tutorial repeats the comparison in one dimension. The target is the mixture

p(z) = 0.3\,\mathcal N(z;\,-2,\ 0.8^2) + 0.7\,\mathcal N(z;\,3,\ 1.1^2),

with a small mode near -2 holding 30% of the mass and a large mode near 3 holding 70%. The family is all 1D Gaussians, q(z)=\mathcal N(z;\,\mu,\sigma^2). A single Gaussian has one mode, so no q can match p.

M-projection in closed form. Only q depends on (\mu,\sigma^2), so minimizing \mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q) = H(p,q) - H(p) means minimizing the cross-entropy -\mathbb{E}_p[\log q(z)]:

\min_{\mu,\sigma^2}\ \left[\frac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2) + \frac{\mathbb{E}_p[(z-\mu)^2]}{2\sigma^2}\right].

Setting the derivatives to zero,

\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}: &\quad \frac{\mu - \mathbb{E}_p[z]}{\sigma^2} = 0 &&\Rightarrow\ \mu^* = \mathbb{E}_p[z], \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma^2}: &\quad \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} - \frac{\mathbb{E}_p[(z-\mu)^2]}{2\sigma^4} = 0 &&\Rightarrow\ \sigma^{2*} = \mathrm{Var}_p(z). \end{aligned}

So the M-projection matches the mean and variance of p. For the mixture,

\begin{aligned} \mu^* &= 0.3(-2) + 0.7(3) = 1.5, \\ \sigma^{2*} &= 0.3\big(0.8^2 + (-2-1.5)^2\big) + 0.7\big(1.1^2 + (3-1.5)^2\big) \approx 6.289, \end{aligned}

so q^*_{\text{M}} = \mathcal N(1.5,\ 2.51^2). The variance uses the rule that a mixture’s variance is the weighted average, over components, of each component’s variance plus its squared distance from the overall mean.

I-projection numerically. For the reverse KL the stationarity equations have no closed form when p is a mixture. The tutorial minimizes a Monte Carlo estimate of \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p) by gradient descent over (\mu, \log\sigma), sampling from q as z = \mu + \sigma\epsilon with \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,1) so that the samples are differentiable in (\mu,\sigma). It finds approximately q^*_{\text{I}} = \mathcal N(2.95,\ 1.17^2): almost exactly the large component \mathcal N(3,\ 1.1^2), ignoring the small one.

Figure 3. The tutorial’s target p = 0.3\,\mathcal N(-2, 0.8^2) + 0.7\,\mathcal N(3, 1.1^2) (shaded), its I-projection \mathcal N(2.95, 1.17^2) (solid), and its M-projection \mathcal N(1.5, 2.51^2) (dashed). Curves computed from these formulas for this figure.

The figure shows the two behaviours side by side. The I-projection is narrow and confident, and assigns almost no probability to the region near -2 where p has 30% of its mass. The M-projection covers both modes, but its peak sits at 1.5, in the valley between them, where p itself is small. Neither is “the right answer”: each is the best Gaussian under a different definition of best.

Note

Each fit wins its own contest. Computing both divergences for both fits by numerical integration (in nats, done for these notes; the tutorial’s own printed values are Monte Carlo estimates):

\mathrm{KL}(q\Vert p) \mathrm{KL}(p\Vert q)
I-projection \mathcal N(2.95,\ 1.17^2) 0.345 2.13
M-projection \mathcal N(1.5,\ 2.51^2) 0.528 0.320

The I-projection has the smaller reverse KL and the M-projection has the smaller forward KL, as their definitions require. The I-projection’s forward KL is large because it misses the small mode.

The reverse KL also has a second, worse local minimum. Started near the small mode, the same optimization settles on approximately \mathcal N(-1.92,\ 0.89^2), with reverse KL about 1.18. This is the 1D version of the slide’s two I-projection solutions. In the slide the two components look alike, so the two solutions are equally good. Here the components carry different mass, so the one on the larger component is better. In both cases the optimization is not convex, and which solution we reach depends on where we start.

Why variational inference uses the I-projection

Most variational inference algorithms use the I-projection. The reason is tractability. The M-projection’s objective \mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}[\log p(x)/q(x)] is an expectation over p. When p is the posterior, that expectation needs either samples from p(z\mid x) or its normalizing constant, the very things we cannot get. The I-projection’s objective is an expectation over q, which we chose to be easy to sample from and evaluate. It still contains \log p(z\mid x), but Tutorial: reverse KL and the ELBO shows that the troublesome constant \log p(x) separates out and does not affect the minimizer.

The lecture adds that the I-projection is closely related to maximizing the entropy of q,

H(q) = -\mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}[\log q(x)],

which measures the uncertainty in q. Mean field for an MRF makes this concrete: for an MRF target, minimizing \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p) becomes maximizing H(q) plus an expected-potential term.

The lecture mentions, without proof, a theorem connecting entropy to Week 1 (Exponential families in Week 1). The problem

\max_q\ H(q) \quad\text{subject to}\quad \mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}[f_i(x)] = t_i \quad\text{for } i=1,\dots,k

is always maximized by an exponential family, with the f_i as its sufficient statistics. This is the principle of maximum entropy: among all distributions with the prescribed averages of f_1,\dots,f_k, the least committal one, the one with the most uncertainty, has the exponential-family form \exp\{\sum_i\eta_i f_i(x) - A(\eta)\}.

Tutorial: reverse KL and the ELBO

The tutorial makes the tractability argument exact. Return to the VI setting, where the target is the posterior p(z\mid x) = p(x,z)/p(x) and the approximation q_\lambda(z) has parameters \lambda (for a Gaussian family, \lambda=(\mu,\Sigma)). Expand the reverse KL with \log p(z\mid x) = \log p(x,z) - \log p(x):

\begin{aligned} \mathrm{KL}\big(q_\lambda(z)\,\|\,p(z\mid x)\big) &= \mathbb{E}_{q_\lambda}\left[\log\frac{q_\lambda(z)}{p(z\mid x)}\right] \\ &= \mathbb{E}_{q_\lambda}\big[\log q_\lambda(z) - \log p(x,z) + \log p(x)\big] \\ &= \log p(x) - \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_\lambda}\big[\log p(x,z) - \log q_\lambda(z)\big]}_{\mathcal{L}(\lambda)}. \end{aligned}

The term \log p(x) came out of the expectation because it does not depend on z.

Definition. The evidence lower bound (ELBO) is \mathcal{L}(\lambda) = \mathbb{E}_{z\sim q_\lambda}\big[\log p(x,z) - \log q_\lambda(z)\big].

Rearranging the expansion,

\log p(x) = \mathcal{L}(\lambda) + \mathrm{KL}\big(q_\lambda(z)\,\|\,p(z\mid x)\big) \ \ge\ \mathcal{L}(\lambda),

since the KL divergence is nonnegative. This explains the name: \mathcal L is a lower bound on the log evidence \log p(x).

Since \log p(x) does not depend on \lambda, the two terms on the right trade off one for one as \lambda changes, and

\arg\max_\lambda\ \mathcal{L}(\lambda) = \arg\min_\lambda\ \mathrm{KL}\big(q_\lambda(z)\,\|\,p(z\mid x)\big).

Minimizing the reverse KL is the same as maximizing the ELBO. The ELBO needs only the joint p(x,z), which we can evaluate, and an expectation under q_\lambda, which we can estimate by sampling from q_\lambda. The intractable p(x) never appears. By contrast, estimating the forward KL \mathbb{E}_{p(z\mid x)}[\log p(z\mid x)/q_\lambda(z)] requires samples from p(z\mid x), which is the difficulty we started with. The lecture says more variational inference comes next week.

Mean field for an MRF

The lecture now applies the I-projection to a Markov random field from Week 3. In this section the target is the MRF distribution itself, written p(x\mid\theta) over variables x; there is no separate split into observed and latent variables. (If some variables were observed, conditioning on them gives another MRF over the rest, so the same method applies to a posterior.)

Write an arbitrary MRF in exponential form,

p(x\mid\theta) = \exp\Big\{\sum_{c\in C}\phi_c(x_c) - \log Z(\theta)\Big\},

where C is the set of cliques, x_c are the variables in clique c, and Z(\theta) is the partition function.

Here \phi_c denotes the whole log-potential of clique c, \phi_c(x_c) = \log\psi_c(x_c). This is not Week 3’s sufficient-statistic vector \phi_C (MRFs as exponential families in Week 3). In the log-linear case the two are related by \phi_c(x_c) = \theta_c^\top\phi_C(x_c), with the slide’s \phi_c on the left and Week 3’s on the right.

We look for q\in Q by I-projection to p. Substituting the MRF into the reverse KL,

\begin{aligned} \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p) &= \mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}\left[\log\frac{q(x)}{p(x\mid\theta)}\right] \\ &= \mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}\Big[\log q(x) - \sum_{c\in C}\phi_c(x_c) + \log Z(\theta)\Big] \\ &= -H(q) - \sum_{c\in C}\mathbb{E}_q[\phi_c(x_c)] + \log Z(\theta). \end{aligned}

The last term \log Z(\theta) does not depend on q, so it can be dropped from the optimization, and minimizing the result is the same as maximizing its negative:

q^* = \arg\min_{q\in Q}\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p) = \arg\max_{q\in Q}\ \Big[\sum_{c\in C}\mathbb{E}_q[\phi_c(x_c)] + H(q)\Big].

The objective balances two pulls. The expected-potential term pulls q toward configurations the MRF favours; the entropy term pulls q toward spreading out. The intractable \log Z has dropped out, exactly as \log p(x) did in Tutorial: reverse KL and the ELBO. Because the KL divergence is nonnegative, the bracketed objective is also a lower bound on \log Z(\theta) for every q, the MRF counterpart of the ELBO.

For tractability we still need a nice set Q: computing \mathbb{E}_q[\phi_c] and H(q) for an arbitrary q over many variables is as hard as the original problem.

Naive mean field

The simplest choice of Q makes every variable independent.

Definition. The naive mean-field approximation restricts Q to fully factorized distributions, q(x) = \prod_{i\in V} q_i(x_i), where V is the set of nodes (variables) and each q_i is a distribution over x_i alone.

Two quantities in the objective now simplify.

Expected potentials. The expectation of \phi_c involves only the variables in c, and under a factorized q their marginal is a product,

\mathbb{E}_q[\phi_c(x_c)] = \sum_{x_c} q(x_c)\,\phi_c(x_c), \qquad q(x_c) = \prod_{i\in c} q_i(x_i).

Entropy. The entropy of a product splits into a sum of single-variable entropies. Since \log q(x) = \sum_i\log q_i(x_i),

\begin{aligned} H(q) = -\sum_x q(x)\log q(x) &= -\sum_x q(x)\sum_i\log q_i(x_i) \\ &= -\sum_i\sum_{x_i} q_i(x_i)\log q_i(x_i)\sum_{x\setminus x_i}\frac{q(x)}{q_i(x_i)} \\ &= -\sum_i\sum_{x_i} q_i(x_i)\log q_i(x_i) = \sum_i H(q_i). \end{aligned}

The second line swaps the order of summation: for each i, sum first over x_i and then over all the other variables x\setminus x_i. The inner sum \sum_{x\setminus x_i} q(x)/q_i(x_i) = \sum_{x\setminus x_i}\prod_{k\ne i}q_k(x_k) is a product of distributions summed over all their values, which is 1.

Putting both together, the naive mean-field problem is

\begin{aligned} q^* = \arg\max_{q}\ &\sum_{c\in C}\sum_{x_c}\phi_c(x_c)\prod_{i\in c}q_i(x_i) + \sum_i H(q_i) \\ \text{subject to}\ \ & q_i(x_i)\ge0\ \text{ and }\ \sum_{x_i}q_i(x_i)=1\ \text{ for every } i. \end{aligned}

The constraints say each q_i must be a probability distribution. Every term now involves only a handful of small tables, one per variable, instead of a table over all joint configurations.

Pairwise MRFs and coordinate ascent

The lecture simplifies further to a pairwise MRF, whose potentials live on the edges E of the graph (Week 3’s Ising model, The Ising model in Week 3, is one). The problem becomes

\begin{aligned} q^* = \arg\max_q\ &\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\ \sum_{x_i,x_j}\phi_{ij}(x_i,x_j)\,q_i(x_i)\,q_j(x_j) - \sum_i\sum_{x_i}q_i(x_i)\log q_i(x_i) \\ \text{subject to}\ \ & q_i(x_i)\ge0\ \text{ and }\ \sum_{x_i}q_i(x_i)=1. \end{aligned}

This problem is hard in general: the objective is not concave in all the q_i jointly, and it has many local maxima. But it is easy in any one q_i with the others held fixed, which suggests optimizing one block at a time.

Lagrange multipliers

Maximizing over q_i alone is a constrained problem: q_i must sum to 1. The lecture’s one-line instruction, “write the Lagrangian, take the derivative, set to zero, and solve”, uses a standard tool.

Definition. To maximize f(u) subject to an equality constraint g(u)=0, the method of Lagrange multipliers introduces a new variable \lambda and the Lagrangian \mathcal{L}(u,\lambda) = f(u) + \lambda\,g(u). At a constrained optimum, the derivative of \mathcal L with respect to every coordinate of u is zero; \lambda is then chosen so that the constraint holds.

The multiplier lets us differentiate as if there were no constraint, then fix up the constraint at the end. In mean field, \lambda will turn out to be a normalizing constant.

The coordinate update

Fix every q_j with j\ne i and collect the terms of the objective that involve q_i. To keep the notation short, write \phi_{ij}(x_i,x_j) for the potential on edge \{i,j\} whichever way round the edge was listed, so \phi_{ji}(x_j,x_i)=\phi_{ij}(x_i,x_j), and let N(i) be the neighbours of i. Then

f(q_i) = \sum_{x_i}q_i(x_i)\sum_{j\in N(i)}\sum_{x_j}q_j(x_j)\,\phi_{ij}(x_i,x_j) - \sum_{x_i}q_i(x_i)\log q_i(x_i) + \text{const},

where “const” collects terms that do not involve q_i. Add a multiplier for \sum_{x_i}q_i(x_i) = 1 and differentiate with respect to one entry q_i(x_i):

\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial}{\partial q_i(x_i)}\Big[f(q_i) + \lambda\Big(\sum_{x_i'}q_i(x_i')-1\Big)\Big] \\ &\qquad= \sum_{j\in N(i)}\sum_{x_j}q_j(x_j)\,\phi_{ij}(x_i,x_j) - \log q_i(x_i) - 1 + \lambda. \end{aligned}

Setting this to zero and solving for q_i(x_i) gives q_i(x_i) = e^{\lambda-1}\exp\{\cdots\}. The factor e^{\lambda-1} is the same for every x_i, and choosing \lambda to satisfy the constraint makes it the normalizer:

q_i(x_i) \propto \exp\Big\{\sum_{j\in N(i)}\sum_{x_j}q_j(x_j)\,\phi_{ij}(x_i,x_j)\Big\}.

Each variable’s new distribution is an exponential of its potentials averaged over its neighbours’ current distributions. This is where the name “mean field” comes from: each neighbour x_j is replaced by its average effect under q_j. The exponential is positive, so the constraint q_i(x_i)\ge0 holds automatically.

Naive mean field by block coordinate ascent

  1. Initialize every q_i(x_i) to the uniform distribution.
  2. Iterate over the nodes i\in V. For each, greedily maximize the objective over q_i with the others fixed, which is the update q_i(x_i) \propto \exp\Big\{\sum_{j\in N(i)}\sum_{x_j}q_j(x_j)\,\phi_{ij}(x_i,x_j)\Big\}.
  3. Repeat until the q_i stop changing.

This is coordinate ascent in blocks: each step exactly maximizes over one block q_i. Each step can only increase the objective, which is bounded above by \log Z, so the procedure is guaranteed to converge. Because the full objective has many local maxima, it can converge to a local optimum rather than the best q, and different initializations can give different answers.

Note

Worked example: two binary variables. A teaching example with invented numbers. Take two variables x_1,x_2\in\{0,1\} joined by one edge, with potential \phi_{12}(x_1,x_2) = 2\,x_1x_2, so the MRF favours both variables being 1. Write m_i = q_i(1). The update for q_1 is

q_1(x_1)\propto\exp\Big\{\sum_{x_2}q_2(x_2)\cdot2x_1x_2\Big\} = \exp\{2x_1m_2\},

so q_1(1) = e^{2m_2}/(1+e^{2m_2}), which is the logistic function \sigma(2m_2). Likewise m_2\leftarrow\sigma(2m_1).

  • Start uniform: m_1=m_2=0.5.
  • Update q_1: m_1 = \sigma(1)\approx0.731.
  • Update q_2: m_2 = \sigma(1.462)\approx0.812.
  • Update q_1: m_1 = \sigma(1.624)\approx0.835. Then m_2\approx0.842.
  • After a few more sweeps both settle at m_1=m_2\approx0.844, where m=\sigma(2m).

Compare with the exact MRF. Its four configurations have unnormalized probabilities 1,1,1,e^2, so the exact marginal is p(x_1=1) = (1+e^2)/(3+e^2)\approx0.807. The mean-field marginal (0.844) is more confident than the truth. The factorized q also misplaces the joint: q(0,0) = (1-0.844)^2\approx0.024, while the exact p(0,0) = 1/(3+e^2)\approx0.096. This is the I-projection’s tendency from Which direction? I-projection and M-projection, under-covering the less probable configurations, showing up in a discrete model.

Summary

Note

Key takeaways from Week 6:

  • Hidden Markov models. An HMM is a latent Markov chain with observations: p(x_{1:T},z_{1:T}) = p(z_1)\prod_t p(z_t\mid z_{t-1})\prod_t p(x_t\mid z_t), specified by an initial distribution \pi, a transition matrix \Psi, and emission probabilities \psi_t.
  • Forward-backward. Filtering \alpha_t\propto\psi_t\odot(\Psi^\top\alpha_{t-1}) and the backward recursion \beta_t = \Psi(\psi_{t+1}\odot\beta_{t+1}) combine into the smoothed posteriors \gamma_t = p(z_t\mid x_{1:T})\propto\alpha_t\odot\beta_t.
  • Viterbi. Replacing the forward sum by a maximum, \delta_t(j) = \psi_t(j)\max_i\delta_{t-1}(i)\Psi(i,j), plus back-pointers and a traceback, gives the MAP hidden sequence. Both HMM algorithms cost on the order of TK^2 instead of K^T, because they follow the chain structure.
  • Why VI. In general latent variable models the evidence p(x) = \int p(x,z)\,dz is intractable, so the posterior is too. Variational inference approximates the posterior by the closest member of a tractable family Q.
  • KL divergence. \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p) = \mathbb{E}_q[\log q/p] is nonnegative, zero only when q=p, and not symmetric.
  • I- vs M-projection. Minimizing \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p) (I-projection) penalizes q for mass where p has none, so it is mode-seeking and underestimates support. Minimizing \mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q) (M-projection) penalizes q for missing mass, so it covers p and, for Gaussian Q, matches its mean and covariance. VI uses the I-projection because it needs only expectations under q; equivalently, it maximizes the ELBO.
  • Naive mean field. For an MRF, the I-projection maximizes \sum_c\mathbb{E}_q[\phi_c] + H(q). With a fully factorized q=\prod_iq_i the entropy splits as \sum_iH(q_i), and coordinate ascent updates q_i(x_i)\propto\exp\{\sum_{j\in N(i)}\sum_{x_j}q_j(x_j)\phi_{ij}(x_i,x_j)\}. It always converges, possibly to a local optimum.

High-level overview

This section retells each part of the week in plain language and without formulas, using the lecture’s own examples. Read it before the chapter for orientation, or after it as a review.

From Markov chains to hidden states (From Markov chains to hidden states). Often the sequence we care about is not directly visible. In speech, we hear the sounds, but the words producing them are hidden. A hidden Markov model keeps a Markov chain for the hidden part and lets each hidden state produce an observation.

Hidden Markov models (Hidden Markov models). Three ingredients: how the hidden chain starts, how it moves from one step to the next, and what each hidden state tends to produce as an observation.

Two inference questions (Two inference questions). Given the observations, we ask: at each time, how likely is each hidden state? And what is the single most likely hidden sequence overall?

The forward algorithm (The forward algorithm). Move from left to right, updating our belief about the current hidden state with each new observation. This is filtering: what we would know in real time.

The forward-backward algorithm (The forward-backward algorithm). Later observations also tell us about earlier states, the way hearing the rest of a sentence helps us make out a word we misheard. A backward pass gathers that later information, and combining both passes gives each state’s probability given the whole sequence. This is smoothing.

Worked example: a two-state HMM (Worked example: a two-state HMM). A two-state, three-step HMM worked through by hand.

The Viterbi algorithm (The Viterbi algorithm). To find the single best hidden sequence, replace “add up all paths” with “keep only the best path into each state” at every step. Remember where each best path came from, then trace back from the end. Both HMM algorithms take time proportional to the length of the sequence, instead of the astronomically many possible hidden sequences.

Posterior inference in latent variable models, and why it is hard (Posterior inference in latent variable models, What the TrueSkill posterior looks like, Why the posterior is hard). Outside chains and trees there is usually no neat recursion. For two TrueSkill players, before any games our uncertainty about their skills is a round blob centred at zero. After A beats B, the blob shifts toward “A is better than B”. Computing that shape exactly requires the total probability of the data, an integral over every hidden variable at once, which is intractable in high dimensions.

Variational methods (Variational methods). Instead of computing the exact posterior, choose a family of simple shapes (for example, all bell curves) and find the member closest to the posterior. Inference becomes an optimization problem.

Jensen’s inequality (Jensen’s inequality). A basic fact about curved functions. It guarantees that the closeness measure below is never negative.

The KL divergence (The KL divergence). A measure of how different two distributions are. It is zero only when they match, and it is lopsided: comparing q to p gives a different number from comparing p to q.

I-projection and M-projection (Which direction? I-projection and M-projection). Because KL is lopsided, there are two ways to fit. The tutorial fits a single bell curve to a distribution with two separate humps. One direction (the I-projection) picks one hump and fits it snugly, ignoring the other. The other direction (the M-projection) stretches a wide curve over both humps, which puts a lot of its mass in the empty gap between them.

Why variational inference uses the I-projection (Why variational inference uses the I-projection). The I-projection needs only averages under the simple approximation, which we can compute. The other direction needs averages under the true posterior, the very thing we could not compute in the first place.

Reverse KL and the ELBO (Tutorial: reverse KL and the ELBO). Rewriting the I-projection’s objective shows that the unknown total probability of the data drops out. What remains, the evidence lower bound (ELBO), can be maximized directly.

Mean field for an MRF, naive mean field, and coordinate ascent (Mean field for an MRF, Naive mean field, Pairwise MRFs and coordinate ascent). Approximate a Week 3 MRF by a distribution in which every variable is independent. Then update one variable at a time: each variable looks at its neighbours’ current average states and adjusts its own probabilities to fit in with them. For two linked yes/no variables that prefer both being 1, each variable’s chance of being 1 rises as the other’s does. Repeating the updates always converges, though possibly to an answer that is best only locally.
