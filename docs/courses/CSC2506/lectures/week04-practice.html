Problem 1: The log-prior of independent skills

Three players have independent normal priors z_i\sim\mathcal N(0,1). Two candidate skill settings are stored as the rows of a 2\times3 array:

\begin{pmatrix}0&1&-2\\0.5&0.5&0.5\end{pmatrix}.

  1. Compute the log-prior \log p(z_1,z_2,z_3) for each row.
  2. Why is the joint log-prior a sum, and along which axis of this array does that sum run? What shape is the result?
TipWorked solution

This uses the prior in the TrueSkill definition of Week 4, Latent variables and the TrueSkill model.

The standard normal log-density is \log\mathcal N(z;0,1)=-\tfrac12\log(2\pi)-\tfrac12z^2, and \tfrac12\log(2\pi)\approx0.9189. Because the priors are independent, \log p(z_1,z_2,z_3)=\sum_{i=1}^3\log\mathcal N(z_i;0,1)=-3\cdot\tfrac12\log(2\pi)-\tfrac12\big(z_1^2+z_2^2+z_3^2\big). Each of the three players contributes one -0.9189, so the constant part is -3(0.9189)=-2.7568 for every row.

  1. Row 1: the squares are 0^2,1^2,(-2)^2=0,1,4, so -2.7568-\tfrac12(0+1+4)=-2.7568-2.5=-5.257. Row 2: the squares are 3\times0.5^2=3\times0.25=0.75, so -2.7568-\tfrac12(0.75)=-3.132.
  2. Independence makes the joint prior a product \prod_ip(z_i), and the log of a product is a sum of logs. Each row is one setting of all players, so the sum runs across the players within a row (axis 1, the column axis), leaving one number per row: shape (2,). Summing down the columns instead would add the same player’s log-density across two different settings, which is not the log of any probability in the model.

Row 2 scores higher because all three skills sit near the prior mean of 0; row 1 pays for the player at -2.

Answer. -5.257 and -3.132. A sum because of independence, taken across players (axis 1), giving shape (2,).

Problem 2: Logs of sums without overflow

Double-precision floats overflow above about 1.8\times10^{308}, so e^{a} is stored as infinity once a exceeds roughly 709 (arithmetic overflow).

  1. Compute \log\big(e^{1000}+e^{998}\big) by hand, without ever forming e^{1000}.
  2. The function \mathrm{logaddexp}(u,v)=\log(e^u+e^v) is computed with the same trick. Write \log(1+e^{a}) using it. What does the naive formula log(1 + exp(a)) return at a=800, and what is the correct value to many digits?
TipWorked solution

This supports evaluating the logistic function of Week 4, Latent variables and the TrueSkill model in log space.

  1. Factor out the largest exponential (the LogSumExp trick). With m=\max(a,b), \log\big(e^{a}+e^{b}\big)=\log\big(e^{m}(e^{a-m}+e^{b-m})\big)=m+\log\big(e^{a-m}+e^{b-m}\big). Every exponent on the right is \le0, so nothing can overflow. Substituting a=1000, b=998, m=1000: \log\big(e^{1000}+e^{998}\big)=1000+\log\big(e^{0}+e^{-2}\big)=1000+\log(1.1353)=1000.1269. The only exponential left to evaluate is e^{-2}, which is harmless.
  2. Since 1=e^0, \log(1+e^{a})=\mathrm{logaddexp}(0,a). Naively, exp(800) overflows to infinity and the log of infinity is infinity. Factoring out e^{a} instead gives 800+\log(1+e^{-800}), and e^{-800} is so small that the answer is 800 to all printed digits. For very negative a the same trick avoids the opposite problem, where e^{a} underflows toward 0 and the log goes to -\infty.

Answer. (1) \approx1000.1269. (2) \log(1+e^a)=\mathrm{logaddexp}(0,a); the naive formula returns infinity at a=800, the true value is 800 (to double precision).

Problem 3: Looking up skills game by game

Four players have skills z = np.array([0.5, -1.0, 2.0, 0.0]). Five games are stored one per row as (winner index, loser index):

games = np.array([[2, 0],
                  [0, 1],
                  [3, 1],
                  [2, 3],
                  [1, 0]])

What does z[games[:, 0]] - z[games[:, 1]] evaluate to, and what does each entry mean in the model?

TipWorked solution

This sets up the “one factor per game” product of Week 4, Latent variables and the TrueSkill model.

  • games[:, 0] is the winner column [2, 0, 3, 2, 1], and indexing z with an integer array returns one entry per index: z[games[:, 0]] is [2.0, 0.5, 0.0, 2.0, -1.0].
  • games[:, 1] is [0, 1, 1, 3, 0], so z[games[:, 1]] is [0.5, -1.0, -1.0, 0.0, 0.5].
  • The elementwise difference has shape (5,), one entry per game.

Each entry is z_{\text{winner}}-z_{\text{loser}}, the argument of \sigma in that game’s likelihood factor. Positive entries are games the stronger player won. The last entry, -1.5, is an upset: player 1 beat a player rated higher, so its factor \sigma(-1.5)\approx0.18 is small. Player indices may repeat across rows, which is how several games between the same pair, or one player in many games, are handled with no loop.

Answer. array([ 1.5, 1.5, 1. , 2. , -1.5]): the winner-minus-loser skill gap for each game.

Problem 4: A two-player posterior up to a constant

Two players A and B have independent priors z_A,z_B\sim\mathcal N(0,1) and the course’s TrueSkill likelihood p(A\text{ beats }B\mid z_A,z_B)=\sigma(z_A-z_B). They play four games: A wins three and B wins one.

  1. Write \log p(z_A,z_B,\text{games}).
  2. Evaluate it, dropping the constant -\log(2\pi), at (z_A,z_B)=(0,0), (0.5,-0.5), and (1.5,0.5). Which is most probable under the posterior?
  3. Why can we draw the posterior’s contour lines from \log p(z_A,z_B,\text{games}) without ever computing p(\text{games})?
  4. Change variables to d=z_A-z_B and s=z_A+z_B. Show that the likelihood depends only on d, and that the prior factorizes into a function of d times a function of s. What does that say about what the games can teach us?
TipWorked solution

This uses the joint distribution in Week 4, Latent variables and the TrueSkill model and the unnormalized-density setting of Sampling from an unnormalized density.

  1. Prior times one factor per game, with games independent given the skills: \log p=-\log(2\pi)-\tfrac12\big(z_A^2+z_B^2\big) {}+3\log\sigma(z_A-z_B)+\log\sigma(z_B-z_A).
  2. Since \sigma(y)=1/(1+e^{-y}), \log\sigma(y)=-\log(1+e^{-y}). Write d=z_A-z_B, so the likelihood part is 3\log\sigma(d)+\log\sigma(-d) and the prior part (without the constant) is -\tfrac12(z_A^2+z_B^2). The values we need are \log\sigma(0)=-\log2=-0.6931, \log\sigma(1)=-\log(1+e^{-1})=-0.3133, and \log\sigma(-1)=-\log(1+e)=-1.3133.
    • (0,0): d=0, likelihood 3(-0.6931)+(-0.6931)=-2.7726, prior -\tfrac12(0+0)=0; total -2.773.
    • (0.5,-0.5): d=1, likelihood 3(-0.3133)-1.3133=-2.2530, prior -\tfrac12(0.25+0.25)=-0.25; total -2.503.
    • (1.5,0.5): d=1 again, so the same likelihood -2.2530, prior -\tfrac12(2.25+0.25)=-1.25; total -3.503.
    (0.5,-0.5) is the most probable of the three.
  3. p(z_A,z_B\mid\text{games})=p(z_A,z_B,\text{games})/p(\text{games}), and the denominator does not depend on (z_A,z_B). Dividing by a constant, or subtracting a constant in log space, shifts every height by the same amount, so the level sets are the same curves; only their labels change.
  4. The likelihood is a function of z_A-z_B=d alone. For the prior, z_A=\tfrac{s+d}{2} and z_B=\tfrac{s-d}{2}, so z_A^2+z_B^2=\tfrac14\big[(s+d)^2+(s-d)^2\big]=\tfrac12\big(s^2+d^2\big), and the prior is proportional to e^{-d^2/4}\,e^{-s^2/4}. (The change of variables also brings a constant Jacobian factor, which does not affect the shape.) The posterior is therefore \big[e^{-d^2/4}\times\text{likelihood}(d)\big]\times e^{-s^2/4}: the games update our belief about the skill difference, while the belief about the sum stays exactly at the prior. Part 2 shows this: (0.5,-0.5) and (1.5,0.5) share d=1 and get the same likelihood, and only the prior separates them.

Answer. (2) -2.773, -2.503, -3.503; (0.5,-0.5) is highest. (3) The normalizer is a constant shift. (4) Likelihood in d only, prior \propto e^{-d^2/4}e^{-s^2/4}, so data inform d and leave s at its prior.

Problem 5: Why leaves go first

A tree has a hub node h joined to five leaves a,b,c,d,e, and nothing else. Every variable takes k=4 values. The MRF has one potential per node and one per edge, nothing is observed, and we want the normalizer Z by variable elimination.

  1. Suppose we sum out the hub x_h first. Which factors does that sum touch, and over how many variables is the intermediate factor it produces? How many entries does that factor have?
  2. Now sum out the five leaves first, one at a time, and the hub last. Over how many variables is each intermediate factor, and how many entries does it have?
  3. Roughly how many multiply-adds does each order cost? Compare the leaf-first count with the O(nk^2) rate from the week chapter.
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 4, Trees and elimination order.

  1. Every edge potential mentions x_h, so the sum over x_h has to wrap \psi_h and all five edge potentials \psi_{ha},\dots,\psi_{he}. What is left after summing is a function of all five leaves at once, \tau(x_a,x_b,x_c,x_d,x_e), with 4^5=1024 entries. This is the “intermediate factor over many variables” the week chapter warns about.
  2. Summing out a leaf, say x_a, touches only \psi_a(x_a) and \psi_{ha}(x_h,x_a), because no other factor mentions x_a. The result \tau_a(x_h)=\sum_{x_a}\psi_a(x_a)\,\psi_{ha}(x_h,x_a) is a function of x_h alone: 4 entries. The same holds for every leaf. After all five, the hub’s sum \sum_{x_h}\psi_h(x_h)\prod_\ell\tau_\ell(x_h) is a single number.
  3. Hub first: \tau has 4^5=1024 entries, and each entry is a sum of 4 terms (one per value of x_h), so filling it takes about 1024\times4=4^6=4096 multiply-adds, before we even start on the leaves. Leaves first: each \tau_\ell has 4 entries, each a sum of 4 terms, so 16 per leaf and 80 for the five, plus 4 for the hub’s final sum. That is about 84, in line with nk^2=6\cdot16=96. No step ever involved more than two variables at once.

Answer. (1) A factor over all five leaves, 4^5=1024 entries. (2) Each leaf leaves a factor over x_h only, 4 entries. (3) About 4096 against about 84: leaves first is the cheap order.

Problem 6: Pushing sums inward on a different tree

A four-node tree has edges 1\text{-}2, 2\text{-}3, 2\text{-}4, so node 2 is the centre. All variables are binary, x_i\in\{0,1\}. Nodes 1 and 4 are observed, and we want p(x_3\mid\bar x_1,\bar x_4). With the evidence plugged in, the potentials are:

Potential Value at 0 Value at 1
\psi_2(x_2) 2 1
\psi_3(x_3) 1 3
\psi_{12}(\bar x_1,x_2), as a function of x_2 3 1
\psi_{24}(x_2,\bar x_4), as a function of x_2 1 2

The observed node potentials are plain numbers, \psi_1(\bar x_1)=2 and \psi_4(\bar x_4)=1. The edge between the two unobserved nodes is \psi_{23}(x_2,x_3)=2 when x_2=x_3 and 1 otherwise.

  1. Name the evidence x_E, the query x_F, and the variables x_R that get summed out.
  2. Write p(x_3\mid\bar x_1,\bar x_4) as a normalized sum over x_2, then group the factors into m_{1\to2}(x_2), m_{4\to2}(x_2), and m_{2\to3}(x_3).
  3. Compute the three messages, the unnormalized belief \tilde b(x_3), and p(x_3=1\mid\bar x_1,\bar x_4).
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 4, Worked example: elimination on a tree becomes message passing. The shape differs from the lecture’s tree: here the one summed-out node sits in the middle, between both pieces of evidence and the query.

  1. x_E=\{x_1,x_4\}, x_F=\{x_3\}, x_R=\{x_2\}.
  2. The joint is the product of all seven potentials. Summing out x_2 and normalizing over x_3: p(x_3\mid\bar x_1,\bar x_4)=\frac{1}{Z^E}\,\psi_3(x_3)\sum_{x_2}\psi_2(x_2)\,\psi_{23}(x_2,x_3) {}\times\big[\psi_1(\bar x_1)\psi_{12}(\bar x_1,x_2)\big]\big[\psi_4(\bar x_4)\psi_{24}(x_2,\bar x_4)\big]. The first bracket involves only x_2: it is m_{1\to2}(x_2). The second is m_{4\to2}(x_2). The whole sum over x_2 is a function of x_3 alone, which is m_{2\to3}(x_3).
  3. Observed senders plug in their value, so m_{1\to2}=2\cdot(3,\ 1)=(6,\ 2),\qquad m_{4\to2}=1\cdot(1,\ 2)=(1,\ 2). Node 2 multiplies its own potential by both incoming messages, one number per value of x_2: 2\cdot6\cdot1=12 at x_2=0 and 1\cdot2\cdot2=4 at x_2=1. Then it multiplies by the edge and sums out x_2: m_{2\to3}(0)=12\cdot2+4\cdot1=28,\qquad m_{2\to3}(1)=12\cdot1+4\cdot2=20. The belief is \tilde b(x_3)=\psi_3(x_3)\,m_{2\to3}(x_3)=(28,\ 60), so Z^E=88 and p(x_3=1\mid\bar x_1,\bar x_4)=\frac{60}{88}=\frac{15}{22}\approx0.682. As a check, listing the four terms of the sum directly gives 24+4=28 at x_3=0 and 36+24=60 at x_3=1. The messages did the same sum in pieces.

Answer. (1) x_E=\{x_1,x_4\}, x_F=\{x_3\}, x_R=\{x_2\}. (3) m_{1\to2}=(6,2), m_{4\to2}=(1,2), m_{2\to3}=(28,20), \tilde b=(28,60), p(x_3=1\mid\bar x_1,\bar x_4)=15/22\approx0.682.

Problem 7: Sum-product on a three-node star

Three binary spins x_1,x_2,x_3\in\{+1,-1\} form a star with centre x_2: the edges are 1\text{-}2 and 2\text{-}3. The potentials are

  • \psi_1(+1)=1, \psi_1(-1)=2, and \psi_2=\psi_3=1;
  • \psi_{12}(x_1,x_2)=3 if x_1=x_2 and 1 otherwise;
  • \psi_{23}(x_2,x_3)=2 if x_2=x_3 and 1 otherwise.

We observe \bar x_3=+1.

  1. Compute m_{1\to2}(x_2) and m_{3\to2}(x_2), then the belief b(x_2)=p(x_2\mid\bar x_3).
  2. Check part 1 by writing out the unnormalized joint \tilde p(x_1,x_2,\bar x_3) for all four settings of (x_1,x_2).
  3. To get b(x_1), which extra message is needed? Compute it and the belief.
TipWorked solution

This uses the message and belief definitions in Week 4, Messages and beliefs, including the rule for an observed sender.

  1. Node 1 is an unobserved leaf, so it sums over its own value: m_{1\to2}(+1)=\psi_1(+1)\cdot3+\psi_1(-1)\cdot1=3+2=5, m_{1\to2}(-1)=\psi_1(+1)\cdot1+\psi_1(-1)\cdot3=1+6=7. Node 3 is observed, so its value is plugged in rather than summed: m_{3\to2}(x_2)=\psi_3(+1)\,\psi_{23}(x_2,+1), which is 2 at x_2=+1 and 1 at x_2=-1. The belief multiplies both in with \psi_2=1: \tilde b(x_2)=(5\cdot2,\ 7\cdot1)=(10,\ 7),\qquad b(x_2)=\big(\tfrac{10}{17},\ \tfrac{7}{17}\big)\approx(0.588,\ 0.412). The observation x_3=+1 pulls x_2 toward +1, while node 1’s preference for -1 pulls the other way through a stronger edge. The observation wins, narrowly.

  2. \tilde p(x_1,x_2,\bar x_3)=\psi_1(x_1)\,\psi_{12}(x_1,x_2)\,\psi_{23}(x_2,+1):

    (x_1,x_2) (+1,+1) (-1,+1) (+1,-1) (-1,-1)
    \tilde p 1\cdot3\cdot2=6 2\cdot1\cdot2=4 1\cdot1\cdot1=1 2\cdot3\cdot1=6

    Summing over x_1 gives 10 at x_2=+1 and 7 at x_2=-1, the same as \tilde b(x_2). The messages did this sum in pieces.

  3. Node 1 has one neighbour, node 2, so it needs m_{2\to1}. Node 2 multiplies in what it heard from its other neighbour, node 3, and sums out x_2: m_{2\to1}(+1)=3\cdot2+1\cdot1=7,\qquad m_{2\to1}(-1)=1\cdot2+3\cdot1=5. Then \tilde b(x_1)=(\psi_1(+1)\cdot7,\ \psi_1(-1)\cdot5)=(7,\ 10), so b(x_1)\approx(0.412,\ 0.588). The table agrees: 6+1=7 at x_1=+1 and 4+6=10 at x_1=-1.

Answer. (1) m_{1\to2}=(5,7), m_{3\to2}=(2,1), b(x_2)=(10/17,\ 7/17). (2) The table sums to the same (10,7). (3) m_{2\to1}=(7,5), b(x_1)=(7/17,\ 10/17).

Problem 8: What goes into a message

Node j in a tree-shaped MRF has three neighbours: i, k, and l.

  1. Write m_{j\to i}(x_i) out in full. Which incoming messages appear in it, and which one is left out?
  2. Now suppose x_j is observed with value \bar x_j. The messages m_{k\to j} and m_{l\to j} still appear in the formula. As functions of x_i, how do they affect m_{j\to i}? What does that say about how much node i learns from the far side of j?
  3. A classmate says the message (0.2,\ 0.9) must be a bug because its entries do not sum to 1. Are they right?
  4. Leaf j is unobserved, with \psi_j=(1,1) and binary edge potential \psi_{ij}=2 when x_i=x_j and 1 otherwise. Compute m_{j\to i}. What does it tell node i?
TipWorked solution

This uses the message definition and the remarks after it in Week 4, Messages and beliefs.

  1. m_{j\to i}(x_i)=\sum_{x_j}\psi_j(x_j)\,\psi_{ij}(x_i,x_j)\,m_{k\to j}(x_j)\,m_{l\to j}(x_j). The messages from k and l appear. The message m_{i\to j} is left out, because node i already knows what it sent.
  2. With x_j fixed, the formula becomes \psi_j(\bar x_j)\,\psi_{ij}(x_i,\bar x_j)\,m_{k\to j}(\bar x_j)\,m_{l\to j}(\bar x_j). The two incoming messages are each evaluated at one value, so they are just numbers: they scale the whole message by a constant and do not change its shape as a function of x_i. After normalizing, m_{j\to i} depends only on \psi_{ij}(x_i,\bar x_j). So node i learns nothing from beyond j once x_j is observed, which is the graph-separation picture of conditional independence in an MRF.
  3. No. A message is a nonnegative score per state, like a potential, not a probability distribution. Only the ratio 0.9/0.2=4.5 matters, and rescaling never changes a normalized belief.
  4. m_{j\to i}(x_i)=\sum_{x_j}\psi_{ij}(x_i,x_j) gives 2+1=3 at both values of x_i. The message is flat, so it multiplies every belief entry by the same 3 and tells node i nothing. A leaf with no preference and a symmetric edge has no opinion to pass on.

Answer. (1) m_{k\to j} and m_{l\to j} appear; m_{i\to j} is excluded. (2) They become constants, so the far side has no effect on node i. (3) No: messages need not sum to 1. (4) (3,\ 3), a flat message carrying no information.

Problem 9: Scheduling messages on a tree

An undirected graph has six nodes and the edges 1\text{-}2,\quad 2\text{-}3,\quad 2\text{-}4,\quad 4\text{-}5,\quad 4\text{-}6.

  1. Check that the graph is a tree and list its leaves. Would it still be a tree if we added the edge 1\text{-}3?
  2. How many messages does belief propagation send in total?
  3. Which messages can be computed before any other message exists?
  4. Which incoming messages must node 4 hold before it can send m_{4\to2}? Which must node 2 hold before it can send m_{2\to1}?
  5. With node 2 as the root, list the messages of the inward pass and of the outward pass.
TipWorked solution

This uses the tree definition in Week 4, Trees and elimination order and the two-pass schedule in Belief propagation on trees.

  1. Every node is reachable from node 2 (1, 3, 4 directly; 5, 6 through 4), so the graph is connected. A connected graph on 6 nodes with no cycles has exactly 5 edges, and this one has 5, so it has no cycles and is a tree. The leaves, the nodes with exactly one neighbour, are 1, 3, 5, and 6. Adding 1\text{-}3 closes the cycle 1\text{-}2\text{-}3\text{-}1, so the graph would no longer be a tree.
  2. One message in each direction along each edge: 2|\mathcal E|=2\cdot5=10.
  3. A node can send to i once it has heard from every neighbour other than i. A leaf has no other neighbours, so the product in the message formula is empty and it can send right away: m_{1\to2}, m_{3\to2}, m_{5\to4}, m_{6\to4}. Nodes 2 and 4 each have at least two neighbours they have not yet heard from, so they must wait.
  4. N(4)\setminus\{2\}=\{5,6\}, so node 4 needs m_{5\to4} and m_{6\to4}. N(2)\setminus\{1\}=\{3,4\}, so node 2 needs m_{3\to2} and m_{4\to2}. The second of these itself waited on nodes 5 and 6, which is how m_{2\to1} ends up summarizing the whole subtree on node 2’s side.
  5. Inward: m_{1\to2}, m_{3\to2}, m_{5\to4}, m_{6\to4}, then m_{4\to2}. Outward: m_{2\to1}, m_{2\to3}, m_{2\to4}, then m_{4\to5}, m_{4\to6}. That is 5+5=10 messages, matching part 2.

Answer. (1) A tree with leaves 1, 3, 5, 6; adding 1\text{-}3 makes a cycle. (2) 10. (3) The four leaf messages. (4) Node 4 needs messages from 5 and 6; node 2 needs messages from 3 and 4. (5) As listed above.

Problem 10: Why not just run elimination once per node?

Problem 11: True or false on loopy BP

Say whether each statement is true or false, with a one-line reason.

  1. On a tree, running the loopy BP updates until they stop changing gives the exact marginals.
  2. On a graph with cycles, loopy BP always converges if we run enough iterations.
  3. With plain updates (no momentum), rescaling each new message so that it sums to 1 does not change the normalized beliefs.
  4. On a graph with cycles, loopy BP beliefs are often more peaked than the true marginals.
  5. Setting \alpha=1 in the momentum update gives back the plain update.
  6. If a node’s message to i also multiplied in the message i had sent it, beliefs on a tree would still be exact.
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 4, Loopy belief propagation and the exclusion rule in Messages and beliefs.

  1. True. On a tree the message updates are the same as BP, and the lecture states loopy BP is exact on trees.
  2. False. The lecture says it may not converge at all, and messages can oscillate.
  3. True. Each update is a sum of products that is linear in every incoming message, so a constant factor on one message passes through as a constant factor on later messages and beliefs. Normalizing the belief removes it, as in the three-node chain example. With momentum, scale does matter: blending (1-\alpha)\,m^{\text{old}}+\alpha\,[\text{update}] weights the two vectors by their sizes as well as by \alpha. That is one reason to normalize before blending.
  4. True. Evidence that leaves a node along one edge of a cycle can return along another and be multiplied in again, like hearing your own rumour back.
  5. True. (1-1)\,m^{\text{old}}+1\cdot[\text{update}]=[\text{update}].
  6. False. Take the chain x_1\text{-}x_2. The modified message would be m_{2\to1}(x_1)=\sum_{x_2}\psi_2(x_2)\,\psi_{12}(x_1,x_2)\,m_{1\to2}(x_2), and m_{1\to2}(x_2)=\sum_{x_1'}\psi_1(x_1')\,\psi_{12}(x_1',x_2) is built from node 1’s own potential. Worse, each message would need the other before it could be computed, so on a tree the schedule could never start. The exclusion k\ne i is what stops a node’s own information from being counted twice.

Problem 13: What the step size does

Loopy BP on a cycle can oscillate. Take a toy version: a single message (p,\ 1-p) whose freshly computed update is always the flipped vector (1-p,\ p). Start from p=0.8 and apply the momentum update m^{\text{new}}=(1-\alpha)\,m^{\text{old}}+\alpha\,[\text{update}].

TipWorked solution

This uses the momentum trick in Week 4, Loopy belief propagation.

  1. The first entry updates as p^{\text{new}}=(1-\alpha)p+\alpha(1-p).
    • \alpha=1 (plain replacement): 0.8\to0.2\to0.8\to0.2. It never settles.
    • \alpha=0.5: 0.5\,(0.8)+0.5\,(0.2)=0.5, then 0.5 forever.

    • B: p(B=1\mid A=1)=0.7 and 0.81\ge0.7, so B=0.
    • C: p(C=1\mid A=1)=0.1 and 0.05<0.1, so C=1.
    • D: p(D=1\mid B=0,C=1)=0.5 and 0.47<0.5, so D=1.
    The sample is (A,B,C,D)=(1,0,1,1). A DAG’s joint factorizes as p(A)\,p(B\mid A)\,p(C\mid A)\,p(D\mid B,C), so its probability is the product of the four factors used, each read at the values drawn: p(A=1)\,p(B=0\mid A=1)\,p(C=1\mid A=1)\,p(D=1\mid B=0,C=1)=0.3\cdot(1-0.7)\cdot0.1\cdot0.5=0.0045. The second factor is 0.3 because B came out 0, so we need 1-p(B=1\mid A=1).
  3. No. D is drawn last, from its parents, so its value is an output of the procedure, not something we can fix in advance. Nothing in the procedure sets D=1 and then updates A. The lecture notes that ancestral sampling draws from the joint with nothing observed and does not directly give conditional samples.

Answer. (1) (a) and (b). (2) (1,0,1,1), probability 0.0045. (3) No: the observed variable is a child, drawn last.

Problem 21: Picking the test function

A random variable takes values x\in\{0,1,2,3\} with probabilities p=(0.1,\ 0.4,\ 0.3,\ 0.2). For each question, write the test function \phi whose expectation answers it, then compute the exact answer.

  1. The mean of x.
  2. The variance of x.
  3. \mathbb P(x\ge2).
  4. \mathbb P(x\text{ is odd and }x\ge2).
  5. Apply the variance recipe of part 2 to the test function of part 3. What do you get, and why is the formula so short?
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 4, What sampling is for.

  1. \phi_1(x)=x: \Phi_1=0(0.1)+1(0.4)+2(0.3)+3(0.2)=1.6.
  2. The lecture’s recipe adds a second test function \phi_2(x)=x^2: \Phi_2=0^2(0.1)+1^2(0.4)+2^2(0.3)+3^2(0.2)=0+0.4+1.2+1.8=3.4, so the variance is \Phi_2-\Phi_1^2=3.4-1.6^2=3.4-2.56=0.84. Once the mean is known, the single test function \phi(x)=(x-1.6)^2 gives the same answer directly: 2.56(0.1)+0.36(0.4)+0.16(0.3)+1.96(0.2)=0.84.
  3. The indicator function \phi(x)=\mathbf 1_{\{x\ge2\}}: its expectation is 0.3+0.2=0.5.
  4. \phi(x)=\mathbf 1_{\{x\text{ odd}\}}\cdot\mathbf 1_{\{x\ge2\}}. A product of indicators is 1 only when both events hold, so it is the indicator of the intersection, here \{3\}: the answer is 0.2.
  5. An indicator only takes the values 0 and 1, so it equals its own square, and \Phi_2=\Phi_1=0.5. The variance is 0.5-0.5^2=0.25, the familiar p(1-p). This is the quantity that sets how many samples a probability estimate needs.

Answer. (1) 1.6. (2) 0.84. (3) 0.5. (4) 0.2. (5) 0.25=p(1-p), because an indicator squared is itself.

Problem 22: A probability from posterior samples

Suppose some sampler has produced eight draws from a joint posterior over two players’ skills (z_i,z_j):

r 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8
z_i 1.2 0.4 1.5 0.9 0.3 1.1 1.8 0.7
z_j 0.8 0.9 1.1 0.2 0.6 1.3 0.7 0.4
  1. Estimate \mathbb P(z_i\ge z_j\mid\text{games}).
  2. Estimate the posterior mean and standard deviation of z_i.
  3. If the draws were independent, roughly how uncertain is the estimate in part 1?
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 4, What sampling is for and Simple Monte Carlo.

  1. A probability is the expectation of an indicator function: \mathbb P(z_i\ge z_j)=\mathbb E\big[\mathbf 1_{\{z_i\ge z_j\}}\big]. The simple Monte Carlo estimate is the fraction of samples where the event holds. Row by row the indicator is 1,0,1,1,0,0,1,1, so the estimate is 5/8=0.625.
  2. Mean: average the test function \phi_1=z_i over the draws. The z_i row sums to 1.2+0.4+1.5+0.9+0.3+1.1+1.8+0.7=7.9, so \hat\Phi_1=7.9/8=0.9875. For the spread, use the second test function \phi_2=z_i^2. The squares are 1.44,\ 0.16,\ 2.25,\ 0.81,\ 0.09,\ 1.21,\ 3.24,\ 0.49, summing to 9.69, so \hat\Phi_2=9.69/8=1.2113, so the variance estimate is \hat\Phi_2-\hat\Phi_1^2=1.2113-0.9752=0.2361 and the standard deviation is 0.486.
  3. The indicator has variance p(1-p), so with independent draws \mathrm{var}[\hat\Phi]=p(1-p)/R\approx0.625\cdot0.375/8=0.029, a standard deviation of about 0.17. Eight samples cannot pin the probability down. The 1/R rate assumes independent draws; samplers that produce each draw from the previous one give correlated samples, and then the error shrinks more slowly than this formula says.

Answer. (1) 0.625. (2) Mean 0.9875, standard deviation \approx0.486. (3) About \pm0.17 (one standard deviation) for independent draws.

Problem 23: How many samples?

  1. A test function has \mathrm{var}[\phi(x)]=9 under p. How many independent samples does simple Monte Carlo need for the standard deviation of \hat\Phi to be at most 0.1? At most 0.05?
  2. We want \mathbb P(x\in A) to within a standard deviation of 0.01, using the indicator function as the test function. TipWorked solution

This uses the variance formula in Week 4, Simple Monte Carlo.

  1. \mathrm{var}[\hat\Phi]=\mathrm{var}[\phi]/R=9/R. A standard deviation of 0.1 means variance 0.01, so R\ge900. Halving the standard deviation to 0.05 means variance 0.0025, so R\ge3600. Halving the error costs four times the samples.

Problem 24: An estimator small enough to list completely

A biased coin gives x=1 with probability 0.3 and x=0 otherwise. We estimate \Phi=\mathbb E[x]=0.3 by simple Monte Carlo with \phi(x)=x and only R=2 independent draws.

  1. List every possible pair of draws, its probability, and the value of \hat\Phi.
  2. From that list, compute \mathbb E[\hat\Phi] and \mathrm{var}[\hat\Phi]. Compare them with \Phi and with \mathrm{var}[\phi(x)]/R.
  3. What is the probability that \hat\Phi equals 0.3 exactly? Does that contradict part 2?
  4. Suppose a bug made the second draw a copy of the first. What is \mathrm{var}[\hat\Phi] now?
TipWorked solution

This uses the unbiasedness and variance results of Week 4, Simple Monte Carlo.

  1. Draws are independent, so probabilities multiply:

    Draws (0,0) (0,1) (1,0) (1,1)
    Probability 0.49 0.21 0.21 0.09
    \hat\Phi 0 0.5 0.5 1

  2. \mathbb E[\hat\Phi]=0.42(0.5)+0.09(1)=0.30=\Phi: unbiased, as promised. For the variance, \mathbb E[\hat\Phi^2]=0.42(0.25)+0.09(1)=0.195, so \mathrm{var}[\hat\Phi]=0.195-0.09=0.105. The single-draw variance is \mathrm{var}[\phi]=0.3\cdot0.7=0.21, and 0.21/2=0.105. The formula holds exactly.

  3. Zero: \hat\Phi can only be 0, 0.5, or 1. No contradiction. Unbiased means the estimates are right on average over repetitions, not that any one estimate is right. The variance measures how far a typical estimate lands from \Phi.

  4. With a copy, \hat\Phi=\tfrac12(x+x)=x, so \mathrm{var}[\hat\Phi]=0.21, no better than one draw. The 1/R in the formula came from adding the variances of independent terms; a copied draw adds no new information.

Answer. (2) \mathbb E[\hat\Phi]=0.3, \mathrm{var}[\hat\Phi]=0.105=0.21/2. (3) Probability 0, consistent with unbiasedness. (4) 0.21.

Problem 26: The canyon a boat never finds

On the unit cube [0,1]^D, a density puts 90% of its mass inside the small corner cube [0,\tfrac12]^D and spreads the other 10% thinly over the rest. We know nothing about where the mass is, so we place “boat” locations uniformly at random in the cube, as in the lecture’s lake picture.

  1. What fraction f of the cube’s volume is the corner cube, for D=1, D=10, and D=30?
  2. With R=1000 uniform draws, what is the probability that none lands in the corner cube, for D=10 and D=30?
  3. We estimate Z=\int\tilde p by the average of \tilde p over the uniform draws (the cube has volume 1). If no draw lands in the corner cube, roughly what will the estimate be, compared with the true Z? Would anything in the output warn us?
TipWorked solution

This uses the lake analogy in Week 4, Why the normalizing constant is hard.

  1. f=(\tfrac12)^D: 0.5 for D=1, 1/1024\approx9.8\times10^{-4} for D=10, and (\tfrac12)^{30}\approx9.3\times10^{-10} for D=30.
  2. Each draw misses with probability 1-f, independently, so all miss with probability (1-f)^{R} with R=1000. For small f, \log(1-f)\approx-f, so (1-f)^R\approx e^{-Rf}. For D=10, Rf=1000/1024\approx0.977 and the probability is about e^{-0.977}\approx0.38. For D=30, Rf=1000\times9.3\times10^{-10}=9.3\times10^{-7}, so e^{-Rf}\approx1-Rf=1-9.3\times10^{-7}: essentially certain.
  3. The average of \tilde p is a simple Monte Carlo estimate of \int\tilde p under the uniform distribution. If every draw misses the corner, the draws only see the thin 10% part, so the estimate comes out near 0.1\,Z, ten times too small. Nothing in the output flags this: the sampled values look perfectly ordinary, because the method never visited the place where it went wrong. This is the narrow, deep canyon from the lecture, holding most of the water and almost never visited.

Answer. (1) 0.5, about 10^{-3}, about 10^{-9}. (2) About 0.38 and essentially 1. (3) Near 0.1\,Z, with no warning sign.

Problem 27: Importance sampling with an unnormalized target

A target on \{1,2,3,4\} is known only up to a constant: \tilde p=(1,\ 2,\ 4,\ 1). The proposal is uniform, \tilde q(x)=1 for every x. Five draws from q come out as 1,\quad 3,\quad 3,\quad 2,\quad 4.

  1. Compute the importance weights \tilde w_r and the normalized weights w_r.
  2. Estimate \mathbb E_p[x] and compare it with the exact value.
  3. Estimate Z_p/Z_q and compare it with the exact value.
  4. Suppose instead \tilde q=(1,\ 1,\ 0.05,\ 1). What weight would a draw of x=3 get, and why is that a problem?
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 4, Importance sampling.

  1. \tilde w_r=\tilde p(x^{(r)})/\tilde q(x^{(r)})=\tilde p(x^{(r)}) here, giving 1,4,4,2,1. They sum to 12, so w_r=\tfrac1{12},\tfrac4{12},\tfrac4{12},\tfrac2{12},\tfrac1{12}.
  2. The self-normalized estimate is \sum_r x^{(r)}w_r=(1\cdot1+3\cdot4+3\cdot4+2\cdot2+4\cdot1)/12=33/12=2.75. Exactly, Z_p=\sum_x\tilde p(x)=1+2+4+1=8 and \mathbb E_p[x]=(1\cdot1+2\cdot2+3\cdot4+4\cdot1)/8=21/8=2.625. Five samples land fairly close.
  3. The average weight estimates Z_p/Z_q: 12/5=2.4. Exactly, Z_p=8 and Z_q=4, so the ratio is 2. Neither normalizer was needed to form the estimate in part 2; the ratio cancels in the self-normalized form.
  4. \tilde w=4/0.05=80, against at most 2 for the other values. The proposal rarely visits x=3, which holds half of p’s mass, so a run either misses it (and underestimates \mathbb E_p[x]) or hits it once and lets that one sample dominate the sum. This is the poor-coverage failure the week chapter warns about.

Answer. (1) \tilde w=(1,4,4,2,1), w=(1,4,4,2,1)/12. (2) 2.75 against 2.625. (3) 2.4 against 2. (4) Weight 80: one rare draw dominates.

Problem 28: Choosing between two proposals

A target on \{1,2,3\} is p=(0.1,\ 0.1,\ 0.8), and we want \Phi=\mathbb E_p[x]. In this small example we know p and q exactly, so we can use the middle form of the week chapter’s derivation, \hat\Phi=\frac1R\sum_r\phi(x^{(r)})\,p(x^{(r)})/q(x^{(r)}), with x^{(r)}\sim q.

  4. Proposal C is q=(0.5,\ 0.5,\ 0). What does the estimate converge to?

This uses Week 4, Importance sampling together with the variance formula of Simple Monte Carlo.

  1. \Phi=1(0.1)+2(0.1)+3(0.8)=2.7.
  2. Write g(x)=\phi(x)\,p(x)/q(x) for one term.

Problem 29: Rejection sampling, and what dimension does to it

Take \tilde p(x)=x(1-x) on [0,1] and the uniform proposal q(x)=1 on [0,1], so \tilde q=q and Z_q=1.

  1. Find Z_p and the smallest valid c. What is the acceptance rate?
  2. With that c, decide accept or reject for these (x,u) pairs, where u was drawn uniform on [0,\ c\,\tilde q(x)]: (0.2,\ 0.10), (0.9,\ 0.12), (0.5,\ 0.24), (0.05,\ 0.03), (0.7,\ 0.22).
  3. What happens to the acceptance rate with c=0.5? What goes wrong with c=0.2?
  4. Now take D independent copies: \tilde p(\mathbf x)=\prod_{d=1}^Dx_d(1-x_d) on the unit cube, with a uniform proposal on the cube. Find the acceptance rate for D=10 and D=100, and the expected number of proposals per accepted sample.
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 4, Rejection sampling, including the acceptance-rate formula at the end of that section.

  1. Z_p=\int_0^1(x-x^2)\,dx=\tfrac12-\tfrac13=\tfrac16. The envelope must satisfy c\cdot1\ge x(1-x) everywhere. The maximum of x(1-x) is \tfrac14 at x=\tfrac12, so the smallest valid c is \tfrac14. The acceptance rate is Z_p/(cZ_q)=\tfrac16/\tfrac14=\tfrac23.
  2. Accept when u\le\tilde p(x):
    • \tilde p(0.2)=0.16\ge0.10: accept.
    • \tilde p(0.9)=0.09<0.12: reject.
    • \tilde p(0.5)=0.25\ge0.24: accept.
    • \tilde p(0.05)=0.0475\ge0.03: accept.
    • \tilde p(0.7)=0.21<0.22: reject.
    The accepted samples are 0.2, 0.5, 0.05.
  3. c=0.5 is still a valid envelope, but the rate becomes \tfrac16/\tfrac12=\tfrac13: half as many acceptances for the same work. c=0.2 is invalid because 0.2<\tilde p(x) near x=\tfrac12. There every u is at most 0.2, so every proposal is accepted, and the accepted samples follow \min(\tilde p(x),\,0.2) rather than \tilde p. The peak is flattened and the samples are no longer from p.
  4. Each factor x_d(1-x_d) depends on its own coordinate only, so the product is maximized by putting every x_d=\tfrac12: the maximum is (\tfrac14)^D, and the smallest c is (\tfrac14)^D. For the same reason the integral over the cube splits into a product of one-dimensional integrals, Z_p=\int_{[0,1]^D}\prod_{d=1}^Dx_d(1-x_d)\,d\mathbf x=\prod_{d=1}^D\int_0^1x_d(1-x_d)\,dx_d=\big(\tfrac16\big)^D, and the uniform proposal has Z_q=1. The rate is (\tfrac23)^D.
    • D=10: (\tfrac23)^{10}\approx0.0173, about 58 proposals per accepted sample.
    • D=100: (\tfrac23)^{100}\approx2.5\times10^{-18}, about 4\times10^{17} proposals per accepted sample.
    Each dimension multiplies the rate by \tfrac23, so it decays exponentially with D. This is the lecture’s reason for presenting rejection sampling as a one-dimensional tool.

Answer. (1) Z_p=\tfrac16, c=\tfrac14, rate \tfrac23. (2) Accept, reject, accept, accept, reject. (3) Rate \tfrac13; c=0.2 is not an envelope and gives samples from the wrong distribution. (4) (\tfrac23)^D: about 0.017 at D=10, about 2.5\times10^{-18} at D=100.

Problem 30: Rejection sampling with a non-uniform proposal

A target on \{1,2,3\} has \tilde p=(1,\ 3,\ 2). The proposal is q=(0.5,\ 0.25,\ 0.25), with \tilde q=q and Z_q=1. Given a proposed x, u is drawn uniformly from [0,\ c\,\tilde q(x)] as in the week chapter’s algorithm.

  1. Find the smallest valid c.
  2. With that c, compute the probability of accepting each x, and the probability of proposing x and accepting it. Show that the accepted draws follow p, and find the overall acceptance rate two ways.
  3. Repeat the acceptance rate for the proposal q=(0.2,\ 0.4,\ 0.4). Why is it higher?
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 4, Rejection sampling, including the proof that accepted samples follow p.

  1. We need c\,q(x)\ge\tilde p(x) at every x, so c\ge\max(1/0.5,\ 3/0.25,\ 2/0.25)=\max(2,12,8)=12. The envelope is 12q=(6,\ 3,\ 3).
  2. Given x, acceptance has probability \tilde p(x)/(c\,\tilde q(x)): \Big(\tfrac16,\ 1,\ \tfrac23\Big). Multiplying by q(x) gives the probability of proposing x and accepting it: \Big(\tfrac1{12},\ \tfrac3{12},\ \tfrac2{12}\Big). These add to \tfrac6{12}=\tfrac12, the acceptance rate. Dividing by it gives the distribution of accepted draws, (\tfrac16,\ \tfrac12,\ \tfrac13), which is exactly p=\tilde p/6. The formula agrees: Z_p/(cZ_q)=6/12=\tfrac12.
  3. Now c\ge\max(1/0.2,\ 3/0.4,\ 2/0.4)=\max(5,\ 7.5,\ 5)=7.5, so the rate is 6/7.5=0.8. The constant c is set by the worst ratio \tilde p/q. Answer. (1) c=12. (2) Acceptance (\tfrac16,1,\tfrac23) given x; joint (\tfrac1{12},\tfrac3{12},\tfrac2{12}); accepted draws follow (\tfrac16,\tfrac12,\tfrac13)=p; rate \tfrac12. (3) Rate 0.8.
