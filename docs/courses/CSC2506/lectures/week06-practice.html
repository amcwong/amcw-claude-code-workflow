Week 6 practice: HMMs and Variational Inference I

Overview. These problems drill Week 6 section by section. Each heading below matches a section of the week chapter, in the same order, and its problems test only that section and what came before it. Try each stem before opening the worked solution. Every HMM, distribution, MRF, and code snippet here is invented for practice; none is a homework item, and none reuses the week’s two worked examples except where a problem says it is modifying one. Notation matches the week chapter: \pi is the initial distribution, \Psi(i,j)=p(z_{t+1}=j\mid z_t=i) the transition matrix, \psi_t(i)=p(x_t\mid z_t=i) the emission vector, and logarithms are natural (KL values in nats).

Several HMM problems share one model, which we call HMM-B: two hidden states \{1,2\}, binary observations, and

\pi = \begin{pmatrix}0.6\\0.4\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Psi = \begin{pmatrix}0.7 & 0.3\\ 0.4 & 0.6\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \begin{array}{c|cc} & x=0 & x=1\\\hline z=1 & 0.9 & 0.1\\ z=2 & 0.2 & 0.8\end{array}

with observed sequence x_{1:3}=(1,0,1).

From Markov chains to hidden states

Problem 1: Hidden structure makes the observations non-Markov

An HMM with two hidden states has \pi=(0.4,\ 0.6), a sticky transition matrix \Psi=\begin{pmatrix}0.9&0.1\\0.1&0.9\end{pmatrix}, and emissions p(x=0\mid z=1)=0.8, p(x=0\mid z=2)=0.2 (so state 1 mostly emits 0 and state 2 mostly emits 1).

  1. Compute p(x_3=0\mid x_2=0,\ x_1=0) and p(x_3=0\mid x_2=0,\ x_1=1). Use filtering: find p(z_2\mid x_{1:2}), push it one step through the chain, then average the emission probability.
  2. Are the observations x_1,x_2,x_3 a first-order Markov chain on their own?
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, From Markov chains to hidden states and the forward recursion of The forward algorithm.

History x_{1:2}=(0,0).

  • \psi_1=(0.8,\ 0.2), so \psi_1\odot\pi = (0.32,\ 0.12) and \alpha_1 = (0.727,\ 0.273).
  • Predict: \Psi^\top\alpha_1 = (0.9\cdot0.727+0.1\cdot0.273,\ \ 0.1\cdot0.727+0.9\cdot0.273) = (0.682,\ 0.318).
  • Update with \psi_2=(0.8,\ 0.2) and normalize: \alpha_2 = (0.896,\ 0.104).
  • Predict z_3: \Psi^\top\alpha_2 = (0.816,\ 0.184).
  • So p(x_3=0\mid x_{1:2}) = 0.8\cdot0.816 + 0.2\cdot0.184 \approx 0.690.

History x_{1:2}=(1,0).

  • \psi_1=(0.2,\ 0.8), so \psi_1\odot\pi = (0.08,\ 0.48) and \alpha_1=(0.143,\ 0.857).
  • Predict: (0.214,\ 0.786). Update with (0.8,\ 0.2): \alpha_2 = (0.522,\ 0.478).
  • Predict z_3: (0.517,\ 0.483).
  • So p(x_3=0\mid x_{1:2}) = 0.8\cdot0.517+0.2\cdot0.483\approx0.510.

Both histories end in x_2=0, yet the prediction for x_3 differs. The earlier observation x_1 changes our belief about the hidden state z_2, and through the sticky chain that belief carries forward. If the observations were Markov, x_1 could not matter once x_2 is known.

Answer. (1) About 0.690 and 0.510. (2) No: p(x_3\mid x_2,x_1) depends on x_1, so the observations alone are not a first-order Markov chain, even though the hidden states are.

Problem 2: Counting parameters

Observations take M=10 values. Compare the number of free parameters in:

  1. a homogeneous HMM with K=3 hidden states;
  2. a homogeneous second-order Markov chain directly on the observations, p(x_t\mid x_{t-1},x_{t-2}) (count only the transition table).

Then say how each count grows if we want dependence on the last m observations instead of two.

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, From Markov chains to hidden states and the parameter list in Hidden Markov models. A distribution over n values has n-1 free numbers, since they must sum to 1.

  1. HMM: \pi has K-1=2; \Psi has K rows of K-1, so 3\cdot2=6; the emissions have K rows of M-1, so 3\cdot9=27. Total 2+6+27=35.
  2. Second-order chain: one distribution over M values for each of the M^2 pairs (x_{t-1},x_{t-2}), so M^2(M-1) = 100\cdot9 = 900.

An order-m chain needs M^m(M-1) transition numbers, which grows exponentially in m. The HMM count does not depend on how far back the dependence reaches: Problem 1 showed that the HMM’s observations depend on the whole past through the hidden state. This is the trade the lecture describes, keeping the temporal dependence in a small unobserved state.

Answer. (1) 35. (2) 900. An order-m chain needs M^m(M-1); the HMM stays at (K-1)+K(K-1)+K(M-1).

Hidden Markov models

Problem 3: The joint probability of one configuration

Under HMM-B with observations x_{1:3}=(1,0,1), compute p(z_{1:3}=(1,1,2),\ x_{1:3}) from the HMM factorization. Name each factor.

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, Hidden Markov models. The factorization is p(z_1)\prod_{t\ge2}p(z_t\mid z_{t-1})\prod_t p(x_t\mid z_t). Group the factors by time step:

  • t=1: initial \pi(1)=0.6 times emission p(x_1=1\mid z_1=1)=0.1, giving 0.06.
  • t=2: transition \Psi(1,1)=0.7 times emission p(x_2=0\mid z_2=1)=0.9, giving 0.63.
  • t=3: transition \Psi(1,2)=0.3 times emission p(x_3=1\mid z_3=2)=0.8, giving 0.24.

Multiply: 0.06\times0.63\times0.24.

Answer. p(z_{1:3}=(1,1,2), x_{1:3}) = 0.009072.

Problem 4: The emission vector is not a distribution

In HMM-B the observation at t=2 is x_2=0. A student writes “\psi_2 = (0.9,\ 0.1), and since it is a probability vector its entries sum to 1.” What is \psi_2 actually, and what is wrong with the claim?

TipWorked solution

This uses the definition of \psi_t in Week 6, Hidden Markov models. \psi_t(i)=p(x_t\mid z_t=i) with x_t fixed at its observed value. So we read the column of the emission table for x=0, one entry per hidden state: \psi_2 = (p(x=0\mid z=1),\ p(x=0\mid z=2)) = (0.9,\ 0.2).

The student read the row for z=1, which is a distribution over x and does sum to 1. The column is a likelihood over hidden states: each entry comes from a different conditional distribution, so nothing forces them to sum to 1. Here they sum to 1.1.

Answer. \psi_2=(0.9,\ 0.2), a likelihood over states whose entries sum to 1.1; the student took a row of the emission table instead of the column for the observed value.

Problem 5: Reading independences off the HMM graph

For a general HMM, mark each statement true or false and give the reason.

  1. x_1\perp x_3\mid z_2
  2. x_1\perp x_3\mid x_2
  3. z_1\perp z_3\mid z_2
  4. z_1\perp z_3\mid x_2
  5. x_2\perp z_1\mid z_2
TipWorked solution

This uses the separation argument in Week 6, Hidden Markov models, which is Week 2’s d-separation applied to Figure 1 of the week chapter. Every path between past and future runs along the hidden chain, and each hidden node on such a path is a chain or fork node, so observing it blocks the path.

  1. True. The only path is x_1\leftarrow z_1\to z_2\to z_3\to x_3, and it passes through the observed z_2.
  2. False. The same path avoids x_2 entirely. x_2 is a child of z_2, not on the path, so observing it does not block anything (Problem 1 showed this numerically).
  3. True. z_2 is the middle of the chain z_1\to z_2\to z_3.
  4. False. z_2 is unobserved, so the chain z_1\to z_2\to z_3 stays open.
  5. True. The only path is x_2\leftarrow z_2\leftarrow z_1, blocked at z_2.

Answer. True, false, true, false, true.

Two inference questions

Problem 6: Brute force versus recursion, and which question is being asked

  1. An HMM has K=4 hidden states and T=20 time steps. How many hidden sequences would brute-force enumeration visit, and roughly how many basic operations does one pass of the forward recursion need?
  2. For each request, say whether it asks for the marginal posteriors p(z_t\mid x_{1:T}) or the MAP sequence:
    1. “How probable is it that the hidden state at time 7 was state 3?”
    2. “Give the single most probable explanation of the whole sequence.”
    3. “At each time step separately, report the most probable state.”
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, Two inference questions.

  1. Each of the T hidden variables takes K values, so there are K^T = 4^{20}\approx1.1\times10^{12} sequences. The forward recursion does a K\times K matrix-vector product per step, about TK^2 = 20\cdot16=320 multiply-adds.
    1. A single marginal \gamma_7(3): forward-backward. (b) The jointly most probable sequence: Viterbi. (c) This is the argmax of each \gamma_t separately, which comes from forward-backward. It is not the same as (b): the most probable sequence need not be built from the individually most probable states (Problem 14 constructs an example).

Answer. (1) About 1.1\times10^{12} sequences against about 320 operations. (2) Marginal, MAP, marginal.

The forward algorithm

Problem 7: A forward pass on HMM-B

Run the forward recursion on HMM-B with x_{1:3}=(1,0,1). Report \alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3 and each normalizer.

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, The forward algorithm: \alpha_1\propto\psi_1\odot\pi and \alpha_t\propto\psi_t\odot(\Psi^\top\alpha_{t-1}). The emission vectors are the table columns for the observed values: \psi_1=\psi_3=(0.1,\ 0.8) and \psi_2=(0.9,\ 0.2).

  • t=1: \psi_1\odot\pi = (0.06,\ 0.32). Normalizer 0.38, so \alpha_1\approx(0.158,\ 0.842).
  • t=2: predict \Psi^\top\alpha_1 = (0.7\cdot0.158+0.4\cdot0.842,\ \ 0.3\cdot0.158+0.6\cdot0.842)\approx(0.447,\ 0.553). Update: (0.9\cdot0.447,\ 0.2\cdot0.553)\approx(0.403,\ 0.111). Normalizer \approx0.513, so \alpha_2\approx(0.785,\ 0.215).
  • t=3: predict \Psi^\top\alpha_2\approx(0.635,\ 0.365). Update: (0.1\cdot0.635,\ 0.8\cdot0.365)\approx(0.064,\ 0.292). Normalizer \approx0.355, so \alpha_3\approx(0.179,\ 0.821).

Each step follows the observation: a 1 favours state 2, a 0 favours state 1, and the chain’s tendency to stay put softens each swing.

Answer. \alpha_1\approx(0.158,0.842), \alpha_2\approx(0.785,0.215), \alpha_3\approx(0.179,0.821); normalizers 0.38, 0.513, 0.355.

Problem 8: A transposition bug

A student implements the forward step as alpha = psi_t * (Psi @ alpha_prev), that is \alpha_t\propto\psi_t\odot(\Psi\,\alpha_{t-1}). Using HMM-B’s \alpha_1 from Problem 7, compute what the student’s code gives for \alpha_2, explain why it is wrong, and give one quick check that would have caught it.

TipWorked solution

This uses the derivation in Week 6, The forward algorithm. The prediction step is p(z_t=j\mid x_{1:t-1})=\sum_i\Psi(i,j)\alpha_{t-1}(i): we sum down column j of \Psi, over where the chain came from. That is (\Psi^\top\alpha_{t-1})(j).

The student’s (\Psi\alpha_{t-1})(i)=\sum_j\Psi(i,j)\alpha_{t-1}(j) sums along row i, weighting the probabilities of leaving i by beliefs about other states. It has no probabilistic meaning here (it has the shape of the backward recursion instead).

With \alpha_1\approx(0.158,\ 0.842): \Psi\alpha_1 \approx(0.7\cdot0.158+0.3\cdot0.842,\ \ 0.4\cdot0.158+0.6\cdot0.842)=(0.363,\ 0.568). Multiply by \psi_2=(0.9,0.2) and normalize: \alpha_2\approx(0.742,\ 0.258), against the correct (0.785,\ 0.215).

The quick check: a predicted distribution must sum to 1. \Psi^\top\alpha_1 always does (each row of \Psi sums to 1), but \Psi\alpha_1 sums to 0.932 here. The bug is invisible when \Psi is symmetric, which is why it should be tested on a non-symmetric \Psi.

Answer. The bug gives \alpha_2\approx(0.742,0.258) instead of (0.785,0.215). Check that the predicted vector sums to 1 before multiplying by \psi_t.

Problem 9: The probability of the data from the normalizers

Using the normalizers from Problem 7, compute p(x_{1:2}) for HMM-B. Then check it by summing the joint over all four hidden pairs (z_1,z_2).

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, The forward algorithm and the arithmetic check in Worked example: a two-state HMM. The normalizer at step t is \sum_j\psi_t(j)\,p(z_t=j\mid x_{1:t-1}) = p(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1}), so by the chain rule the product of the first two normalizers is p(x_1)\,p(x_2\mid x_1)=p(x_{1:2}):

p(x_{1:2}) = 0.38\times0.513\approx0.195.

Brute force with x_{1:2}=(1,0), each term \pi(z_1)\,\psi_1(z_1)\,\Psi(z_1,z_2)\,\psi_2(z_2):

  • (1,1): 0.6\cdot0.1\cdot0.7\cdot0.9 = 0.0378
  • (1,2): 0.6\cdot0.1\cdot0.3\cdot0.2 = 0.0036
  • (2,1): 0.4\cdot0.8\cdot0.4\cdot0.9 = 0.1152
  • (2,2): 0.4\cdot0.8\cdot0.6\cdot0.2 = 0.0384

The sum is 0.195.

Answer. p(x_{1:2})=0.195 both ways.

The forward-backward algorithm

Problem 10: Smoothing HMM-B

Finish forward-backward on HMM-B: compute \beta_3,\beta_2,\beta_1, then \gamma_1,\gamma_2,\gamma_3. Compare \gamma_1 with \alpha_1.

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, The forward-backward algorithm: \beta_T=\mathbf 1, \beta_t=\Psi(\psi_{t+1}\odot\beta_{t+1}), and \gamma_t\propto\alpha_t\odot\beta_t. The \alpha’s come from Problem 7.

Backward pass.

  • \beta_3=(1,\ 1).
  • \beta_2 = \Psi(\psi_3\odot\beta_3) = \Psi\,(0.1,\ 0.8)^\top = (0.07+0.24,\ \ 0.04+0.48) = (0.31,\ 0.52).
  • \beta_1 = \Psi(\psi_2\odot\beta_2) = \Psi\,(0.279,\ 0.104)^\top = (0.7\cdot0.279+0.3\cdot0.104,\ \ 0.4\cdot0.279+0.6\cdot0.104) = (0.2265,\ 0.174).

Combine.

  • \gamma_1\propto(0.158\cdot0.2265,\ 0.842\cdot0.174)\approx(0.0358,\ 0.1465), so \gamma_1\approx(0.196,\ 0.804).
  • \gamma_2\propto(0.785\cdot0.31,\ 0.215\cdot0.52)\approx(0.243,\ 0.112), so \gamma_2\approx(0.685,\ 0.315).
  • \gamma_3=\alpha_3\approx(0.179,\ 0.821).

\gamma_1 is close to \alpha_1=(0.158,0.842) but shifted toward state 1: the next observation is a 0, which favours state 1 at t=2, and the chain makes state 1 at t=1 a little more plausible as a result. Enumerating all 8 hidden sequences gives the same \gamma’s.

Answer. \beta_2=(0.31,0.52), \beta_1=(0.2265,0.174); \gamma_1\approx(0.196,0.804), \gamma_2\approx(0.685,0.315), \gamma_3\approx(0.179,0.821).

Problem 11: What the backward vector carries

  1. Suppose every emission is uninformative: p(x\mid z=i) is the same for all states i, for every observed x. Predict \beta_t and \gamma_t without computing.
  2. Show that for every t, \;p(x_{1:t})\sum_i\alpha_t(i)\beta_t(i) = p(x_{1:T}). Check it on HMM-B at t=1, t=2, and t=3.
TipWorked solution

This uses the definitions of \alpha_t and \beta_t in Week 6, The forward algorithm and The forward-backward algorithm.

  1. If \psi_{t+1}(j)=c_{t+1} for all j, then \beta_t=\Psi(c_{t+1}\beta_{t+1}). Starting from \beta_T=\mathbf 1 and using that each row of \Psi sums to 1, every \beta_t is a constant vector. A constant vector disappears in the normalization, so \gamma_t=\alpha_t. The future observations carry no information about which state we were in. In the same way, the \psi’s in the forward pass are constant, so \alpha_t is just the chain’s own marginal p(z_t): the observations tell us nothing at all.
  2. From the definitions, \alpha_t(i)\,p(x_{1:t}) = p(z_t=i, x_{1:t}), and multiplying by \beta_t(i)=p(x_{t+1:T}\mid z_t=i)=p(x_{t+1:T}\mid z_t=i, x_{1:t}) gives p(z_t=i, x_{1:T}). Summing over i leaves p(x_{1:T}), whatever t is.

On HMM-B:

  • t=1: 0.38\times(0.158\cdot0.2265+0.842\cdot0.174)\approx0.38\times0.1823\approx0.0693.
  • t=2: 0.195\times(0.785\cdot0.31+0.215\cdot0.52)\approx0.195\times0.3552\approx0.0693.
  • t=3: 0.0693\times(0.179+0.821)=0.0693.

Answer. (1) Every \beta_t is constant and \gamma_t=\alpha_t. (2) The sum equals p(x_{1:3})\approx0.0693 at each t, which is a useful bug check for an implementation.

Problem 12: Remove the chain’s memory

Take the week’s worked example (same \pi, emissions, and observations (0,1,1)) but replace the transition matrix with \Psi=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.5\\0.5&0.5\end{pmatrix}. Predict \gamma_1,\gamma_2,\gamma_3 before computing, then verify with forward-backward. Why did the original worked example’s \gamma_1 differ from \alpha_1, and why can that not happen here?

TipWorked solution

This modifies Week 6, Worked example: a two-state HMM. With every row of \Psi equal to (0.5,0.5), the next state ignores the current one, so the hidden states are independent and each z_t is uniform. Then z_t can only learn from its own observation: \gamma_t\propto\psi_t.

Verify. \psi_1=(0.8,\ 0.4) and \psi_2=\psi_3=(0.2,\ 0.6).

  • Forward: \alpha_1\propto(0.4,0.2), so (0.667,0.333). Prediction is \Psi^\top\alpha_1=(0.5,0.5) whatever \alpha_1 is, so \alpha_2=\alpha_3\propto(0.2,0.6), which is (0.25,0.75).
  • Backward: \beta_3=(1,1); \beta_2=\Psi(0.2,0.6)^\top=(0.4,0.4); \beta_1=\Psi(0.08,0.24)^\top=(0.16,0.16). Every \beta_t is constant.
  • So \gamma_t=\alpha_t: \gamma_1=(0.667,0.333), \gamma_2=\gamma_3=(0.25,0.75), which is each \psi_t normalized.

In the original example the sticky chain linked z_1 to z_2 and z_3, so the two later 1’s pulled \gamma_1 toward state 2. Smoothing adds information only through dependence between hidden states. Without it, \beta_t is constant and the future observations say nothing about z_t.

Answer. \gamma_1\approx(0.667,0.333), \gamma_2=\gamma_3=(0.25,0.75); each equals the normalized emission vector, and \gamma_t=\alpha_t because the hidden states are independent.

Problem 13: Viterbi on HMM-B

Run the Viterbi algorithm on HMM-B with x_{1:3}=(1,0,1). Report every \delta_t, every back-pointer, the MAP sequence, and its posterior probability (use p(x_{1:3})\approx0.06927 from Problem 11).

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, The Viterbi algorithm.

  • t=1: \delta_1=\pi\odot\psi_1=(0.06,\ 0.32).
  • t=2, j=1: candidates 0.06\cdot0.7=0.042 and 0.32\cdot0.4=0.128. Winner i=2, so \theta_2(1)=2 and \delta_2(1)=0.9\cdot0.128=0.1152.
  • t=2, j=2: candidates 0.06\cdot0.3=0.018 and 0.32\cdot0.6=0.192. Winner i=2, so \theta_2(2)=2 and \delta_2(2)=0.2\cdot0.192=0.0384.
  • t=3, j=1: candidates 0.1152\cdot0.7=0.08064 and 0.0384\cdot0.4=0.01536. Winner i=1, so \theta_3(1)=1 and \delta_3(1)=0.1\cdot0.08064=0.008064.
  • t=3, j=2: candidates 0.1152\cdot0.3=0.03456 and 0.0384\cdot0.6=0.02304. Winner i=1, so \theta_3(2)=1 and \delta_3(2)=0.8\cdot0.03456=0.027648.
  • Termination: z^*_3=2. Traceback: z^*_2=\theta_3(2)=1, z^*_1=\theta_2(1)=2.

Its posterior probability is 0.027648/0.06927\approx0.399. Here the MAP sequence agrees with the per-step argmaxes of the \gamma’s from Problem 10, but that is a property of this example, not a rule.

Answer. MAP sequence (2,1,2), joint probability 0.027648, posterior probability about 0.40.

Problem 14: When the best sequence is not built from the best states

An HMM has \pi=(0.1,\ 0.9), \Psi=\begin{pmatrix}0.7&0.3\\0.3&0.7\end{pmatrix}, and p(x=0\mid z=1)=0.8, p(x=0\mid z=2)=0.4. We observe x_{1:2}=(0,0).

  1. Compute the joint p(z_{1:2},x_{1:2}) for all four hidden pairs and normalize.
  2. Find the MAP sequence and the argmax of each marginal p(z_t\mid x_{1:2}).
  3. Explain the difference.
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, Two inference questions and the remark in The Viterbi algorithm that the most probable sequence need not agree with the marginals.

  1. Each term is \pi(z_1)\,p(x_1=0\mid z_1)\,\Psi(z_1,z_2)\,p(x_2=0\mid z_2):
    • (1,1): 0.1\cdot0.8\cdot0.7\cdot0.8=0.0448
    • (1,2): 0.1\cdot0.8\cdot0.3\cdot0.4=0.0096
    • (2,1): 0.9\cdot0.4\cdot0.3\cdot0.8=0.0864
    • (2,2): 0.9\cdot0.4\cdot0.7\cdot0.4=0.1008
    The total is 0.2416, so the posteriors are about 0.185, 0.040, 0.358, 0.417.

Problem 21: Which way does the inequality point for a concave function?

This uses Week 6, Jensen’s inequality and Tutorial: reverse KL and the ELBO.

Problem 28: Which objective survives an unknown normalizer?

We can evaluate \tilde p(z), where p(z)=\tilde p(z)/Z and Z is unknown, and we can draw samples from our own q. For each of \mathrm{KL}(q\Vert p) and \mathrm{KL}(p\Vert q), say whether we can minimize it over q, and why.

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, Why variational inference uses the I-projection.

Reverse KL. Substitute \log p=\log\tilde p-\log Z:

\mathrm{KL}(q\Vert p)=\mathbb{E}_q[\log q(z)-\log\tilde p(z)]+\log Z.

The expectation is over q, so we can estimate it by averaging \log q-\log\tilde p over samples from q, and both functions can be evaluated. The unknown \log Z is a constant that does not change which q is best. So we can minimize it.

Problem 29: Maximum entropy gives an exponential family (stretch)

Among all distributions p on \{0,1,2\} with mean \mathbb{E}_p[x]=t, find the form of the one with maximum entropy, using a Lagrange multiplier for each constraint. What is the answer when t=1?

TipWorked solution

This uses the maximum-entropy remark in Week 6, Why variational inference uses the I-projection and the method of Lagrange multipliers.

Maximize H(p)=-\sum_x p(x)\log p(x) subject to \sum_x p(x)x=t and \sum_x p(x)=1. The Lagrangian is

-\sum_x p(x)\log p(x) + \eta\Big(\sum_x p(x)\,x - t\Big) + \lambda\Big(\sum_x p(x)-1\Big).

Problem 34: Entropy of products and of correlated distributions

  1. Let q_1=(0.5,\ 0.5) and q_2=(0.8,\ 0.2) on \{0,1\}, and q=q_1q_2. Compute H(q) from the four joint probabilities and check that it equals H(q_1)+H(q_2).
  2. Now take the correlated joint r with r(0,0)=r(1,1)=0.4 and r(0,1)=r(1,0)=0.1. Compute its marginals and compare H(r) with the sum of the marginal entropies.
TipWorked solution

This uses the entropy decomposition in Week 6, Naive mean field.

  1. H(q_1)=\log2\approx0.693 and H(q_2)=-(0.8\log0.8+0.2\log0.2)\approx0.500, summing to 1.194. The joint is q(x_1,x_2): (0,0)=0.4, (0,1)=0.1, (1,0)=0.4, (1,1)=0.1, so H(q)=-2(0.4\log0.4+0.1\log0.1)\approx-2(-0.367-0.230)=1.194. They agree, as the decomposition says.
  2. Both marginals of r are (0.5,0.5), so the marginal entropies sum to 2\log2\approx1.386. But r has the same four numbers as q in part 1, rearranged, so H(r)\approx1.194<1.386.

A product of marginals is the most spread-out joint with those marginals; correlation removes uncertainty. When mean field replaces p by a product, the entropy it computes, \sum_iH(q_i), is exact for q but describes a joint with no correlation at all.

Answer. (1) H(q)\approx1.194=H(q_1)+H(q_2). (2) H(r)\approx1.194<1.386, the sum of its marginal entropies.

Problem 35: When naive mean field is exact

  1. An MRF has unary potentials only, no edges: p(x)\propto\exp\{\sum_i\phi_i(x_i)\}. Show that the naive mean-field optimum equals p.
  2. Now the MRF has at least one edge whose potential really couples its two variables (so p is not a product). Can naive mean field ever give q=p? What is the smallest KL it can reach?
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, Naive mean field and property 2 of the KL divergence.

  1. Without edges, p(x)=\prod_i\big(e^{\phi_i(x_i)}/Z_i\big) is already a product of single-variable distributions, so it is a member of the mean-field family Q. The I-projection minimizes \mathrm{KL}(q\Vert p), which reaches 0 at q=p. So the optimum is p.
  2. If p is not a product, no member of Q equals p, so \mathrm{KL}(q\Vert p)>0 for every q\in Q. The best mean field can do is a strictly positive KL, which is also the gap between the mean-field objective and \log Z (Problem 32). The week’s two-variable worked example shows this: the mean-field marginal came out more confident than the exact one.

Answer. (1) Exact, because p itself factorizes. (2) Never exact; the minimum KL is strictly positive.

Pairwise MRFs and coordinate ascent

Problem 36: The Lagrange step in isolation

On \{1,2,3\}, maximize \;\sum_x q(x)\,a(x) + H(q)\; over distributions q, where a=(0,\ \log2,\ \log3). Use a Lagrange multiplier for \sum_xq(x)=1. Report q^* and the maximum value.

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, Lagrange multipliers and The coordinate update, which solves exactly this problem with a(x_i)=\sum_{j}\sum_{x_j}q_j(x_j)\phi_{ij}(x_i,x_j).

The Lagrangian is \sum_xq(x)a(x)-\sum_xq(x)\log q(x)+\lambda(\sum_xq(x)-1). Differentiating with respect to q(x): a(x)-\log q(x)-1+\lambda=0, so q(x)=e^{\lambda-1}e^{a(x)}\propto e^{a(x)}. Here e^{a}=(1,2,3), so q^*=(1/6,\ 2/6,\ 3/6).

The value: \log q^*(x)=a(x)-\log6, so \sum_xq^*(x)a(x)+H(q^*)=\sum_xq^*(x)a(x)-\sum_xq^*(x)\big(a(x)-\log6\big)=\log6\approx1.792.

The mean-field update is this computation, one variable at a time, with a supplied by the neighbours.

Answer. q^*=(1/6,\ 1/3,\ 1/2), and the maximum value is \log6\approx1.792.

Problem 37: Two sweeps on a three-node chain

Three binary variables x_1-x_2-x_3, each in \{0,1\}, form a chain MRF with \phi_{12}=x_1x_2 and \phi_{23}=x_2x_3. Write m_i=q_i(1) and \sigma(y)=1/(1+e^{-y}).

  1. Derive the coordinate updates for m_1, m_2, m_3.
  2. Starting uniform, run two sweeps in the order 1,2,3.
  3. The fixed point is about (0.690,\ 0.799,\ 0.690), and the exact marginals are about (0.679,\ 0.776,\ 0.679). Comment.
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, The coordinate update.

  1. For node 1 (one neighbour): q_1(x_1)\propto\exp\{\sum_{x_2}q_2(x_2)\,x_1x_2\}=\exp\{x_1m_2\}, so m_1=e^{m_2}/(1+e^{m_2})=\sigma(m_2). Likewise m_3=\sigma(m_2). Node 2 has two neighbours, whose averaged potentials add: m_2=\sigma(m_1+m_3).
  2. Start m=(0.5,0.5,0.5).
    • Sweep 1: m_1=\sigma(0.5)\approx0.623; m_2=\sigma(0.623+0.5)=\sigma(1.123)\approx0.754; m_3=\sigma(0.754)\approx0.680.
    • Sweep 2: m_1=\sigma(0.754)\approx0.680; m_2=\sigma(0.680+0.680)\approx0.796; m_3=\sigma(0.796)\approx0.689.
    Each update uses the newest values of the neighbours, which is what makes this coordinate ascent rather than a simultaneous update.
  3. The middle node has two attractive neighbours, so it ends up the most confident in both. Mean field overstates every marginal a little, the same direction as the week’s two-variable example: replacing the neighbours by their averages ignores the joint configurations in which they are both 0.

Answer. (1) m_1=\sigma(m_2), m_2=\sigma(m_1+m_3), m_3=\sigma(m_2). (2) After two sweeps, m\approx(0.680,\ 0.796,\ 0.689). (3) Close to exact, slightly overconfident.

Problem 38: Unary terms, and a fixed point that is not a maximum (stretch)

  1. Add unary log-potentials \phi_i(x_i) to the pairwise MRF. Rederive the coordinate update.
  2. Two spins x_1,x_2\in\{-1,+1\} have only the pairwise potential \phi_{12}=Jx_1x_2 with J=2, and no unary terms. Write m_i=\mathbb{E}_{q_i}[x_i] and show the update is m_1\leftarrow\tanh(Jm_2), m_2\leftarrow\tanh(Jm_1).
  3. Run the updates from a uniform start (m_1=m_2=0), and from a start with m_2=0.2. What happens in each case?
  4. The exact marginals are uniform by symmetry. What does mean field get wrong, and how is it related to the I-projection?
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, The coordinate update and the mode-seeking behaviour from Which direction? I-projection and M-projection.

  1. A unary term adds \sum_{x_i}q_i(x_i)\phi_i(x_i) to the part of the objective involving q_i. Its derivative with respect to q_i(x_i) is \phi_i(x_i), so the stationarity condition gains that term and q_i(x_i)\propto\exp\Big\{\phi_i(x_i)+\sum_{j\in N(i)}\sum_{x_j}q_j(x_j)\phi_{ij}(x_i,x_j)\Big\}.

  2. \sum_{x_2}q_2(x_2)Jx_1x_2=Jx_1m_2, so q_1(x_1)\propto e^{Jx_1m_2} and m_1=\frac{e^{Jm_2}-e^{-Jm_2}}{e^{Jm_2}+e^{-Jm_2}}=\tanh(Jm_2). The same for m_2.

  3. From m_1=m_2=0: \tanh(0)=0, so nothing ever moves; uniform is a fixed point. From m_2=0.2: m_1=\tanh(0.4)\approx0.380, m_2=\tanh(0.760)\approx0.641, then 0.857, 0.937, and the values settle at m\approx0.957, the positive solution of m=\tanh(2m). Starting from m_2=-0.2 gives m\approx-0.957 by symmetry.

    Compare objective values, \mathbb{E}_q[Jx_1x_2]+H(q_1)+H(q_2)=Jm_1m_2+H(q_1)+H(q_2). The uniform point gives 0+2\log2\approx1.386; the tilted fixed point gives about 2(0.957)^2+2(0.104)\approx2.039. Both are below \log Z=\log(2e^2+2e^{-2})\approx2.711. So the uniform point is a fixed point of the updates but not a maximum: coordinate ascent can get stuck at it only because the start was exactly symmetric.

  4. The exact p puts almost all its mass on (+1,+1) and (-1,-1) equally, so each exact marginal is uniform. A product q cannot put mass on both of those corners without also putting mass on the mixed corners, which p penalizes. So the best product picks one corner, like the I-projection picking one bump of a mixture. The resulting marginals (q_i(+1)\approx0.98) are confidently wrong about each spin, even though the objective is as high as a product can get.

Answer. (1) Add \phi_i(x_i) inside the exponent. (2) m_i\leftarrow\tanh(Jm_j). (3) Stuck at 0 from a uniform start; from m_2=0.2, converges to m\approx0.957 (or -0.957 from the opposite start). (4) Mean field breaks the symmetry and picks one mode, as the I-projection does.
