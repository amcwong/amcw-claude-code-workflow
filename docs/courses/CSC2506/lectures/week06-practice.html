Try each stem before opening the worked solution. Every HMM, distribution, MRF, and code snippet here is invented for practice; none is a homework item, and none reuses the week’s two worked examples except where a problem says it is modifying one. Notation matches the week chapter: \pi is the initial distribution, \Psi(i,j)=p(z_{t+1}=j\mid z_t=i) the transition matrix, \psi_t(i)=p(x_t\mid z_t=i) the emission vector, and logarithms are natural (KL values in nats).

Several HMM problems share one model, which we call HMM-B: two hidden states \{1,2\}, binary observations, and

\pi = \begin{pmatrix}0.6\\0.4\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Psi = \begin{pmatrix}0.7 & 0.3\\ 0.4 & 0.6\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \begin{array}{c|cc} & x=0 & x=1\\\hline z=1 & 0.9 & 0.1\\ z=2 & 0.2 & 0.8\end{array}

with observed sequence x_{1:3}=(1,0,1).

From Markov chains to hidden states

Problem 1: Hidden structure makes the observations non-Markov

An HMM with two hidden states has \pi=(0.4,\ 0.6), a sticky transition matrix \Psi=\begin{pmatrix}0.9&0.1\\0.1&0.9\end{pmatrix}, and emissions p(x=0\mid z=1)=0.8, p(x=0\mid z=2)=0.2 (so state 1 mostly emits 0 and state 2 mostly emits 1).

  1. Compute p(x_3=0\mid x_2=0,\ x_1=0) and p(x_3=0\mid x_2=0,\ x_1=1). Use filtering: find p(z_2\mid x_{1:2}), push it one step through the chain, then average the emission probability.
  2. Are the observations x_1,x_2,x_3 a first-order Markov chain on their own?
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, From Markov chains to hidden states and the forward recursion of The forward algorithm.

Main idea. The question is whether the observations inherit the Markov property of the hidden chain. They do not. Each x_t is only a noisy view of z_t, so after seeing x_2 we still do not know z_2 exactly, and the older observation x_1 tells us something more about z_2. Because the chain is sticky, that information carries forward to z_3 and changes the prediction for x_3. The formula \alpha_1\propto\psi_1\odot\pi is a mechanical step in the computation, not the idea itself.

History x_{1:2}=(0,0).

  • \psi_1=(0.8,\ 0.2), so \psi_1\odot\pi=(0.32,\ 0.12). Dividing by the sum 0.44 gives \alpha_1=(0.727,\ 0.273).
  • Predict: \Psi^\top\alpha_1=(0.682,\ 0.318). The sticky chain keeps most of the mass in state 1.
  • Update with \psi_2=(0.8,\ 0.2) and normalize: \alpha_2=(0.896,\ 0.104).
  • Predict z_3: \Psi^\top\alpha_2=(0.816,\ 0.184).
  • So p(x_3=0\mid x_{1:2})=0.8\cdot0.816+0.2\cdot0.184\approx0.690.

History x_{1:2}=(1,0).

  • \psi_1=(0.2,\ 0.8), so \psi_1\odot\pi=(0.08,\ 0.48) and \alpha_1=(0.143,\ 0.857). The first observation points strongly to state 2.
  • Predict: \Psi^\top\alpha_1=(0.214,\ 0.786). Update with (0.8,\ 0.2): \alpha_2=(0.522,\ 0.478).
  • Predict z_3: \Psi^\top\alpha_2=(0.517,\ 0.483).
  • So p(x_3=0\mid x_{1:2})=0.8\cdot0.517+0.2\cdot0.483\approx0.510.

How to read the comparison. Both histories end in x_2=0, and the emission at time 2 is identical in both. The only difference is the belief about z_2: \alpha_2=(0.896,\ 0.104) versus (0.522,\ 0.478). That gap comes entirely from x_1, and the transition matrix carries it into the prediction for x_3. If the observations were a first-order Markov chain, p(x_3\mid x_2,x_1) would equal p(x_3\mid x_2), and x_1 would have no effect once x_2 is known. The two values 0.690 and 0.510 show that it does have an effect.

Answer. (1) About 0.690 and 0.510. (2) No: p(x_3\mid x_2,x_1) depends on x_1, so the observations alone are not a first-order Markov chain, even though the hidden states are.

Key takeaways.

  • The hidden states can be Markov while the observations are not: a noisy observation does not pin down z_t, so older observations still carry information about it.
  • To predict the next observation in an HMM, filter to get \alpha_t, push it through \Psi^\top, then average the emission probabilities.
  • To show a sequence is not first-order Markov, one pair of histories with the same last value and different predictions is enough.
TipQuestion: Which equations does this problem use, and how does it map onto them?

Key equations. Filtering tracks the belief about the current hidden state:

\alpha_1 \propto \psi_1\odot\pi, \qquad \alpha_t \propto \psi_t\odot\left(\Psi^\top\alpha_{t-1}\right)\quad (t>1).

The one-step-ahead prediction of the next observation averages the emission over the predicted state:

p(x_{t+1}=v\mid x_{1:t}) = \sum_j p(x_{t+1}=v\mid z_{t+1}=j)\,\big(\Psi^\top\alpha_t\big)_j.

Variables.

  • \alpha_t(j)=p(z_t=j\mid x_{1:t}): our belief about the hidden state at time t after seeing the data so far. The problem needs \alpha_2 once for each history.
  • \pi: the prior on z_1, here (0.4,\ 0.6).
  • \psi_t(j)=p(x_t\mid z_t=j) at the observed x_t: (0.8,\ 0.2) when x_t=0 and (0.2,\ 0.8) when x_t=1.
  • \Psi^\top\alpha_t: the predicted distribution of z_{t+1} before x_{t+1} arrives.
  • \odot multiplies entry by entry, and \propto means “divide by the sum so the entries add to 1”.

How to read the two rules. The first rule is Bayes’ rule: prior \pi times likelihood \psi_1, normalized. The second is prediction (\Psi^\top\alpha_{t-1} pushes yesterday’s belief through the chain) followed by an update (\psi_t reweights each state by how well it explains x_t).

How the problem maps onto them.

  1. “Find p(z_2\mid x_{1:2})” is \alpha_2: apply the first rule with x_1, then the second rule once with x_2=0.
  2. “Push it one step through the chain” is \Psi^\top\alpha_2=p(z_3\mid x_{1:2}).
  3. “Average the emission probability” is the prediction formula with v=0: a dot product of (0.8,\ 0.2) with \Psi^\top\alpha_2.
  4. Part 2 uses the definition of a first-order Markov chain, p(x_3\mid x_2,x_1)=p(x_3\mid x_2) for all values. One pair of histories that disagrees is enough to show it fails.

Problem 2: Counting parameters

Observations take M=10 values. Compare the number of free parameters in:

  1. a homogeneous HMM with K=3 hidden states;
  2. a homogeneous second-order Markov chain directly on the observations, p(x_t\mid x_{t-1},x_{t-2}) (count only the transition table).

Then say how each count grows if we want dependence on the last m observations instead of two.

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, From Markov chains to hidden states and the parameter list in Hidden Markov models. A distribution over n values has n-1 free numbers, since they must sum to 1.

  1. HMM: \pi has K-1=2; \Psi has K rows of K-1, so 3\cdot2=6; the emissions have K rows of M-1, so 3\cdot9=27. Total 2+6+27=35.
  2. Second-order chain: one distribution over M values for each of the M^2 pairs (x_{t-1},x_{t-2}), so M^2(M-1) = 100\cdot9 = 900.

An order-m chain needs M^m(M-1) transition numbers, which grows exponentially in m. The HMM count does not depend on how far back the dependence reaches: Problem 1 showed that the HMM’s observations depend on the whole past through the hidden state. This is the trade the lecture describes, keeping the temporal dependence in a small unobserved state.

Answer. (1) 35. (2) 900. An order-m chain needs M^m(M-1); the HMM stays at (K-1)+K(K-1)+K(M-1).

Key takeaways.

  • Count the free parameters of a conditional table as (number of conditioning values) \times (number of outcome values -\,1).
  • A Markov chain on the observations pays exponentially in how far back it looks. An HMM stores long-range dependence in a small hidden state at a fixed parameter cost.
TipQuestion: Which equations does this problem use, and how does it map onto them?

Key fact. A distribution over n values has n-1 free numbers, because the last one is fixed by summing to 1. A conditional probability table has one such distribution per value of what it conditions on:

\#\text{free parameters} = (\#\text{conditioning values})\times(\#\text{outcome values}-1).

Applied to the two models:

\text{HMM: } (K-1)+K(K-1)+K(M-1), \qquad \text{order-}m\text{ chain: } M^m(M-1).

Variables.

  • K=3: number of hidden states. M=10: number of observation values. m: how many past observations the chain conditions on (m=2 here, then general).
  • \pi: one distribution over K states, conditioned on nothing.
  • \Psi: one distribution over K next states for each of the K current states.
  • Emission table: one distribution over M observations for each of the K states.

How the problem maps onto them.

  1. For the HMM, list the three tables, count rows times (values minus 1) for each, and add: 2+6+27.
  2. For the second-order chain, the conditioning value is the pair (x_{t-1},x_{t-2}), which has M^2 possibilities, each with M-1 free numbers.
  3. For general m, the conditioning tuple has M^m values, so the count grows exponentially in m. Nothing in the HMM formula mentions m.

Problem 3: The joint probability of one configuration

Under HMM-B with observations x_{1:3}=(1,0,1), compute p(z_{1:3}=(1,1,2),\ x_{1:3}) from the HMM factorization. Name each factor.

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, Hidden Markov models. The factorization is p(z_1)\prod_{t\ge2}p(z_t\mid z_{t-1})\prod_t p(x_t\mid z_t). Group the factors by time step:

  • t=1: initial \pi(1)=0.6 times emission p(x_1=1\mid z_1=1)=0.1, giving 0.06.
  • t=2: transition \Psi(1,1)=0.7 times emission p(x_2=0\mid z_2=1)=0.9, giving 0.63.
  • t=3: transition \Psi(1,2)=0.3 times emission p(x_3=1\mid z_3=2)=0.8, giving 0.24.

Multiply: 0.06\times0.63\times0.24.

Answer. p(z_{1:3}=(1,1,2), x_{1:3}) = 0.009072.

Key takeaways.

  • When every variable is fixed, the HMM joint is a product of table lookups: one initial factor, T-1 transitions, and T emissions.
  • Grouping the factors by time step ("how the chain got here" times "what it emitted") makes the product hard to get wrong.
TipQuestion: Which equations does this problem use, and how does it map onto them?

Key equation. The HMM factorization of one complete configuration:

p(z_{1:T},x_{1:T}) = \pi(z_1)\prod_{t=2}^{T}\Psi(z_{t-1},z_t)\prod_{t=1}^{T}p(x_t\mid z_t).

Variables.

  • z_{1:3}=(1,1,2) and x_{1:3}=(1,0,1) are both given, so nothing is summed.
  • \pi(z_1): the entry of \pi for the first state, here \pi(1)=0.6.
  • \Psi(z_{t-1},z_t): row is the previous state, column is the next state. Here \Psi(1,1)=0.7 and \Psi(1,2)=0.3.
  • p(x_t\mid z_t): row z_t, column x_t of the emission table.

How the problem maps onto them.

  1. Count the factors: one initial factor, T-1=2 transition factors, T=3 emission factors.
  2. Group them by time: at each t, “how did the chain get to z_t” times “what did z_t emit”.
  3. Look up each entry and multiply.

Problem 4: The emission vector is not a distribution

In HMM-B the observation at t=2 is x_2=0. A student writes “\psi_2 = (0.9,\ 0.1), and since it is a probability vector its entries sum to 1.” What is \psi_2 actually, and what is wrong with the claim?

TipWorked solution

This uses the definition of \psi_t in Week 6, Hidden Markov models. \psi_t(i)=p(x_t\mid z_t=i) with x_t fixed at its observed value. So we read the column of the emission table for x=0, one entry per hidden state: \psi_2 = (p(x=0\mid z=1),\ p(x=0\mid z=2)) = (0.9,\ 0.2).

The student read the row for z=1, which is a distribution over x and does sum to 1. The column is a likelihood over hidden states: each entry comes from a different conditional distribution, so nothing forces them to sum to 1. Here they sum to 1.1.

Answer. \psi_2=(0.9,\ 0.2), a likelihood over states whose entries sum to 1.1; the student took a row of the emission table instead of the column for the observed value.

Key takeaways.

  • \psi_t is a column of the emission table (observed value fixed, state varying), so it is a likelihood over states and need not sum to 1.
  • Rows of the emission table are distributions over observations; columns are not. A sum check tells them apart.
TipQuestion: Which equations does this problem use, and how does it map onto them?

Key definition. With the emission table B(i,v)=p(x=v\mid z=i) and observed value v_t,

\psi_t(i) = p(x_t=v_t\mid z_t=i) = B(i,v_t), \qquad i=1,\dots,K.

Variables.

  • Row i of B: a distribution over observations for state i. It always sums to 1.
  • Column v of B: one number from each state’s distribution. It is a likelihood over states and has no reason to sum to 1.
  • Here x_2=0, so \psi_2 is the x=0 column: (0.9,\ 0.2).

How the problem maps onto them.

  1. The observed value picks the column; the hidden-state index runs down it.
  2. The student’s vector (0.9,\ 0.1) is the row for z=1, so it answers a different question (“what does state 1 emit?”).
  3. The sum check exposes the mix-up: rows sum to 1, columns generally do not.

Problem 5: Reading independences off the HMM graph

For a general HMM, mark each statement true or false and give the reason.

  1. x_1\perp x_3\mid z_2
  2. x_1\perp x_3\mid x_2
  3. z_1\perp z_3\mid z_2
  4. z_1\perp z_3\mid x_2
  5. x_2\perp z_1\mid z_2
TipWorked solution

This uses the separation argument in Week 6, Hidden Markov models, which is Week 2’s d-separation applied to Figure 1 of the week chapter. Every path between past and future runs along the hidden chain, and each hidden node on such a path is a chain or fork node, so observing it blocks the path.

  1. True. The only path is x_1\leftarrow z_1\to z_2\to z_3\to x_3, and it passes through the observed z_2.
  2. False. The same path avoids x_2 entirely. x_2 is a child of z_2, not on the path, so observing it does not block anything (Problem 1 showed this numerically).
  3. True. z_2 is the middle of the chain z_1\to z_2\to z_3.
  4. False. z_2 is unobserved, so the chain z_1\to z_2\to z_3 stays open.
  5. True. The only path is x_2\leftarrow z_2\leftarrow z_1, blocked at z_2.

Answer. True, false, true, false, true.

Key takeaways.

  • In an HMM, conditioning on a hidden state that lies on the path blocks it. Conditioning on an observation never does, because observations hang off the chain.
  • To test a conditional independence, write the path, then check whether a conditioned node sits on it.
TipQuestion: Which equations does this problem use, and how does it map onto them?

Key facts. The HMM graph is the chain z_1\to z_2\to z_3 with an arrow z_t\to x_t at each time. Two variables are conditionally independent given a set S when every path between them is blocked. On this graph, every node on a path is a chain node (\to\bullet\to) or a fork node (\leftarrow\bullet\to), and such a node blocks the path exactly when it is in S.

Variables.

  • The two variables named in each statement are the endpoints of the path.
  • The variable after \mid is the conditioning set S.

How the problem maps onto them. For each statement, write the unique path, then ask whether it passes through a node in S:

Statement Path Node in S on the path? Verdict
1 x_1\leftarrow z_1\to z_2\to z_3\to x_3 z_2, yes true
2 same path x_2 is not on it false
3 z_1\to z_2\to z_3 z_2, yes true
4 z_1\to z_2\to z_3 x_2 is not on it false
5 x_2\leftarrow z_2\leftarrow z_1 z_2, yes true

Two inference questions

Problem 6: Brute force versus recursion, and which question is being asked

  1. An HMM has K=4 hidden states and T=20 time steps. How many hidden sequences would brute-force enumeration visit, and roughly how many basic operations does one pass of the forward recursion need?
  2. For each request, say whether it asks for the marginal posteriors p(z_t\mid x_{1:T}) or the MAP sequence:
    1. “How probable is it that the hidden state at time 7 was state 3?”
    2. “Give the single most probable explanation of the whole sequence.”
    3. “At each time step separately, report the most probable state.”
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, Two inference questions.

  1. Each of the T hidden variables takes K values, so there are K^T = 4^{20}\approx1.1\times10^{12} sequences. The forward recursion does a K\times K matrix-vector product per step, about TK^2 = 20\cdot16=320 multiply-adds.
    1. A single marginal \gamma_7(3): forward-backward. (b) The jointly most probable sequence: Viterbi. (c) This is the argmax of each \gamma_t separately, which comes from forward-backward. It is not the same as (b): the most probable sequence need not be built from the individually most probable states (Problem 14 constructs an example).

Answer. (1) About 1.1\times10^{12} sequences against about 320 operations. (2) Marginal, MAP, marginal.

Key takeaways.

  • Brute force grows as K^T; the forward recursion grows as TK^2, linear in the sequence length.
  • "The whole sequence" calls for Viterbi; "at time t" or "each step separately" calls for the marginals \gamma_t. The per-step argmaxes need not form the MAP sequence.
TipQuestion: Which equations does this problem use, and how does it map onto them?

Key equations.

\#\text{hidden sequences}=K^T, \qquad \text{forward-recursion cost}\approx TK^2.

The two inference targets:

\gamma_t(j)=p(z_t=j\mid x_{1:T}) \;\;(\text{forward-backward}), \qquad z^*_{1:T}=\arg\max_{z_{1:T}}p(z_{1:T}\mid x_{1:T}) \;\;(\text{Viterbi}).

Variables.

  • K=4 states per time step and T=20 time steps.
  • \gamma_t: a distribution over the state at one time. z^*_{1:T}: one whole sequence.

How the problem maps onto them.

  1. Part 1: plug in. K^T counts choices made independently at each of T steps; TK^2 counts one K\times K matrix-vector product per step.
  2. Part 2: read the shape of the request.
    • “Probability of state 3 at time 7” is one entry, \gamma_7(3).
    • “Single most probable explanation of the whole sequence” is z^*_{1:T}.
    • “Most probable state at each step separately” is \arg\max_j\gamma_t(j) for each t, computed from the \gamma’s, not from Viterbi.

The forward algorithm

Problem 7: A forward pass on HMM-B

Run the forward recursion on HMM-B with x_{1:3}=(1,0,1). Report \alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3 and each normalizer.

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, The forward algorithm: \alpha_1\propto\psi_1\odot\pi and \alpha_t\propto\psi_t\odot(\Psi^\top\alpha_{t-1}). The emission vectors are the table columns for the observed values: \psi_1=\psi_3=(0.1,\ 0.8) and \psi_2=(0.9,\ 0.2).

  • t=1: \psi_1\odot\pi = (0.06,\ 0.32). Normalizer 0.38, so \alpha_1\approx(0.158,\ 0.842).
  • t=2: predict \Psi^\top\alpha_1 = (0.7\cdot0.158+0.4\cdot0.842,\ \ 0.3\cdot0.158+0.6\cdot0.842)\approx(0.447,\ 0.553). Update: (0.9\cdot0.447,\ 0.2\cdot0.553)\approx(0.403,\ 0.111). Normalizer \approx0.513, so \alpha_2\approx(0.785,\ 0.215).
  • t=3: predict \Psi^\top\alpha_2\approx(0.635,\ 0.365). Update: (0.1\cdot0.635,\ 0.8\cdot0.365)\approx(0.064,\ 0.292). Normalizer \approx0.355, so \alpha_3\approx(0.179,\ 0.821).

Each step follows the observation: a 1 favours state 2, a 0 favours state 1, and the chain’s tendency to stay put softens each swing.

Answer. \alpha_1\approx(0.158,0.842), \alpha_2\approx(0.785,0.215), \alpha_3\approx(0.179,0.821); normalizers 0.38, 0.513, 0.355.

Key takeaways.

  • Each forward step is predict (\Psi^\top\alpha_{t-1}), update (multiply by \psi_t), normalize. Record each normalizer, since later problems need it.
  • By hand, \Psi^\top\alpha is the belief-weighted average of the rows of \Psi.
TipQuestion: Which equations does this problem use, and how does it map onto them?

Key equations.

\alpha_1 \propto \psi_1\odot\pi, \qquad \alpha_t \propto \psi_t\odot\left(\Psi^\top\alpha_{t-1}\right),

c_1=\sum_j\psi_1(j)\pi(j), \qquad c_t=\sum_j\psi_t(j)\big(\Psi^\top\alpha_{t-1}\big)_j, \qquad \big(\Psi^\top\alpha\big)_j=\sum_i\alpha(i)\,\Psi(i,j).

Variables.

  • HMM-B: \pi=(0.6,\ 0.4), \Psi with rows (0.7,\ 0.3) and (0.4,\ 0.6).
  • \psi_t is the emission column for the observed value: \psi_1=\psi_3=(0.1,\ 0.8) for x=1 and \psi_2=(0.9,\ 0.2) for x=0.
  • c_t: the normalizer at step t, the sum of the unnormalized vector. The problem asks for it explicitly.

How the problem maps onto them. Each step has three moves:

  1. Predict. \Psi^\top\alpha_{t-1} is the average of the rows of \Psi, weighted by the current belief: \alpha_{t-1}(1)\times\text{row }1+\alpha_{t-1}(2)\times\text{row }2. This is the quickest way to do it by hand.
  2. Update. Multiply entry by entry by \psi_t.
  3. Normalize. Record the sum as c_t, then divide by it to get \alpha_t.

At t=1 there is nothing to predict from, so \pi plays the role of the predicted vector.

Problem 8: A transposition bug

A student implements the forward step as alpha = psi_t * (Psi @ alpha_prev), that is \alpha_t\propto\psi_t\odot(\Psi\,\alpha_{t-1}). Using HMM-B’s \alpha_1 from Problem 7, compute what the student’s code gives for \alpha_2, explain why it is wrong, and give one quick check that would have caught it.

TipWorked solution

This uses the derivation in Week 6, The forward algorithm. The prediction step is p(z_t=j\mid x_{1:t-1})=\sum_i\Psi(i,j)\alpha_{t-1}(i): we sum down column j of \Psi, over where the chain came from. That is (\Psi^\top\alpha_{t-1})(j).

Posterior inference in latent variable models

Problem 16: Naming and computing the four pieces

A latent variable z\in\{1,2,3\} has prior p(z)=(0.5,\ 0.3,\ 0.2). For the observed x, the likelihood is p(x\mid z)=(0.1,\ 0.4,\ 0.8) for z=1,2,3. Compute the evidence and the posterior, and say which value of z is favoured before and after seeing x.

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, Posterior inference in latent variable models.

  • Joint p(x,z)=p(z)\,p(x\mid z): (0.05,\ 0.12,\ 0.16).
  • Evidence p(x)=\sum_z p(x,z) = 0.33. With three values of z, the sum is three terms; with a high-dimensional continuous z, it is the intractable integral of the week chapter.
  • Posterior p(z\mid x)=p(x,z)/p(x)\approx(0.152,\ 0.364,\ 0.485).

Before the data the prior favours z=1; the likelihood is eight times larger at z=3 than at z=1, enough to overturn a prior that was only 2.5 times larger the other way.

Answer. p(x)=0.33; posterior \approx(0.152,\ 0.364,\ 0.485); the favoured value moves from z=1 to z=3.

Key takeaways.

  • Joint is prior times likelihood, evidence is the sum of the joint, and posterior is joint divided by evidence.
  • A large enough likelihood ratio can overturn a prior. The odds form shows by how much without computing the evidence.
  • This is the same computation as \alpha_1\propto\psi_1\odot\pi in the forward algorithm.
TipQuestion: Which equations does this problem use, and how does it map onto them?

Key equations. The four pieces of Bayes’ rule:

p(x,z)=p(z)\,p(x\mid z), \qquad p(x)=\sum_z p(x,z), \qquad p(z\mid x)=\frac{p(x,z)}{p(x)}.

The odds form compares two values of z without the evidence:

\frac{p(z=a\mid x)}{p(z=b\mid x)}=\frac{p(z=a)}{p(z=b)}\times\frac{p(x\mid z=a)}{p(x\mid z=b)}.

Variables.

  • Prior p(z)=(0.5,\ 0.3,\ 0.2): a distribution over z.
  • Likelihood p(x\mid z)=(0.1,\ 0.4,\ 0.8): a function of z for the fixed x. Like \psi_t in Problem 4, it does not sum to 1.
  • Evidence p(x): one number. Posterior p(z\mid x): a distribution over z.

How the problem maps onto them.

  1. Multiply prior and likelihood entry by entry to get the joint.
  2. Add the joint entries to get the evidence.
  3. Divide to get the posterior.
  4. The “before and after” comparison is the odds form with a=3, b=1: prior odds 0.4 times likelihood ratio 8 gives posterior odds 3.2.

What the TrueSkill posterior looks like

Problem 17: What game outcomes cannot tell us

Three players have skills z_A,z_B,z_C with independent \mathcal N(0,\sigma^2) priors and the course’s TrueSkill likelihood p(i\text{ beats }j\mid z_i,z_j)=\sigma(z_i-z_j). We observe that A beat B and B beat C.

  1. Show that the likelihood is unchanged if we add the same constant c to all three skills.
  2. Which combination of skills is left exactly as uncertain as the prior made it? (Hint: write the skills as their average \bar z=(z_A+z_B+z_C)/3 plus deviations from it, and use that, for independent equal-variance Gaussians, the average is independent of the deviations.)
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, What the TrueSkill posterior looks like and the model in Week 4.

  1. The likelihood is \sigma(z_A-z_B)\,\sigma(z_B-z_C). Adding c to every skill leaves each difference unchanged, so the likelihood is unchanged.
  2. The likelihood depends only on the deviations z_A-\bar z, z_B-\bar z, z_C-\bar z (the pairwise differences are differences of deviations). Under the prior, \bar z\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2/3) and \bar z is independent of the deviations. So the posterior factorizes into (prior on \bar z) times (something depending only on the deviations), and the posterior of \bar z is exactly its prior, \mathcal N(0,\sigma^2/3), however many games we observe.

This is the three-player form of the lecture’s fourth picture: game outcomes can pin down how players compare, never how good they all are in an absolute sense. Only the prior fixes the overall level.

Answer. (1) Every pairwise difference is unchanged. (2) The average skill \bar z keeps its prior \mathcal N(0,\sigma^2/3).

Key takeaways.

  • Game outcomes depend only on skill differences, so they cannot inform the average skill. Only the prior fixes the overall level.
  • A likelihood that is flat along some direction leaves the prior unchanged along it, when that direction is independent of the rest under the prior.
TipQuestion: Which equations does this problem use, and how does it map onto them?

Key equations.

p(\text{data}\mid z)=\sigma(z_A-z_B)\,\sigma(z_B-z_C), \qquad p(z\mid\text{data})\propto p(z)\,p(\text{data}\mid z).

Decompose each skill into the average plus a deviation, z_k=\bar z+d_k. Under the prior, \bar z\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2/3) and \bar z is independent of d=(d_A,d_B,d_C). If the likelihood depends only on d, then

p(\bar z,d\mid\text{data})\propto p(\bar z)\,\big[p(d)\,L(d)\big] \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p(\bar z\mid\text{data})=p(\bar z).

Variables.

  • \sigma(\cdot) is the logistic function. \sigma^2 is the prior variance. These are two different uses of the same letter.
  • \bar z=(z_A+z_B+z_C)/3, with variance 3\sigma^2/9=\sigma^2/3.
  • L(d): the likelihood written as a function of the deviations.

How the problem maps onto them.

  1. Part 1: substitute z_k+c into the likelihood. Each difference z_i-z_j is unchanged.
  2. Part 2: rewrite the variables as (\bar z,d). The prior factorizes, and the likelihood touches only d, so the posterior of \bar z is its prior.

Why the posterior is hard

Problem 18: Grids in high dimension, and why HMMs escaped

  1. We approximate p(x)=\int p(x,z)\,dz on a grid with 100 points per dimension. How many evaluations of p(x,z) are needed when z has dimension 2, and when it has dimension 50 (say, a TrueSkill model with 50 players)?
  2. An HMM with T=50 binary hidden states also has an evidence that is a sum over 2^{50} terms, yet forward-backward computes it easily. Name the structural difference that lets the HMM escape and that a fully connected 50-player TrueSkill posterior lacks.
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, Why the posterior is hard and the forward recursion.

  1. A grid with 100 points per axis in d dimensions has 100^d points: 10^4 for d=2 and 10^{100} for d=50. The first is trivial; the second is beyond any computer.
  2. In an HMM each hidden variable interacts only with its two neighbours in a chain, so the sum can be pushed inside one variable at a time; the forward normalizers multiply to p(x_{1:T}) in about TK^2 steps (Problem 9). After many games among 50 players, the posterior couples most pairs of skills (Week 4’s explaining away), so there is no chain or tree to eliminate along, and the variables are continuous with logistic factors that have no closed-form integral. Exact elimination is unavailable, which is why the course turns to sampling (Week 5) and variational methods (this week).

Answer. (1) 10^4 and 10^{100}. (2) The HMM’s chain structure (a tree) lets the recursion eliminate one variable at a time; the coupled TrueSkill posterior has no such structure.

Key takeaways.

  • A grid costs G^d evaluations, exponential in the dimension.
  • Exact inference is cheap when each factor touches a small group of variables arranged in a chain or tree. A densely coupled posterior has no such order to eliminate along.
TipQuestion: Which equations does this problem use, and how does it map onto them?

Key equations.

\#\text{grid points}=G^d, \qquad p(x_{1:T})=\sum_{z_1,\dots,z_T}\pi(z_1)\,\psi_1(z_1)\prod_{t=2}^{T}\Psi(z_{t-1},z_t)\,\psi_t(z_t).

Every factor in the HMM sum involves at most two neighbouring variables, so the sums can be done one variable at a time at a cost of about TK^2 rather than K^T.

Variables.

  • G=100 points per axis; d=2 or d=50 dimensions.
  • T=50 binary hidden states, so K=2 and K^T=2^{50} terms.

How the problem maps onto them.

  1. Part 1: plug in G^d.
  2. Part 2: look at which variables each factor touches. In the HMM, each factor touches neighbours on a chain, so summing out z_1 produces a function of z_2 only, and so on (this is the forward recursion). In the 50-player posterior, factors couple most pairs of players, so summing out one skill leaves a function of many others.

Problem 20: Checking Jensen with numbers

A random variable y equals 1 or 4, each with probability 1/2. With \phi(y)=-\log y, compute \mathbb{E}[\phi(y)] and \phi(\mathbb{E}[y]), check Jensen’s inequality, and say how to change y’s distribution so the two sides are equal.

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, Jensen’s inequality. \phi=-\log is convex because \phi''(y)=1/y^2>0.

  • \mathbb{E}[\phi(y)] = \tfrac12(-\log1) + \tfrac12(-\log4) = -\tfrac12\log4 = -\log2\approx-0.693.
  • \mathbb{E}[y]=2.5, so \phi(\mathbb{E}[y]) = -\log2.5\approx-0.916.

Indeed -0.693\ge-0.916. Equality requires y to be constant (since -\log is not linear), for example y=2.5 with probability 1, where both sides equal -\log2.5.

Answer. -0.693\ge-0.916, as Jensen requires; the sides are equal only when y is constant.

Key takeaways.

  • For a convex \phi, applying \phi and then averaging gives at least as much as averaging and then applying \phi.
  • The gap grows with the spread of y and vanishes only when y is constant.
TipQuestion: Which equations does this problem use, and how does it map onto them?

Key equation. Jensen’s inequality for a convex function \phi:

\mathbb{E}[\phi(y)]\ \ge\ \phi(\mathbb{E}[y]).

For a strictly convex \phi, equality holds only when y is constant. A twice-differentiable \phi is convex when \phi''\ge0.

Variables.

  • y\in\{1,4\}, each with probability 1/2.
  • \phi(y)=-\log y, with \phi''(y)=1/y^2>0.

How the problem maps onto them.

  1. Left side: apply \phi to each value of y, then average.
  2. Right side: average y first (giving 2.5), then apply \phi.

Problem 21: Which way does the inequality point for a concave function?

Use Jensen’s inequality to decide which is larger, \log\mathbb{E}[y] or \mathbb{E}[\log y], for any positive random variable y. Then explain in one or two sentences how this same inequality is behind the ELBO being a lower bound.

TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, Jensen’s inequality and Tutorial: reverse KL and the ELBO.

Apply Jensen to the convex \phi=-\log: \mathbb{E}[-\log y]\ge-\log\mathbb{E}[y]. Multiplying by -1 flips the inequality: \mathbb{E}[\log y]\le\log\mathbb{E}[y]. For a concave function, the average of the function is at most the function of the average. With Problem 20’s numbers: \log2\approx0.693\le\log2.5\approx0.916.

For the ELBO, take y=p(x,z)/q(z) with z\sim q. Then \mathbb{E}_q[y]=\int p(x,z)\,dz = p(x), so

\mathcal L = \mathbb{E}_q[\log y] \le \log\mathbb{E}_q[y] = \log p(x).

The week chapter reached the same bound through \mathrm{KL}\ge0, which is itself proved with Jensen.

Answer. \mathbb{E}[\log y]\le\log\mathbb{E}[y]; applied to y=p(x,z)/q(z) it gives \mathcal L\le\log p(x).

Key takeaways.

  • For a concave function such as \log, the inequality flips: \mathbb{E}[\log y]\le\log\mathbb{E}[y].
  • Choosing y=p(x,z)/q(z) with z\sim q makes the right side \log p(x), which is why the ELBO is a lower bound.

y=\frac{p(x,z)}{q(z)},\ \ z\sim q, \qquad \mathbb{E}_q[y]=\sum_z q(z)\frac{p(x,z)}{q(z)}=\sum_z p(x,z)=p(x).

  2. For the ELBO, choose y so that its expectation under q is the evidence p(x). Then the left side, \mathbb{E}_q[\log y], is the ELBO, and the right side is \log p(x).

Problem 28: Which objective survives an unknown normalizer?

TipWorked solution

Answer. Reverse KL: yes, using samples from q; \log Z only shifts the objective. Forward KL: not directly, because it needs expectations under p.

Key takeaways.

  • An objective can be optimized by sampling when its expectation is under a distribution we can sample and every function inside it can be evaluated.
  • The reverse KL meets both conditions, with \log Z only a constant shift. The forward KL needs expectations under p.

Problem 29: Maximum entropy gives an exponential family (stretch)

Among all distributions p on \{0,1,2\} with mean \mathbb{E}_p[x]=t, find the form of the one with maximum entropy, using a Lagrange multiplier for each constraint. What is the answer when t=1?

TipWorked solution

This uses the maximum-entropy remark in Week 6, Why variational inference uses the I-projection and the method of Lagrange multipliers.

Maximize H(p)=-\sum_x p(x)\log p(x) subject to \sum_x p(x)x=t and \sum_x p(x)=1. The Lagrangian is

-\sum_x p(x)\log p(x) + \eta\Big(\sum_x p(x)\,x - t\Big) + \lambda\Big(\sum_x p(x)-1\Big).

Answer. p(x)\propto e^{\eta x} with \eta set by the mean constraint; for t=1, \eta=0 and p is uniform.

Key takeaways.

  • Maximizing entropy under expectation constraints gives an exponential family, with one natural parameter per constraint.
  • The pattern "Lagrangian, differentiate, exponentiate" reappears in Problem 36 and in the mean-field update.

Answer. q^*=(1/6,\ 1/3,\ 1/2), and the maximum value is \log6\approx1.792.

Key takeaways.

  • Maximizing "expected score plus entropy" gives the softmax of the score, and the maximum value is the log-sum-exp.
  • The mean-field update solves this problem once per variable, with the score supplied by the neighbours.

Problem 37: Two sweeps on a three-node chain

Three binary variables x_1-x_2-x_3, each in \{0,1\}, form a chain MRF with \phi_{12}=x_1x_2 and \phi_{23}=x_2x_3. Write m_i=q_i(1) and \sigma(y)=1/(1+e^{-y}).

  1. Derive the coordinate updates for m_1, m_2, m_3.
  2. Starting uniform, run two sweeps in the order 1,2,3.
  3. The fixed point is about (0.690,\ 0.799,\ 0.690), and the exact marginals are about (0.679,\ 0.776,\ 0.679). Comment.
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, The coordinate update.

  1. For node 1 (one neighbour): q_1(x_1)\propto\exp\{\sum_{x_2}q_2(x_2)\,x_1x_2\}=\exp\{x_1m_2\}, so m_1=e^{m_2}/(1+e^{m_2})=\sigma(m_2). Likewise m_3=\sigma(m_2). Node 2 has two neighbours, whose averaged potentials add: m_2=\sigma(m_1+m_3).
  2. Start m=(0.5,0.5,0.5).
    • Sweep 1: m_1=\sigma(0.5)\approx0.623; m_2=\sigma(0.623+0.5)=\sigma(1.123)\approx0.754; m_3=\sigma(0.754)\approx0.680.
    • Sweep 2: m_1=\sigma(0.754)\approx0.680; m_2=\sigma(0.680+0.680)\approx0.796; m_3=\sigma(0.796)\approx0.689.
    Each update uses the newest values of the neighbours, which is what makes this coordinate ascent rather than a simultaneous update.
  3. The middle node has two attractive neighbours, so it ends up the most confident in both. Mean field overstates every marginal a little, the same direction as the week’s two-variable example: replacing the neighbours by their averages ignores the joint configurations in which they are both 0.

Answer. (1) m_1=\sigma(m_2), m_2=\sigma(m_1+m_3), m_3=\sigma(m_2). (2) After two sweeps, m\approx(0.680,\ 0.796,\ 0.689). (3) Close to exact, slightly overconfident.

Key takeaways.

  • For a binary variable, the mean-field update is the logistic function of the score difference a(1)-a(0).
  • Coordinate ascent plugs in the newest neighbour values at each step.
  • With attractive couplings, mean field tends to be slightly overconfident, because it ignores correlated configurations of the neighbours.

Problem 38: Unary terms, and a fixed point that is not a maximum (stretch)

  1. Add unary log-potentials \phi_i(x_i) to the pairwise MRF. Rederive the coordinate update.
  2. Two spins x_1,x_2\in\{-1,+1\} have only the pairwise potential \phi_{12}=Jx_1x_2 with J=2, and no unary terms. Write m_i=\mathbb{E}_{q_i}[x_i] and show the update is m_1\leftarrow\tanh(Jm_2), m_2\leftarrow\tanh(Jm_1).
  3. Run the updates from a uniform start (m_1=m_2=0), and from a start with m_2=0.2. What happens in each case?
  4. The exact marginals are uniform by symmetry. What does mean field get wrong, and how is it related to the I-projection?
TipWorked solution

This uses Week 6, The coordinate update and the mode-seeking behaviour from Which direction? I-projection and M-projection.

  1. A unary term adds \sum_{x_i}q_i(x_i)\phi_i(x_i) to the part of the objective involving q_i. Its derivative with respect to q_i(x_i) is \phi_i(x_i), so the stationarity condition gains that term and q_i(x_i)\propto\exp\Big\{\phi_i(x_i)+\sum_{j\in N(i)}\sum_{x_j}q_j(x_j)\phi_{ij}(x_i,x_j)\Big\}.

  2. \sum_{x_2}q_2(x_2)Jx_1x_2=Jx_1m_2, so q_1(x_1)\propto e^{Jx_1m_2} and m_1=\frac{e^{Jm_2}-e^{-Jm_2}}{e^{Jm_2}+e^{-Jm_2}}=\tanh(Jm_2). The same for m_2.

  3. From m_1=m_2=0: \tanh(0)=0, so nothing ever moves; uniform is a fixed point. From m_2=0.2: m_1=\tanh(0.4)\approx0.380, m_2=\tanh(0.760)\approx0.641, then 0.857, 0.937, and the values settle at m\approx0.957, the positive solution of m=\tanh(2m). Starting from m_2=-0.2 gives m\approx-0.957 by symmetry.

    Compare objective values, \mathbb{E}_q[Jx_1x_2]+H(q_1)+H(q_2)=Jm_1m_2+H(q_1)+H(q_2). The uniform point gives 0+2\log2\approx1.386; the tilted fixed point gives about 2(0.957)^2+2(0.104)\approx2.039. Both are below \log Z=\log(2e^2+2e^{-2})\approx2.711. So the uniform point is a fixed point of the updates but not a maximum: coordinate ascent can get stuck at it only because the start was exactly symmetric.

  4. The exact p puts almost all its mass on (+1,+1) and (-1,-1) equally, so each exact marginal is uniform. A product q cannot put mass on both of those corners without also putting mass on the mixed corners, which p penalizes. So the best product picks one corner, like the I-projection picking one bump of a mixture. The resulting marginals (q_i(+1)\approx0.98) are confidently wrong about each spin, even though the objective is as high as a product can get.

Answer. (1) Add \phi_i(x_i) inside the exponent. (2) m_i\leftarrow\tanh(Jm_j). (3) Stuck at 0 from a uniform start; from m_2=0.2, converges to m\approx0.957 (or -0.957 from the opposite start). (4) Mean field breaks the symmetry and picks one mode, as the I-projection does.

Key takeaways.

  • With \pm1 spins the update uses \tanh, and with 0/1 variables it uses \sigma. They are related by \tanh(y)=2\sigma(2y)-1.
  • A fixed point of the updates need not be a maximum, so compare objective values.
  • With strong coupling, mean field breaks the symmetry and picks one mode, as the I-projection does with a mixture.
